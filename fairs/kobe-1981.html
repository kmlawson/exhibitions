Kobe Portopia 81 was a six-month event held in Kobe in 1981, on Port Island, a site reclaimed from the sea (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163; Allwood et al. 2001p. 163; Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). The text calls it "not an official expo" and says it was devised outside the rules of the BIE (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163; Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Its success is the reason given for Tokyo's plan for a World City Expo in 1996, which was cancelled (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Why it was held outside the BIE rules

Japan had announced its next official exposition for 1985, which was nine years away (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The text asks whether "an international event outside the BIE rules" could be devised, and gives Kobe Portopia 81 as the answer (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). The BIE convention covered only invitations to nations made through diplomatic channels, so the organisers invited seaports that traded with Kobe instead, and "the rules were comfortably side-stepped" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Kobe's sister ports took part (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163).

Site, exhibits and visitors

Port Island has 436 hectares, of which some 20 per cent was temporarily allocated to the exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). A 580 billion yen urban development project paid for the infrastructure, including a new rapid transit system (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Five international pavilions showed exhibits from maritime trading interests (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Japanese official and private-sector pavilions dominated, with over 500 exhibitors in 27 pavilions, many using new audio-visual techniques (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). The text gives "over 16 million visitors to the event", a figure that exceeded predictions and gave the organisers an operating profit (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). It names no organising body and gives no cost for the exhibition itself (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163).

Aftermath and place in the series

The author credits the management and planning with adding Japanese know-how that was used at Tsukuba Expo 85 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). The success of Kobe's Portopia Expo in 1981 prompted Tokyo to propose a World City Expo for 1996, on reclaimed land in Tokyo Bay (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). Large Japanese firms were to provide the main attractions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). Planning was well advanced when a change in political leadership doomed the event, and the new Governor of Tokyo, Yukio Aoshima, cancelled World City Expo 96 less than a year before it was to open (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Exhibitions outside BIE rules — the text presents Kobe as Japan's answer to the problem of holding an event when the BIE calendar had no slot free (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163)
  • Port and urban redevelopment — the exhibition used newly reclaimed land and was tied to a large infrastructure project (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163)
  • Japanese domestic pavilions — the text says the official and private sector pavilions dominated the scene, with audio-visual displays (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163)
  • Continuity in Japan's exposition programme — the text links Kobe to Tsukuba 1985 and to the 1996 proposal (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163; Allwood et al. 2001p. 166)
  • Political change and cancelled projects — the 1996 Tokyo proposal failed when leadership changed, not for lack of planning (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166)

Questions for study

  1. The text gives a 580 billion yen project but no cost for the exhibition itself. How would a historian separate the cost of the event from that of the city works around it? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163)
  2. The text says the event was not an official expo yet achieved an international character. What did the organisers gain from avoiding BIE registration, and who lost? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163; Allwood et al. 2001p. 163)
  3. Why did a change in Tokyo's political leadership, rather than the exhibition's own record, end the 1996 proposal? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166)

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
7Some Concluding Reflectionsp. 186Asia

Hotta-Lister gives an overall verdict on the 1910 Exhibition, concluding that it was on balance a success rather than the failure of Japanese opinion. The argument rests on the partnership between Imre Kiralfy, the commercial entrepreneur, and the Japanese government under Komura, which the author sees as the seed of later controversy in the Diet. Komura's aims were mainly diplomatic, to ease anti-Japanese feeling and support the Alliance, with trade and loans as secondary goals, and the proposed Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1917 is shown to have been a further reason for the venture. The Japanese negative reviews are traced to Kiralfy's popular attractions and to the elite's embarrassment at Ainu and village displays, which the author compares with exhibitions in the United States. The closing pages set the 1910 event beside the Great Japan Exhibition of 1981/82 and the Japan Festival of 1991 in London, which shared a wish to correct misunderstanding of Japan but differed in scale, funding and educational follow-up. The author concludes that the Exhibition helped turn the Alliance into a link between peoples, though only to a limited extent.

A reference work on world exhibitions, here in a chapter on Eastern Hemisphere events from 1975 to 1999. The Kobe Portopia 81 passage is a few paragraphs, placed between the Okinawa Expo 75 and Tsukuba Expo 85. The author argues that Japan found a way to hold an event outside the BIE rules, using invitations to Kobe's trading ports, and that its success led Tokyo to plan a 1996 successor, which was then cancelled. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

On the web

Wikipedia

Portopia '81 (Japanese: 神戸ポートアイランド博覧会), also abbreviated "Portopia", was an exhibition in Japan held at Port Island and March 20 - September 15, 1981. Its theme was "Creation of a New Cultural City on the Sea". Portopia was organized to commemorate the opening of Port Island. It was visited by over 16 million people.
