British and Colonial Industrial Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliography

The British and Colonial Industrial Exhibition was held in Manchester in 1894 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400). Two reference works list it only in an appendix of fairs left out of their main entries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). A third text, Hale's study of France, mentions British colonial expositions of 1894 without naming a city (Hale 2008p. 168). The texts give no organisers, site, exhibits or visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400), and they do not show that Hale's event is this one (Hale 2008p. 168).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Listing and status

Both reference works give the fair the same name, year and city: British and Colonial Industrial Exhibition, Manchester, 1894 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400). Neither gives it an entry of its own. Both place it in Appendix D, which lists fairs and exhibitions held after 1851 that fell short of "meeting the qualifications to be included in the main body of this book" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395).

The appendix gives only general reasons. Many of its fairs were very specialised, and others lacked a suitable international scope (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). It also says that each fair was important to its host city (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). The texts give no evidence for that claim about Manchester (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400).

Empire and colony

The texts show very little that was imperial or colonial about this exhibition. Its name includes the word Colonial (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400). Hale does not name Manchester at all. He writes that the British "held colonial expositions in 1894, 1899, and 1924–1925" (Hale 2008p. 168).

Only Hale comments on these events, and only as a point of comparison. He describes Great Britain as France's major international competitor, both in the scramble for colonies and on the exposition circuit (Hale 2008p. 168; Hale 2008p. 168). French organisers measured their own displays against those of other imperial powers (Hale 2008p. 168; Hale 2008p. 168).

The texts name no colony or dominion that took part, no colonised people on display, and no aim the organisers gave for the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400). The Historical Dictionary places a British colonial exposition at Wembley in 1924 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). That is a different year and place, so it is not this fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Excluded from the main entries — both reference works list the fair only in Appendix D, which covers fairs that fell short of their criteria (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395).
  • Local importance — the appendix says each listed fair mattered to its host city, but the texts give no evidence of this for Manchester (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425).
  • Imperial rivalry — Hale treats British colonial expositions as rivals to French ones, and mentions the 1894 event only in passing (Hale 2008p. 168; Hale 2008p. 168).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the two reference works place the Manchester fair in an appendix, and what would it have taken for it to enter the main entries?
  2. Did the 1894 British colonial exposition that Hale mentions take place in Manchester, and what evidence outside these three texts would settle that?
  3. How would a colonial exhibition in Manchester have differed from the French colonial expositions Hale describes?
  4. What would a student need to find to test the appendix's claim that such fairs mattered to their host cities?

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Hale, Dana S. Races on Display: French Representations of Colonized Peoples, 1886–1940. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2008.
ch. 8, 'La Mère-Patrie and Her Colonial Children: France on Display' (PDF p. 181; printed page not seen)

In a passing comparison within its chapter on the French colonial expositions, Hale notes that the British held colonial expositions in 1894, 1899 and 1924-1925, as Britain's rivals for colonies and for the exposition circuit. The 1894 event is not named by place and is not described, so the text does not show whether it is the Manchester fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hale asks how the Third Republic represented its colonial subjects and, through them, its own identity. Using trademark labels registered in Paris and Marseilles from 1886 to 1940 and the colonial sections of the fairs in those cities, the book compares sub-Saharan Africans, North Africans and Indochinese. Part I (1886–1913) covers the empire, racial theory and the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900; Part II (1914–1940) covers the Marseilles and Paris expositions of 1922, 1931 and 1937. A final chapter treats France's self-image as parent and brother to its colonial children. Hale argues that the images stressed human difference and French superiority, and that this framework still shapes France's ties with its former colonies.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are central to the book, but almost entirely through Indochina, one of the three colonial groups the study is built around. Chapters 4 ('Indochinese: Gentle Subjects') and 7 ('Indochinese: Fils Doué') are devoted to it, covering trademarks and the 1900, 1922, 1931 and 1937 fairs. Chapter 1 covers Indochina's conquest and its pavilions at the 1889 Paris fair, and chapter 8 treats the Indochinese as part of France's self-presentation. Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos appear as conquered territories and as exhibits: the Angkor Wat reconstructions of 1922 and 1931, the Cambodian royal ballet, the Théâtre Annamite, the dragon procession and the Rue Annamite, with artisans, soldiers and performers housed on the fairgrounds. Asian visitors include the prince of Annam in 1889. East Asia gets only passing attention. Chinese appear as neighbours seen as troublesome in the Tonkin affair that opens chapter 4, and as workers in pay and hours disputes (chapter 4), and as rickshaw pullers whose planned employment in Paris in 1931 was dropped after the Chinese consul protested (chapter 7). Japan and Korea get no discussion, though kimono-clad women recur on trademarks. In Southeast Asia, Thailand appears only as a threat to Cambodia, and the Javanese dancers of 1889 and the Malays of the 1931 ethnographic directives are passing mentions; the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma are absent.
