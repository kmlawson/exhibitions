Proposed national exhibition of China (Pan Shengnian memorial)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

In 1898 Pan Shengnian, a Chinese official of the Board of Works, submitted a memorial to the Qing court proposing an exhibition in Beijing during the Hundred Days’ Reforms (Hurp. 33). The memorial reached the court exactly a week before Empress Cixi’s coup and received no consideration (Hurp. 33). The texts describe no exhibition that followed from it. Hur treats the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910 as China’s first national exhibition (Hurp. 149).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Reformist proposals before 1898

Chinese reformers argued through the 1890s that China should hold its own exhibitions. Zheng Guanying’s proposal covered the site, budget, invitation of foreign nations and official regulation, and Hur calls him "the first advocate of holding exhibitions in China" (Hurp. 31; Hurp. 31). Chen Chi, who wrote the preface to Zheng’s book, made the same case and linked Japan’s growth to its exhibitions (Hurp. 31). Liu Zhenlin’s article in the Macao journal Zhixinbao made the same argument (Hurp. 33).

The memorial of 1898

Hur writes that, with the Emperor Guangxu’s sponsorship in 1898, an exhibition in China seemed distinctly possible (Hurp. 33). Pan Shengnian then proposed an exhibition in Beijing (Hurp. 33). Hur’s second chapter describes the same petition as one for a national exhibition in China and says Chen Jinru was apparently unaware of it (Hurp. 83).

Why no exhibition followed

Hur argues that foreign encroachment, the Boxer Uprising and the evacuation of the court to Xi’an left a polity vacuum from 1898 to 1901, in which reformers led the debate on exhibitions. The court’s return to Beijing in early 1902 changed this (Hurp. 46; Hurp. 46). In April 1903 the Zhongwai ribao printed a commendatory ode by Chen Jingru, a Lieutenant Colonel (Hurp. 81; Hurp. 82). The ode said: "The court is also aware of this necessity, but no one has proposed this yet" (Hurp. 83). After the disputes over China’s display at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the Chinese press called for a national exhibition of China, and a Hong Kong newspaper reported that the Empress Dowager looked displeased with the Chinese display (Hurp. 84; Hurp. 84). The Qing court then sent an Imperial Commission to the St Louis Exposition of 1904 (Hur; Hurp. 84).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Reformist advocacy — Zheng Guanying, Chen Chi and Liu Zhenlin argued in the 1890s for Chinese exhibitions, and Hur places Pan’s memorial within that case (Hurp. 31; Hurp. 33; Hurp. 33).
  • Hundred Days’ Reforms — the memorial reached the court a week before the coup that ended the reforms, and received no consideration (Hurp. 33).
  • Court and press — Hur links the court’s weakness after 1898 to the reformers’ control of the debate, and the press to the court’s later response at Osaka (Hurp. 46; Hurp. 84).
  • Series — the proposal is connected to the 1903 ode and to the Nanyang Exposition of 1910, which Hur calls China’s first national exhibition (Hurp. 83; Hurp. 149).

Questions for study

  1. Chen Jingru’s 1903 ode said no one had yet proposed a national exhibition, but Hur’s note says Pan Shengnian had petitioned in 1898 (Hurp. 83; Hurp. 83). What does this gap suggest about how far reform proposals reached officials?
  2. Hur describes the memorial as proposing an exhibition in Beijing in chapter one and as a petition for a national exhibition in chapter two (Hurp. 33; Hurp. 83). Does an exhibition in the capital differ from a national one, and why might that matter?
  3. Hur reports, citing a Hong Kong newspaper, that the Empress Dowager looked displeased with China’s display at Osaka (Hurp. 84). Why might a court that gave the 1898 memorial no consideration respond to a foreign exhibition in 1903?

4The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition, Omaha, 1898: “Concomitant to Empire”Asia

Rydell reads the Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition of 1898 as an ideological event staged as the Spanish-American War began. He traces how Omaha's businessmen and the Knights of Ak-Sar-Ben backed the fair, and how Edward Rosewater and the Bureau of American Ethnology's James Mooney shaped its Indian Congress, a display first imagined as a vast encampment of all tribes. The main evidence is the Congress itself: its staged sham battle, the parade before President McKinley, and Mooney's complaints that the show had become a Wild West show. Midway villages of African Americans, Chinese, and Filipinos, and the Buffalo Bill day, are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and empire. The chapter closes with the fair's record attendance and the Greater America Exposition that followed the next year, which turned the exposition movement toward the colonies.

Moderate discussion 1

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
Chapter One, 'One China and the World Exhibitions before 1903' (printed pp. 30-33 and 46); Chapter Two, note 176 (p. 83) and text p. 84; Abstract

Hur's dissertation on China's representation at the world exhibitions treats Pan Shengnian's 1898 memorial briefly, in a few paragraphs of Chapter One. Hur places it among the 1890s reformist arguments for a Chinese exhibition, led by Zheng Guanying, Chen Chi and Liu Zhenlin, and uses the timing of the memorial, a week before the coup that ended the Hundred Days' Reforms, to explain why it received no consideration. Hur then argues that the court's weakness from 1898 to 1901 left reformers in control of the debate. Chapter Two returns to the petition in a footnote to note that Chen Jingru, writing in 1903, seems to have been unaware of it. The wider argument concerns how the Customs, Japan and the reformist press contested China's exhibits, not this proposal.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
