London · 1886

Colonial and Indian Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Colonial and Indian Exhibition opened at South Kensington, London, on 4 May 1886 and closed on 10 November, drawing about 5.5 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Filipová 2015p. 221). It was the last of four thematic shows held on the South Kensington site from 1883, and the first fair devoted solely to imperial themes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Historians use it to study how Britain presented its empire to a mass public, through Indian courts, colonial pavilions and displays of colonised people, and the show set a model that later French and British fairs followed (Hoffenberg 2001p. 18; Roche 2000p. 58; Rydell 1993p. 63).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The Prince of Wales, later Edward VII, gave the show his patronage. Steffel writes that the Prince had admired colonial exhibits at the Paris exhibition of 1878 and wanted Britons to see the colonies as advanced states that had contributed to the wider commonwealth (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). The purpose, in Steffel's account, was to build public support for colonialism by showing the colonies' economic value and their trade with Britain (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Greenhalgh places the show at a turning point. After the scramble for Africa, the British grew wary of rival empires, and the exhibition revealed "a move from complacent pride in empire to a propagandistic defence of it" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58).

Organisation and finance

The Official Guide called the show "in no sense international". It was confined to colonial and Indian subjects, and British and foreign exhibitors were excluded (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). The organisers adopted no overall classification. Steffel gives two reasons: recently founded colonies should stand on an equal footing, and colonial products were hard to classify. Only colonial people or colonial businessmen could exhibit, and each colony's space depended on its relative importance (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Prasch concludes that each government arranged its own contribution, so the fair became a forum for competing visions of empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90).

Steffel gives the profit as about £35,000. That sum paid off the £5,964 debt of the International Instrument and Music Exhibition and provided £25,000 towards the proposed Imperial Institute (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96). The exact figure differs between texts: £34,642 in the Encyclopedia's statistics (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 414) and £34,643 in the table of Allwood, Allan and Reid (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206).

Site, buildings and exhibits

The show took place in the gardens of the Royal Horticultural Society, behind the Albert Hall (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). Steffel says the organisers leased the arcades of the 1883 Fisheries Exhibition and added galleries for an Indian Palace, an Australian exhibit and a colonial market, and gives the ground as 24 acres (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Two other texts give 13 acres (Badger 1979p. 131; Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). Dining halls served colonial cuisine, and there were a wine bar and stalls selling coffee, tea and cocoa. Markets sold colonial fruit, vegetables and frozen meat, which Steffel says showed that produce and meat could be shipped to British markets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96).

The most prominent building was the Indian Palace. Its entrance was the Gwalior Gate, made for the Calcutta exhibition of 1883-84 by Indian stone carvers, and the Durbar Hall was designed by Caspar Purdon Clarke (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). Prasch calls the India shown increasingly hybrid (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). Old London, a reconstructed historic street designed by George H. Birch, had been shown at South Kensington since 1884 (Filipová 2015p. 203). Geppert argues that this show made the retrospective old village, with a street such as Old London, a standard fair feature (Geppert 2010p. 52). By 1886 the organisers no longer tied Old London to the theme, and it became office space. Wilson Smith calls it "a hollow if still spectacular sideshow" (Filipová 2015p. 210).

Nations and colonies on show

Each colony showed its exports, history, political life, geography and ethnography (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Steffel gives India about 26 per cent of interior floor space, Canada 22 per cent and Australasia 25 per cent (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). Prasch gives India almost a third of the exhibit space, with displays five times larger than at any previous exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). The texts agree that India was the centre of the show, but they do not agree on its share.

Canada stressed industrial products, and Australasia its minerals and agricultural wealth (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95). The Official Guide said Australia would soon stop needing manufactured goods from Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). The Colonial Arts Gallery had no indigenous artists, and white artists worked in European genres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). Rice notes that New Zealand first entered the fine art galleries as a British colony at this show (Filipová 2015p. 234). Lindauer's The Maori at Home was the centrepiece of Walter Buller's collection there (Filipová 2015p. 244).

Peoples on display

Qureshi counts 89 living performers in the six-month show, including 46 Indians, ten Ceylonese, ten Red Indians from British Guiana, five Cypriots, five Malays, nine Africans from the Cape and four from the Straits Settlements (Qureshi 2011p. 238). Benedict's count, from the appendix to the Reminiscences of the show, differs: 45 Indians, one Burmese, ten Senegalese, ten Red Indians, five Cypriots, five Cape Malays, nine Kaffirs, four Malays and eight Hong Kong Chinese (Benedict 1983p. 46). The people were housed in a compound outside the South Kensington buildings (Benedict 1983p. 46). Burris calls it the first international exposition to display actual peoples in a significant way, and the first of many to show dominions and colonies exclusively (Burris 2001p. 83). Model natives were chosen to cover a wide range of skin colours and were shown as subordinates while also being held up as valued parts of the empire (Burris 2001p. 83).

Prisoners were recruited for the show. Qureshi says the Royal Commission hired Henry S. King and Company to employ 31 craftsmen from India, and that Dr John William Tyler, superintendent of the Agra Central Jail, selected men from among the inmates (Qureshi 2011p. 134). Mathur counts 34 men in the Indian artisans display and says most were Agra jail inmates. Tulsi Ram was the exception. Tulsi Ram had been arrested repeatedly for vagrancy in England, and by summer 1886 was presented to Western audiences as a sweetmeat maker from Agra (Mathur 2007p. 59; Mathur 2007p. 61; Mathur 2007p. 61). Qureshi argues that the performers were trained in their professed trades and made to perform them, so that prisoners became potters, weavers and diamond washers (Qureshi 2011p. 245). Qureshi also argues that displayed peoples were "portrayed in both their ostensibly natural and their built environments" (Qureshi 2011p. 241). Cape Kaffirs were shown washing diamonds, an example of people performing industrial tasks (Benedict 1983p. 46).

The Anthropological Institute held meetings at South Kensington in June and July 1886 (Qureshi 2011p. 256). Galton tested the strength of a performer with an apparatus used earlier on about 10,000 people at the 1884 Health Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 257).

Visitors and reception

The Queen opened the show on 4 May. Prasch notes, citing Hoffenberg, that the Queen took her place on a golden throne looted from Lahore (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 88). The programme mixed Tennyson's ode, set by Sullivan, with God Save the Queen, whose second verse was sung in Sanskrit in a translation by Max Müller (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Benedict 1983p. 46). Prasch reads this mix as a sign of the message's incoherence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89).

Prasch puts the closing attendance at about 5.5 million, including about 400,000 working-class visitors whose reduced-fare passes the London Trades Council underwrote (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). Steffel adds cheaper admission for London workingmen from mid-July and excursions from the provinces (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96). Steffel's entry gives a total of 52 million, a figure no other text supports and probably a misprint. The other texts give 5,550,745 (Filipová 2015p. 221; Badger 1979p. 131). Mathur says 2.7 million visitors came through the Indian entrance, which the new underground railway served, and that this was more than half the total (Mathur 2007p. 57).

Lord Derby told the royal commission in 1886 that interest in exhibitions was unabated and ever increasing, despite talk that they had been played out (Geppert 2010p. 212). Mukharji wrote that the Indian Bazaar crowd was astonished to see Indians produce art with tools they had long discarded (Qureshi 2011p. 245). The Times described the visitor carried from the struggle of modern life into the unchanging East (Mathur 2007p. 57). Liberty's attempt to recreate an Indian village in Battersea failed, and the failure became part of public perceptions of imperial display (Mathur 2007p. 58).

Aftermath and legacy

The show's collections and organisation led to the Imperial Institute in South Kensington, later the Commonwealth Institute (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96). Steffel says the exhibits were meant to form a permanent display there, but this came to nothing (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96). Kew acquired an indigo factory model from the show in 1886. Cornish concludes that Kew's work promoted natural indigo but did not improve labour conditions or stop the move to synthetic dyes (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 130; Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 142). Geppert records that the Empress Theatre at Earl's Court was a remnant of the show that had never been dismantled (Geppert 2010p. 103).

Later British and French fairs drew on it. Rydell links the 1886 show with Marseilles in 1922 and Wembley in 1924-25 as part of the colonial exhibition traditions of both countries (Rydell 1993p. 63). Roche says the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900 greatly expanded the imperial theme, partly in reaction to the London show's success (Roche 2000p. 58). Greenhalgh notes that several sources say the show inspired the enlarged imperial display at Paris in 1889. Greenhalgh calls that possible, but holds that the decision to build the pavilions was wholly French (Greenhalgh 1988p. 66). Geppert records that the planners of Wembley placed it in a national tradition running from 1851 through this show to the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Geppert 2010p. 153).

How historians have interpreted it

Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions helped imagine the empire as a single federated whole. A contemporary expected the show to bind Britain, India and the colonies more closely together (Hoffenberg 2001p. 20). Hoffenberg reads the colonial commissioners' classification as stressing each colony's character while imperial rule supplied the uniformity (Hoffenberg 2001p. 26), and treats visitors and commissioners as active participants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 29). Prasch reads the same show as propaganda undermined from within. With no overall classification, each government staged its own empire, and the incoherence did not trouble contemporaries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90). Both agree the show was propaganda. They differ on whether its disorder weakened the message or was itself part of the way empire was ordered.

Greenhalgh argues that the show marked a move from pride in empire to a defence of it, and that India was widely thought of as a reservoir to be drained into the British economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh also reads the fine art label given to Indian painting and sculpture as a hierarchy based on race (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). Mathur argues that the show offered an idealised, timeless India, built on the village community (Mathur 2007p. 57), and that the Indian craftsman was presented as a reified figure whose timelessness was the basis of its appeal (Mathur 2007p. 58). Qureshi argues that the 1851 and 1886 British exhibitions need reevaluating in the history of displayed peoples (Qureshi 2011p. 248). Bell reads The Laughing Girl, Lindauer's painting of Meri Nereaha, as an idealised image that fitted the imperial dream of unity (Bell 1992p. 219). Thomas uses a contemporary's praise of the show's lessons to argue that objects make colonial meanings seem real (Thomas 1991p. 176). Benedict argues that the aim was to educate the British public about their empire (Benedict 1983p. 47). Hur and Coté place the show within a wider pattern in which colonial peoples were displayed as inferior or as stages on a path to civilisation (Hurp. 19; Cotép. 12).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India

The Indian displays were the largest Asian presence and the most expensive. Greenhalgh gives the Indian exhibits 103,000 square feet at a cost of £22,000 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Hoffenberg counts over 65,000 square feet in the main halls and another 40,000 in the separate Indian Palace (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9). Greenhalgh breaks the £22,000 into the Indian Government's £10,000, the Royal Commission's £3,000, a Bombay Exhibition Grant of £6,850 and a Royal Commission Screen Grant of £2,500. These parts add to £22,350, and Greenhalgh concludes that all the money came from government sources (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Elsewhere, the Indian government usually paid half to two thirds of the cost of Indian palaces, with tea companies the main private funders (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59).

Officials ran the displays. Edward C. Buck, executive commissioner for India, organised exhibits and lectures on silk (Hoffenberg 2001p. 51). Watt and Mukharji ran the Economic Court (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 129). Trilokya Nath Mukharji, a Bengali official, was the most influential South Asian exhibition official (Hoffenberg 2001p. 50). The Indian Palace had the Gwalior Gate, which had been made for the Calcutta exhibition of 1883-84 by about a thousand Indian stone carvers, and later went to the Victoria and Albert Museum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77). In the Economic Court, a clay model of an indigo factory with about 100 figures was made by Rakhal Chunder Pal and commissioned by Mukharji (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 127; Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 128).

The Indian artisans display in the Indian Palace had 34 men (Mathur 2007p. 59). Mathur says most were prisoners from Agra jail, Tulsi Ram being the exception (Mathur 2007p. 60). Qureshi gives 31 craftsmen recruited by King and Company, with Dr Tyler selecting the men from the jail (Qureshi 2011p. 134). The Queen received the men at Windsor Castle, and Mukharji recorded the Queen's kindly attention to Indian subjects (Mathur 2007p. 60). George Birdwood's catalogue essay praised the calm dignity of the artisans' lives (Mathur 2007p. 58). Seven men described as Bombay servants ran an outdoor café serving Indian tea to crowds (Mathur 2007p. 59).

Interpretations differ. Mathur reads the artisans as an idealised, timeless India (Mathur 2007p. 57). Qureshi reads the trained prisoners as productive colonial labour (Qureshi 2011p. 245). Cornish reads the indigo model as global knowledge that did little for Indian workers (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 142). Greenhalgh reads the fine art label as a race hierarchy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). Hoffenberg places the Indian experts within an imperial cultural system that assimilated South Asian products into exhibitions such as the Calcutta International and Colonial and Indian Exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 51).

Ceylon, Burma, Malaya and Hong Kong

Qureshi's count includes ten people from Ceylon, five Malays and four from the Straits Settlements (Qureshi 2011p. 238). Benedict's appendix lists one Burmese, four Malays, five Cape Malays and eight Hong Kong Chinese (Benedict 1983p. 46). Benedict also says the compound housed Hindus, Muslims and Buddhists, and people from Perak and Hong Kong (Benedict 1983p. 46). Hur states that some Hong Kong Chinese were displayed alongside Indians and Burmese (Hurp. 19). The texts name no Japanese, Korean or Chinese display beyond Hong Kong.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial propaganda — the texts agree the show was designed to build support for empire, but they disagree on whether it worked: Greenhalgh sees a move to defensive propaganda (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58), while Prasch sees an incoherent message (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90).
  • India at the centre — India took the largest share of space and money, and its displays are the most closely studied part of the show (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95; Greenhalgh 1988p. 59; Hoffenberg 2001p. 9).
  • Human displays — living people were shown in a compound and in courts, some recruited from prisons, and the texts read these displays as a hierarchy of peoples (Qureshi 2011p. 238; Burris 2001p. 83; Benedict 1983p. 46).
  • Timeless India and reconstructed pasts — the Indian artisans and the reconstructed Old London presented a past removed from modern industry (Mathur 2007p. 57; Filipová 2015p. 210; Geppert 2010p. 52).
  • Commerce and resources — colonial produce, frozen meat and Indian goods were shown as trade opportunities and as resources to be drawn on (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 96; Greenhalgh 1988p. 62; Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 129).
  • Expertise and classification — Indian officials and experts such as Buck and Mukharji built the displays, and the absence of a common classification is read in opposed ways (Hoffenberg 2001p. 51; Hoffenberg 2001p. 26; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 90).
  • Art and race — the absence of Western art meant Indian painting and sculpture were labelled fine art, which Greenhalgh reads as a race hierarchy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

Questions for study

  1. The show had no common classification. Did that make it incoherent propaganda, as Prasch argues, or a way of ordering the empire, as Hoffenberg argues? What evidence would settle the question?
  2. Several texts describe the Indian artisans as an unchanging village people. How did the display of living workers shape what visitors took away about India?
  3. Qureshi and Mathur both show that prisoners were recruited for the show. What can the sources tell us about the performers' own views, and what is lost?
  4. The texts disagree on attendance, site size and cost. Why might reference works differ on these figures, and how should a student check them?
  5. Old London and the Gwalior Gate were reconstructions of the past. How did the scholars link such historic reconstructions to imperial politics?
  6. Several scholars treat 1886 as a precedent for Paris in 1889 and Wembley in 1924. Does seeing the show as a precedent change how its own aims should be judged?

Substantial discussion 3

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
Entry 'London 1886' (Colonial and Indian Exhibition), by Thomas Prasch, pp. 88-92

Encyclopedia entry on the 1886 London Colonial and Indian Exhibition, the last of the South Kensington series. Prasch covers the opening, the crowds, the finances, the musical programme, the layout and the main displays, with India as the centre of the show. His central argument is that the official message of imperial unity was undercut from within: with no overall classification, each government staged its own version of empire, so settler-colony self-promotion, racial and ethnographic displays and a hybrid, British-managed India competed with the official picture. He sets the show against the scramble for Africa and rising Indian Home Rule agitation, and presents it as imperial propaganda whose incoherence did not trouble contemporaries.

Greenhalgh's history of expositions treats the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition as the last of the 1883-86 South Kensington series and as the start of an era of imperial propaganda at exhibitions. He argues that it marked a shift from complacent pride in empire to a defence of it, in the context of British wariness of rivals after the scramble for Africa. He uses the cost and size of the Indian displays and their government funding to show the show's commitment to India as a resource, and he reads the lack of Western art as the reason India's painting and sculpture were labelled fine art, a hierarchy he ties to race. Burris's study of religion at expositions calls the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition the first international exposition to display actual peoples in a significant way, and the first of many to show dominions and colonies exclusively. On the web

Wikipedia

The Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 was held in South Kensington in London with the objective to (in the words of the then Prince of Wales) "stimulate commerce and strengthen the bonds of union now existing in every portion of her Majesty's Empire". The exhibition was opened by Queen Victoria, and had received 5.5 million visitors by the time it closed. It was housed in a collection of purpose-built buildings designed in an Indian style.
