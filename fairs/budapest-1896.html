Hungarian Millennium Exhibition (Budapest)

1896 évi Ezredéves Országos Kiállítás
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Hungarian Millennium Exhibition, called 1896 évi Ezredéves Országos Kiállítás in Hungarian, opened in Budapest's City Park on 2 May 1896 and ran for six months (Filipová 2015p. 113; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249). It celebrated the arrival of the Magyar tribes in the Carpathian Basin, and Franz Joseph opened it in his guise as King of Hungary (Filipová 2015p. 116). The enabling legislation was sponsored by the trade minister Gábor Baross (Filipová 2015p. 119). Albert treats it as a national exhibition in the Hungarian half of Austria-Hungary, and uses it to show how a multi-ethnic state displayed its nationalities (Filipová 2015p. 113).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The government fixed the millennium year from a committee's window of 888 to 900 and chose 895 in 1893, so the year was first set as 1895 and then postponed by one year (Filipová 2015p. 116). Székely gives a different sequence: 1894 was fixed for practical reasons and the date was later moved to 1896 (Filipová 2015p. 31). Count Jenő Zichy drafted the programme of celebrations in June 1890 (Filipová 2015p. 31).

Albert says the organisers came to see the Historical Exhibition as the main purpose of the event. It presented Hungarian cultural development, with its periods grouped into four temporary buildings and the history stopping at 1867 (Filipová 2015p. 117).

Organisers, site and finance

Zichy had organised the National General Exhibition of 1885 in Budapest, which the texts treat as the forerunner of the 1896 event (Filipová 2015p. 31). The 1896 exhibition used City Park, the site of the 1885 exhibition. Of that earlier exhibition only the Royal Pavilion, later a branch of the restaurant Gerbeaud, and the Industry Hall remained (Filipová 2015p. 117). A subway linked the park to the city centre. Albert calls it a simple cut-and-cover line (Filipová 2015p. 117), while Barenscott describes it as electrically powered (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 250). Various counties financed and built the houses of the Ethnographic Village (Filipová 2015p. 121). The three permanent structures of the Millennium, namely the Fine Arts Museum, the Art Pavilion and the Millennium Monument, stood in Heroes Square, not in the park (Filipová 2015p. 117).

Site and buildings

The Historical Exhibition stood on a small island in the park lake, in four temporary buildings of staff, a plaster and fibre material. Three of these were designed by Ignác Alpár (Filipová 2015p. 118). The Modern Exhibition had more than 100 structures, among them the Törley Champagne Pavilion and the Nobel Dynamite Pavilion (Filipová 2015p. 119). Houze says Alpár designed the City Park complex, and that Hungary's pavilion at the Paris 1900 exposition was a smaller version of it (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 149). Barenscott describes the neo-classical Palace of Art at the entrance to the fairgrounds (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249).

Exhibits and attractions

The Ethnographic Village had 24 house replicas from across greater Hungary. Twelve were Magyar and twelve represented non-Magyar nationalities: Bulgarians, Slovaks, Rumanians and Danube Germans (Filipová 2015p. 121). A church modelled on one in Kalotaszeg held artefacts from Zichy's Caucasian expeditions (Filipová 2015p. 121). Houze places the village among folk displays at Chicago in 1893 and at Prague in 1895 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). Wooden Székely gates marked the entrances to the fairgrounds (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 157).

Ős-Budavára was an amusement area designed by Oskar Marmorek. It recreated the Buda castle hill at the time of the Ottoman occupation, with theatres, restaurants and a mosque. Muslims from Bosnia-Herzegovina prayed there daily (Filipová 2015p. 121). Konsztantinápoly Budapesten, a second amusement area, is placed by Albert in the Lágymányos district beside the Danube (Filipová 2015p. 122). Barenscott places the same reconstruction on a large island on the lower right bank of the Danube, and describes its night-time theatricality (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 251).

Empire and colony

Budapest was the capital of the Hungarian part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire (Filipová 2015p. 113). Albert notes that the monarchy had two governments, a Hungarian and an Austrian, both headed by Franz Joseph (Filipová 2015p. 116). The opening was therefore presented as a Royal Hungarian event rather than an Imperial one (Filipová 2015p. 116). Hungary administered Croatia and Bosnia-Hercegovina, and both had regional pavilions in the Modern Exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 119). Albert also says the Budapest exhibition had almost exclusively Hungarian exhibitors (Filipová 2015p. 125).

Albert argues that the organisers aimed to celebrate the Magyar past and to subordinate the non-Magyar peoples of the kingdom. The Ethnographic Village presented a complex state identity, "one subsumed to the Hungarian state" (Filipová 2015p. 121). He says the organisers played the multi-ethnic problem down, and almost ignored it (Filipová 2015p. 115). Publications produced for the 1896 Millennium, he writes, show that the event was "clearly conceived as a celebration of the nation but not a celebration of the constituent ethnicities" (Filipová 2015p. 127). Albert calls the imposition of a Magyar vision of the past on areas outside it "a type of internal colonization" (Filipová 2015p. 127). Ős-Budavára, on his reading, turned the exotic and the alien into entertainment, which lessened the trauma of Ottoman and Habsburg rule (Filipová 2015p. 122). The passages I read describe no overseas colony and no colonised people from outside Central Europe taking part. The non-Magyar groups shown were inside the Hungarian state (Filipová 2015p. 121).

Visitors and reception

The foreign press praised Budapest for putting on a fair up to Western standards (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249). Barenscott cites a New York Times article of 9 June 1896 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 252). She says the exhibition was made for city visitors and tourists, and that its primary function was to present the progress and modernity endorsed by the organisers (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 250). She also says many visitors anticipated the off-site entertainments as much as, if not more than, the traditional displays (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 252). The passages I read give no visitor total for Budapest (Filipová 2015p. 126; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249).

Politics and aftermath

Monuments were raised across greater Hungary, and parades, speeches and the dedication of monuments took place in many places outside Budapest (Filipová 2015p. 116). Székely says that national representation through art and architecture became a major element of official cultural politics after 1896 (Filipová 2015p. 34). The City Park remains, and some structures were rebuilt in durable materials. One of these now houses the Agricultural Museum (Filipová 2015p. 128).

Its place in the series

Székely judges Blána's plan of 1874 for a Budapest exhibition impossible to realise (Filipová 2015p. 28). Zichy's proposal of June 1891 for a universal exhibition combined national, international and universal aspects (Filipová 2015p. 32). In other printed versions he called the event a general national exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 40). Lánczy's 1911 proposal for a Budapest universal exhibition in 1917 was printed, but no parliamentary or ministerial records discuss it (Filipová 2015p. 36). Székely calls it the last attempt of its kind (Filipová 2015p. 38). The Industry Hall of 1885 was modelled on the exhibition hall of Vienna's 1873 fair (Filipová 2015p. 117).

Dates, names and accounts

The texts differ on the millennium date. Albert gives 1895 as the original year (Filipová 2015p. 116), while Székely gives 1894 (Filipová 2015p. 31). The texts also differ on scope. Albert says the Magyar exhibitions were aimed at national or regional audiences (Filipová 2015p. 115). Barenscott says the exhibition opened to an international audience (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249). Filipová, summarising Albert, writes that the two exhibitions were meant as international events but were turned into peripheral affairs (Filipová 2015p. 9). The name "Budapest Universal Exhibition" in the volume's index refers to the proposals, not to the 1896 event (Filipová 2015p. 352). The accounts also differ on the site of Konsztantinápoly Budapesten, which Albert places in Lágymányos and Barenscott on a Danube island (Filipová 2015p. 122; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 251).

How historians have interpreted it

Albert reads the exhibition as an inward-looking celebration of the nation, and sets it against Bucharest's outward-facing exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 115). Barenscott reads the exhibition as a place where the city's position between East and West was turned into exotic, bohemian and profitable entertainment (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 251). She deliberately calls it an exhibition rather than a world's fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 252). Houze concludes that national identity was an elusive concept at the Hungarian displays of these fairs (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 166). Filipová summarises Albert's argument that the exhibition was strongly motivated by a wish to put the city on the map of world exhibitions (Filipová 2015p. 6). Albert cites Barenscott's 2010 article on the exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 130).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Magyar national identity — Albert reads the exhibition as a celebration of Magyar history in which the Hungarian state framed every other group (Filipová 2015p. 126p. 121).
  • Multi-ethnic display — the Ethnographic Village placed non-Magyar houses within a Hungarian state identity, and the organisers played down the nationality problem (Filipová 2015p. 121p. 115).
  • Off-site spectacle — Barenscott argues that the subway, the guides and the Constantinople reconstruction drew visitors beyond the official grounds (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 250).
  • East and West — Ős-Budavára and Konsztantinápoly Budapesten used Ottoman imagery as entertainment, and Barenscott reads the city's position between East and West as marketable (Filipová 2015p. 122; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 251).
  • Unrealised plans — Székely traces the unrealised proposals of Blána, Zichy and Lánczy (Filipová 2015p. 31).

Questions for study

  1. Why did a national exhibition give so much room to the non-Magyar peoples of the kingdom, and how does the Ethnographic Village present them? (Filipová 2015p. 121)
  2. Albert describes the Hungarian exhibition as an internally focused celebration. How would the exhibition have looked had it been designed for international visitors, as Barenscott's account suggests? (Filipová 2015p. 126; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 249)
  3. Ős-Budavára placed the Ottoman occupation inside a city park. What did this do to the memory of that occupation? (Filipová 2015p. 122)
  4. Zichy's printed versions call the same event a universal exhibition in one draft and a general national exhibition in another. What does this change in vocabulary suggest about the organisers' aims? (Filipová 2015p. 40)

Introduction: The Margins of Exhibitions and Exhibitions StudiesMarta Filipováp. 1Asia

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

1A Capital in the Margins: Concepts for a Budapest Universal Exhibition between 1867 and 1917Miklós Székelyp. 23

Székely argues that the proposals for a universal exhibition in Budapest between 1867 and 1917 show how a semi-sovereign state presented itself at home and abroad. Within the Dual Monarchy Hungary had its own government but no independent foreign or military policy, so its exhibitions asserted a 'sovereign' and 'Hungarian' identity through pavilion architecture, the revival of historic styles and vernacular art, and commemoration. The chapter follows three planning efforts: Szilárd Blána's utopian permanent exhibition of 1874, Count Jenő Zichy's proposal to mark the 1896 Millennium with a universal exhibition, and Leo Lánczy's 1911 scheme for 1917, the fiftieth anniversary of Franz Joseph's coronation. Its evidence comes from Hungarian displays at Paris in 1867 and 1900, the forestry pavilion and ethnographic village at Vienna in 1873, and the Turin pavilion of 1911. Székely concludes that none of the Budapest universal exhibitions was realised, and that the plans show a modernising nation looking to its glorious past while displaying its present achievements abroad.

In the contents of this work

5The Nation for Itself: The 1896 Hungarian Millennium and the 1906 Romanian National General ExhibitionSamuel D. Albertp. 113

Albert compares two smaller national exhibitions that sit at the margins of the great world's fairs: the Hungarian Millennium Exhibition in Budapest in 1896 and the Romanian National General Exhibition in Bucharest in 1906. He argues that these events were aimed deliberately at national or regional audiences, and that this self-limitation, together with a historiography built on the large fairs, has kept them marginal. In Budapest the Historical Exhibition presented a thousand years of Magyar history in reconstructed period buildings, and the Ethnographic Village included houses of non-Magyar groups under Hungarian rule, subsumed into a Hungarian state identity. The Ős-Budavára amusement area turned three centuries of Ottoman and Habsburg rule into entertainment. Bucharest was built on virgin ground, centred on King Carol I and his link to Trajan, and its pavilions and medals claimed populations outside the kingdom. Albert concludes that the Hungarian exhibition looked inward and the Romanian outward, but that in both cases the nation was the chief beneficiary.

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
4.3Official and Unofficial Spaces of the Budapest Millennial Exhibition, 1896Dorothy Barenscottp. 249

Barenscott examines the 1896 Budapest Millennial Exhibition, which the author deliberately calls an exhibition rather than a world's fair, and asks how visitors perceived it. The official fairgrounds in a city park offered the conventional repertoire of nineteenth-century expositions: monuments to national heroes, a neo-classical Palace of Art and a tidy map of pavilions, which the foreign press praised as meeting Western standards. Barenscott argues that the most vivid experiences lay beyond the grounds: Budapest's new electric underground railway, guidebooks promoting its cafes, cabarets and baths, and a reconstructed Constantinople on an island in the Danube, which offered night-time illusion and a safe encounter with the Ottoman east. The argument is that the city's position between East and West was packaged as exotic, bohemian and profitable, and that commercialized fairs of the 1890s moved spectacle off the fairgrounds. The text breaks off in the middle of this argument, with a comparison to Chicago in 1893 still open, so no conclusion is given.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Houze follows Hungarian decorative art through four later international exhibitions, from Paris 1900 to Milan 1906, and mentions the 1896 Budapest exhibition as their starting point. She argues that the Paris 1900 national pavilion was a smaller version of the 1896 City Park complex, and that Lechner's folk-inspired decorative designs and the Székely gates shown at the 1896 fairgrounds fed into later Hungarian displays. For Houze the ethnographic village at the 1896 exhibition belongs with folk displays in other exhibitions, and she concludes that national identity at these fairs was 'an elusive concept' shaped by political, economic and fashionable aims. She refers readers to her own 2012 article on the 1896 ethnographic display.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.
