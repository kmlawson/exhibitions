International Exhibition of Arts and Techniques in Modern Life

Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques dans la Vie Moderne
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The International Exhibition of Arts and Techniques in Modern Life (Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques dans la Vie Moderne) opened in Paris in 1937, on the Trocadero and Champ de Mars, and closed on 2 November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 295). It was the largest Paris exhibition so far (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). Historians value it because its German and Soviet pavilions faced each other across the site, and because its French organisers offered a middle way between ideologies that the fair could not sustain (Kargon et al. 2015p. 18; Kargon et al. 2015p. 29).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair was a sequel to the 1925 decorative arts exhibition. Planning began in 1928, stopped after the Stavisky riots of February 1934, and was revived under Doumergue, who appointed Edmond Labbé commissioner general (Kargon et al. 2015p. 8). One account dates the first plan to 1936, as a show of decorative arts and modern industry under the Minister of Commerce, later moved to 1937 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 290). The Depression then turned the fair towards economic recovery and the fight against unemployment, and 718 murals were commissioned (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 290). After the left-wing Popular Front government took office in June 1936, pavilions were added, including Le Corbusier's Pavillon des Temps Nouveaux and a pavilion for the CGT trade union confederation (Kargon et al. 2015p. 13).

The title broke the older idea of a universal exhibition into nationalism, a split between arts and technics, and the transforming power of art and science (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 284). In practice art became artisanship and science became technology (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 284). Labbé and Paul Leon presented France as a third way between subsistence production of an Asiatic kind and American production (Kargon et al. 2015p. 11).

Organisation and finance

The works ran late. Strikes, bureaucratic delay and changes of plan pushed the opening back again and again (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 285; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 291). Labbé resigned in despair, and Parisian workers demanded guaranteed work after the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 285). Unions would allow extra labour only if the government found other jobs for the workers when the fair closed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 114). An Architectural Record correspondent praised the fair's conception but found its aims foiled by poor coordination (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 284).

The texts disagree on the figures. The Encyclopedia gives 34 million visitors and a loss of 13 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294). The Historical Dictionary gives some thirty-one million visitors and a loss of 495 million francs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 288; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 288), and its closing verdict is fewer than 32 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 289). The BIE table in Allwood, Allan and Reid and the table in Roche give 34 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 209; Roche 2000p. 43). Kargon and co-authors give over twenty-four million (Kargon et al. 2015p. 7). Officials pointed to gains: theatre audiences rose, Louvre and Versailles admissions doubled, and hotel guests increased (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294). The fair closed with 16,704 prizes and over 600 congresses (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294).

Site and architecture

The fair covered the whole Trocadero and Champ de Mars area and closed the gap to the Invalides on both banks (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). The 1878 Trocadero Palace was pulled down with little protest (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 285). The texts date its demolition differently: Geppert gives 1934 (Geppert 2010p. 256), Çelik gives 1935 (Çelik 1992p. 221–222). Its replacement, the Palais de Chaillot by Carlu, Boileau and Azéma, was to be modern but not too modern (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 292). Chandler notes that some observers saw its neoclassicism as a prime symbol of fascism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 292), and that its colonnade conjures up an architecture of fascism (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 286). Chermayeff called it retrogressive (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168). Le Corbusier was excluded from the design teams, so his followers set up a tent beyond the Porte Maillot to show his city plans (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 286; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 286).

Nations and colonies on show

The German and Soviet pavilions were the most popular foreign exhibits, with Italy a distant third (Kargon et al. 2015p. 18). Both stood on the central axis facing each other (Roche 2000p. 99). Speer's German pavilion had a tower of fifty-four metres and an eagle on a swastika (Greenhalgh 1988p. 130). The Soviet pavilion carried Vera Mukhina's statue of a worker couple (Kargon et al. 2015p. 20). The British pavilion drew criticism for its genteel leisure, golf balls and cardboard Chamberlain (Allwood et al. 2001p. 114; Greenhalgh 1988p. 133). The Spanish pavilion held Picasso's Guernica, begun after the bombing of the Basque town of Guernica in April 1937 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 134; Gelernter 1995p. 230).

The colonial sections stood on the Ile des Cygnes, an island whose capacity platforms quadrupled (Hale 2008p. 112). The commissioner, Henry Berenger, had said in 1936 that the fair would not repeat the scale or the exoticism of 1931 (Hale 2008p. 111). Greenhalgh found the floating pavilions noncommittal beside the 1931 Vincennes exhibition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 69). Hale argues that the displays moved from spectacle to traditional crafts, craft schools and a rhetoric of partnership, while white superiority stayed in place (Hale 2008p. 111). Hale calls the fair the last major government attempt to show sub-Saharan Africa to the metropolitan public (Hale 2008p. 92).

Peoples on display

Hale's fullest case is the Miss France d'Outre-Mer contest, proposed by Maurice de Waleffe and approved by Berenger and Labbé (Hale 2008p. 138). It was held on 23 July 1937 in the Moroccan pavilion, where crowds broke ticket windows, and Monique Casalan of Guadeloupe was chosen days later (Hale 2008p. 139). No Arab, Berber or central African candidates took part. Organisers filled those places with women from the Antilles and Indochina, which Hale reads as showing that North African women were not seen as mothers of French children (Hale 2008p. 139). The Equatorial African section brought fourteen Africans to Paris (Hale 2008p. 113), and two Cameroonian artisans were poisoned by carbon monoxide from an illegal stove (Hale 2008p. 114).

Visitors and reception

Most observers counted the fair as "something less than an unqualified success" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294). Exhibitors withheld technical information, and guards banned photography (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 289). Anne O'Hare McCormick, a New York Times correspondent quoted by Kargon and co-authors, called the fair the first to display political parties and symbols as distinct from nations (Kargon et al. 2015p. 18).

Politics and controversy

Chandler ends the account with the sentence "The ritual of peace and progress was over." (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 289) Hopson argues that Hitler's Reich tried from the start to dominate the fair and present Germany as a peaceful cultural power and a bastion against communism (Hopsonp. 147). Roche says the two pavilions were deliberately placed to face each other (Roche 2000p. 99). Benedict reads the towers as giant symbolic totems (Benedict 1983p. 17). Greenhalgh says the publicity claimed a universal will to peace, while the exhibition was "saturated with an abrasive nationalism" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 130; Greenhalgh 1988p. 130). Sarah Wilson, quoted by Greenhalgh, notes that the Spanish civil war was left out of the visitors' book while Germany had a large space (Greenhalgh 1988p. 135).

Aftermath and legacy

Chandler records exhibitors going home to countries preparing to arm (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 295). Hopson reports Rivoirard's view that the fair's legacy may lie in being part of the last stand of peace (Hopsonp. 148). Maloney shows New York's landscape designers borrowing the Paris lighting methods, and Jacques Greber was later retained as a consultant for New York (Maloney 2012p. 179; Maloney 2012p. 179). Molella and Knowles note that Soviet pavilion designers from Paris and New York returned for Brussels 1958 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 28). Hale notes that a decade after 1937 the French public discovered that most Indochinese did not want the guidance France had offered (Hale 2008p. 160).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh argues that 1937 was the last Exposition Universelle, that its peace message hid an abrasive nationalism, and that Paris and New York both failed as modernism because they lost sight of human needs (Greenhalgh 1988p. 130; Greenhalgh 1988p. 170). Chandler, in both encyclopedia entries, reads the fair as a decorative arts show reshaped by the Depression and ending in a mixed balance: a large deficit against gains in theatre, transport and hotels (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 288; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294). Kargon and co-authors argue that France imagined itself as a "rational middle way between contending ideologies", a message the Soviet and German pavilions overshadowed (Kargon et al. 2015p. 29); with hindsight they judge that hope a forlorn one (Kargon et al. 2015p. 29). Hopson compares Paris with Nagoya and argues that both fairs combined lofty and banal aims and neither stopped the drift to war (Hopsonp. 148). Roche places Paris in a run of over-frequent late-1930s expositions that the 1928 BIE agreement did not prevent (Roche 2000p. 96). Hale reads the colonial displays as partnership rhetoric that kept hierarchy in place (Hale 2008p. 111), and Benedict treats the fair as competitive national symbolism (Benedict 1983p. 17).

The scholars agree that the German-Soviet confrontation was the fair's central image (Kargon et al. 2015p. 18; Greenhalgh 1988p. 131; Benedict 1983p. 17). They differ over what the fair was trying to say. Kargon and co-authors read a French message of harmony; Greenhalgh and Chandler read a propaganda contest. They also differ over Guernica: Allwood, Allan and Reid call it an apocalyptic statement on war (Allwood et al. 2001p. 114), while Hopson stresses its political and artistic legacy (Hopsonp. 147).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan had a pavilion by Junzō Sakakura, who had spent five years in Le Corbusier's office and was one of his students (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 286). The building was modernist but housed traditional crafts such as baskets, lacquerware, metalwork and ceramics (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25). Moholy-Nagy praised it as both Japanese and modern (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25). The commissioner general, Suganami Sohji, wrote in the fair's magazine in July and August 1937 that Japan would show its most modern aspects, and that it had taken on Western civilisation and adapted it (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25; Kargon et al. 2015p. 26; Kargon et al. 2015p. 26).

In practice the Ministry of Commerce and Industry chose the contents, and a separate body took charge of the exhibits (Kargon et al. 2015p. 26; Kargon et al. 2015p. 27). Kargon and co-authors judge the displays largely traditional and touristic, with a photomural of Mount Fuji and kimono-clad women (Kargon et al. 2015p. 26). Some exhibits were first shown in Japan and drew sharp press criticism there (Kargon et al. 2015p. 26). The association that organised Japan's participation published an administrative report in 1939 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25). Kōno, a member of the Nippon Kōbō design group, helped design the photographic murals in the Japanese pavilions at Paris (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Kargon and co-authors note that war had just broken out with China as the fair opened (Kargon et al. 2015p. 26).

Indochina

Indochina had its own pavilion in the French colonial section on the Ile des Cygnes (Greenhalgh 1988p. 69; Hale 2008p. 112). The architects abandoned authenticity: they took a segment of Angkor Wat as the model but topped it with a copy of the Tower of Bayon (Hale 2008p. 157). Twenty-two artisans in ten workshops, among them weavers, silk workers, lacquerers and silversmiths, worked in a small Indochinese village around the tower (Hale 2008p. 157).

Hale describes the French organisers' aim as instruction through colonial products, French improvements in health and education, miniature Indochinese communities and performances (Hale 2008p. 159). The peoples of Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos were shown as pliable members of the empire, with France as protector of a declining civilisation (Hale 2008p. 159). Hale says the performance did little to raise Indochina's standing, a point the Ministry of Commerce admitted (Hale 2008p. 159). The image of Indochina rested on Cambodia, although Vietnam had the larger population (Hale 2008p. 159; Hale 2008p. 159). Geppert expects Angkor replicas to recur at the 1937 fair (Geppert 2010p. 191).

Other Asian presence

The texts say nothing about South Asian or other Southeast Asian displays at the fair, and name no Asian visitors or observers apart from the Japanese commissioner (Kargon et al. 2015p. 25). The organisers' rhetoric used Asia as a foil: France's third way was defined against subsistence production of an Asiatic kind (Kargon et al. 2015p. 11).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Art and artisanship — Guernica apart, the texts describe the fair's art as decoration, and the colonial and regional sections present artisans rather than artists (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 292; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 287).
  • The German-Soviet confrontation — The two pavilions facing each other are the fair's most cited image, read as a sign of hostility before the war (Kargon et al. 2015p. 18; Greenhalgh 1988p. 131; Roche 2000p. 99; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 289).
  • Nationalism and propaganda — Every national pavilion promoted its own system, and the peace theme covered rising nationalism (Greenhalgh 1988p. 130; Greenhalgh 1988p. 130; Kargon et al. 2015p. 18).
  • Colonial display and craft — The colonial sections moved from the 1931 spectacle towards traditional crafts and partnership rhetoric (Hale 2008p. 111; Greenhalgh 1988p. 69).
  • Finance, labour and delay — The works ran late, and the texts give conflicting attendance and loss figures (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 285; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 294; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 288).
  • Modernism and architecture — The Chaillot Palace and the modern pavilions divided critics, and Greenhalgh judges that neither fair served human needs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 292; Greenhalgh 1988p. 170).

Questions for study

  1. Was the French "third way" of artisanship a serious programme, or a presentation that the Soviet and German pavilions made impossible to sustain?
  2. How should a student weigh the 34 million, 31 million and 24 million visitor figures when the reference works disagree?
  3. Did the colonial displays teach partnership, as their organisers claimed, or reinforce hierarchy, as Hale argues?
  4. Was Guernica a moral statement, a political act, or both, and does the answer change with the pavilion it hung in?
  5. Why did Japan's pavilion present itself as modern while its contents were traditional, and who decided that balance?

Kargon and co-authors devote their second chapter to the 1937 Paris exposition, which they read as a case of 'modernity a la francaise'. Substantial discussion 3

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
chapter 'Between the Wars' (1924-38), pp. 113-116, Paris section (no separate author)

Allwood, Allan and Reid give the 1937 Paris exposition a three-page section in a chapter on the interwar years, calling it the largest Paris exhibition so far. Their emphasis is on the staging: the Eiffel Tower illumination, the replacement of the 1878 Trocadéro by the Palais de Chaillot, and the German-Soviet confrontation, including Speer’s account of seeing the secret Soviet sketch. They describe the British pavilion as a picture of genteel leisure that drew criticism, and present Guernica in the Spanish pavilion as a statement on the horrors of war. The section sits within a survey of 150 years of exhibitions and presents the 1937 fair as one stage in that history.

Moderate discussion 1

Hopson, Nathan. “‘A Bad Peace?’: The 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition.” Journal article.
section 'Nagoya and Paris' (pp. 146-148)

Hopson's article on the 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition uses the Paris 1937 fair as its main comparison, calling Paris far larger and more internationally prominent. He argues that the two expositions were alike in being self-promotion in industry, trade, science and empire, in being built into their cities, and in being shaped by rising tension, with Germany's Reich seeking to dominate Paris as a peaceful cultural power. His conclusion is that both combined lofty and banal aims and neither defused the drift to war. He quotes the architect Philippe Rivoirard on the 'absurd' peace tower at Paris and Rivoirard's view that the fair's legacy may lie in being part of the last stand of peace.

Brief discussion 8

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
PDF p. 14 (illustrations list); PDF p. 26 (photo caption and potlatch passage); PDF p. 35 (pavilion towers); PDF p. 49 (Table 3); printed page numbers not visible in the extraction

Benedict's essay on world's fairs uses the 1937 Paris fair as an example of competitive national symbolism. He describes the Soviet and German pavilions facing each other across the mall, their towers topped by a worker and a worker-farmer group with hammer and sickle and by an eagle on a swastika, which he calls giant symbolic totems designed to impress. He applies the potlatch model with caution and suggests that such fairs may rearrange status hierarchies and validate the rise of the middle class. Otherwise the 1937 fair appears in his tables comparing fairs.

Wikipedia

The Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques dans la Vie Moderne (International Exposition Dedicated to Art and Technology in Modern Life) was held from 25 May to 25 November 1937 in Paris, France. Both the Palais de Chaillot, housing the Musée de l'Homme, and the Palais de Tokyo, which houses the Musée d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, were created for this exhibition that was officially sanctioned by the Bureau International des Expositions. A third building, Palais d'Iéna, housing the permanent Museum of Public Works, which was originally to be among the new museums created on the hill of Chaillot on the occasion of the Exhibition, was not built until January 1937 and inaugurated in March 1939.
