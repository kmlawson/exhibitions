International Colonial and Export Trade Exhibition (Amsterdam)

Internationale Koloniale en Uitvoerhandel Tentoonstelling
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Colonial and Export Trade Exhibition (Internationale Koloniale en Uitvoerhandel Tentoonstelling) ran in Amsterdam from 1 May to 31 October 1883 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79). Its organisers presented it as a colonial fair because the Netherlands was known mainly for its empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72). Rydell and Della Coletta both call it Europe's first international exhibition devoted primarily to colonialism (Rydell 1993p. 61; Della Coletta 2006p. 96). Historians cite it as the start of official living exhibits of colonial peoples, and as a case of a small European country using a world fair to show its colonies (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 27; Cotép. 2).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and organisation

The fair grew out of commercial ambition in Amsterdam. Findling writes that the Netherlands had little economic growth in the first half of the nineteenth century, but that the Noord Canal, completed in 1876, brought ocean-going ships to Amsterdam and a new central station opened in 1879 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72). In 1880 the French entrepreneur Edouard Agostini saw potential for a fair in the Netherlands and mailed brochures to influential Amsterdam business leaders (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). A local committee brought Agostini to Amsterdam as technical adviser. Findling gives the committee's reasoning for the theme: "Because the Netherlands was not well known except for the wealth of its colonial empire, the committee decided to organize the fair as a colonial exhibition" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72). Gregory gives the same reasoning in shorter form (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78).

National support was minimal. Findling says a preliminary report went to King William I, who endorsed the plan, and that this was about all the help the organisers received from the government (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). Gregory names King William II as the monarch who endorsed the plan (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). The two entries differ on this point, and the texts do not settle it. Both say the committee could not obtain national funding, and that the city of Amsterdam gave the use of 62 acres in its western section (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). Both say the committee then contracted a Belgian firm to develop the site and build the main hall (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). Neither entry gives a profit or loss figure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78).

Site and architecture

The main building was the centre of the site. Findling and Gregory both give its size as about 1,000 by 420 feet, covering some 13 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). It was a temporary wooden structure with a glass roof, covered in plaster and painted canvas to look like marble. Its facade had an Indian motif, with plaster elephant heads and lions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). The Colonial Building measured 417 by 250 feet, and the Machinery Hall and Art Palace were each 667 by 100 feet (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). Findling says ethnography and fine arts were placed in the nearby Rijksmuseum, probably because it was far more fireproof than the main building (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). Smaller attractions included a pavilion for the city of Amsterdam, a Japanese bazaar, shops and restaurants, and a canal with a bamboo bridge that held a Chinese junk (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78).

Nations and colonies on show

Only the Netherlands and Belgium had comprehensive exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). Gregory lists other participants: most European countries, China, Japan, India, Siam, the Dutch East Indies, New South Wales and Victoria, Persia, Turkey, Egypt, Tunisia and Mauritius, and from the Western Hemisphere the United States, Canada, Jamaica, Haiti, Brazil, Uruguay and Venezuela (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). Findling gives a similar list and says some 40 other countries or colonies took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). The texts describe few of these displays in any detail.

Peoples on display

The main attraction was a set of model villages, with natives brought from Dutch colonies in the Caribbean and Southeast Asia, who displayed their way of life (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). The texts do not name the peoples. Findling and Gregory both link these villages to the anthropological displays later used at American fairs. Findling calls them a foreshadowing of exhibits using Filipinos, a staple of US fairs in the early twentieth century (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79). The Hollengreen volume dates the start of official living exhibits of colonial peoples, organised, funded and managed by the exposition companies, to the 1883 Amsterdam colonial exposition (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 27). Della Coletta says that after 1883 native villages with vendors and dancing performers were routinely recreated at fairs, and that the native villages at Turin and Palermo were small by comparison (Della Coletta 2006p. 96).

Commerce, tea and Indian produce

The best documented commercial display was the free tea-room. Greenhalgh places the Amsterdam teahouse among the Indian and Ceylonese teahouses that the British erected at exhibitions from 1880, paid for mainly by the Indian Tea Syndicate to popularise tea among Europeans (Greenhalgh 1988p. 104; Greenhalgh 1988p. 104). Greenhalgh says some of the better class of visitors at first objected to drinking tea without payment, but that the tea-room was soon thronged (Greenhalgh 1988p. 104). Findling reports the same unease and says the Syndicate subsidised the tea, which was a success (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73).

Hoffenberg places India's displays in a commercial frame. The Indian government and private parties sent extensive commercial displays to the fair (Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). T. N. Mukharji, one of the Indian government's representatives at Amsterdam, displayed the manufactures of British India to strengthen trade links and open new markets (Hoffenberg 2001p. 100; Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). The Bombay Chamber of Commerce set up a subcommittee to forward goods to Amsterdam, which it saw as a market for growing exports from Bombay (Hoffenberg 2001p. 100). Carritt, agent for the tea syndicate, organised tea displays at Amsterdam and Calcutta to show that exhibitions could advertise tea (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114).

Visitors and reception

Both entries give attendance as just over a million and the closing date as 31 October (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79). In India, the Calcutta newspaper The Statesman and Friend of India praised Mukharji's Amsterdam catalogue for its scientific and vernacular names and its notes on uses (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53). Hoffenberg says the analyses prepared for the Glasgow and Amsterdam shows became common reference points for later writers on Indian art (Hoffenberg 2001p. 55).

Aftermath and legacy

Findling says the fair was an important influence on the 1885 exposition at Antwerp (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). The Antwerp entry says that the decision to display the Congo and Africans there was partly influenced by displays at Amsterdam in 1883 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Prina writes that the Italian government coordinated its participation at Antwerp to avoid the criticism it had received at Paris in 1878 and at Amsterdam, where state sponsorship had not been fully granted (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55). Okurka notes that the Amsterdam site was smaller than the grounds of the Liberec exhibition of 1906 (Filipová 2015p. 99). Rydell says that after 1883 the imperial powers routinely organised colonial expositions to build support for imperial policy (Rydell 1993p. 61). Allwood and colleagues place the Amsterdam fair in a wave of 1880s exhibitions across Belgium and the Netherlands, with Antwerp in 1885 and Brussels in 1888 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55).

The Dutch and their empire on show

Greenhalgh sets the Dutch apart from the other European exhibitors. He says they concentrated almost exclusively on the history of their empire and on its basis in trade, whereas the British tried to tie empire into the national economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 64; Greenhalgh 1988p. 64). He calls Holland and Belgium the most consistent European exhibitors of empire after Britain and France, and says Dutch colonial displays went to every Paris Exposition Universelle and to South Kensington in 1862 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). In 1931 the Dutch still showed an imperial spirit at the Paris colonial exhibition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). There the Netherlands built its own pavilion (Geppert 2010p. 180), and a fire destroyed that pavilion on the night of 27 June 1931 (Geppert 2010p. 198).

Coté adds that the Dutch made wide use of native exhibitions, including working villages, both at home and at Dutch displays abroad, and lists Amsterdam in 1883 among the fairs with Dutch exhibits (Cotép. 2).

The tea syndicate's wider circuit

The Indian Tea Districts Association exhibited and sold tea at nearly all the major exhibitions from Sydney in 1879 to the Franco-British exhibition of 1908 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114). Thomas Carritt also worked on tea exhibitions and sales in Belgium, Holland and Germany. The association's managers, however, cared most about England, and turned to the South Kensington shows of the 1880s (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). At the International Health Exhibition of 1884, which drew over four million visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115), more than 150,000 cups were sold at 3p a cup (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). At the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in 1886 the court sold over 24,000 lb of tea in packets, and a further 300,000 cups were drunk at the Tea Court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).

Hoffenberg records that at Paris in 1889 twelve attendants who spoke French served tea (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117), and that at Chicago in 1893 Indian servants in uniform staffed the tea house (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). The texts say nothing about who served tea at Amsterdam.

Mukharji's catalogues and later work

Mukharji's Amsterdam catalogue was one part of a larger written project. He proposed "a sort of Index to the manufactures and raw materials" of India, so that visitors could look up what they saw in the courts (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54). His survey of artware gave catalogue notes, the reactions of jurors at earlier shows, and the uses of each object (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54). The Anglo-Indian officer George Watt adopted many of Mukharji's analytical interpretations in his later dictionaries of Indian products (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54).

Three years after Amsterdam, at a silk conference during the Colonial and Indian Exhibition, Mukharji rose as a native of India to thank Thomas Wardle, and committed himself to persuading native reelers to improve their methods (Hoffenberg 2001p. 112). Hoffenberg reads his embrace of European reeling technology as a sign of unresolved tension in Indian national culture about machinery and the market (Hoffenberg 2001p. 113).

Amsterdam as a precedent

A Madrid newspaper, La época, used the Amsterdam fair when it argued that Barcelona should host Spain's exhibition. It listed "Holland in Amsterdam" among major fairs held outside the capital cities (Filipová 2015p. 59). On the Belgian side, Théophile Fumière, a Belgian architect (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 60), published a work in Brussels in 1883 called L'exposition d'Amsterdam et la Belgique aux Pays-Bas (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 69). The texts give only its title. None of them reports what it says about the Belgian section.

Zoos and living displays

Santesso argues that the histories of the world's fair and the zoo are "deeply intertwined" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41). He lists Amsterdam among the fairs that cooperated with local zoos (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41). None of the texts says what that cooperation meant at Amsterdam, or whether any of its villagers came through a zoo.

Greenhalgh describes a Javanese village at a French exhibition, subtitled "Life in the Dutch Indies" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 89). Its manager had lived in Java for seventeen years and chose the villagers (Greenhalgh 1988p. 89). Greenhalgh finds no evidence that the Dutch government ran it (Greenhalgh 1988p. 89).

Visitors as a social mass

Hoffenberg argues that exhibition officials sorted crowds into social groups and portraits of the empire (Hoffenberg 2001p. 203). His examples are the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, which brought colonists from across the empire to South Kensington (Hoffenberg 2001p. 205). He says that dividing visitors by labour, costume and other criteria made an undifferentiated mass look orderly (Hoffenberg 2001p. 204). The chapter has no passage on Amsterdam, so the texts do not show how its visitors were classed.

How historians have interpreted it

The reference entries are mainly narratives. Findling argues that the Dutch chose a colonial theme because the Netherlands was not well known apart from its colonial empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72). Gregory argues that the native villages foreshadowed the anthropological displays later used at American fairs, and calls the 1883 fair the last major international exhibition held in Amsterdam (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79). Hollengreen and colleagues treat the Amsterdam displays as part of a persistent series of ethnological exhibits, which they link to a wider attempt to counter the rise of the artificial (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 43).

Hoffenberg reads the Indian catalogues as a way of organising knowledge about India for trade, since the official guidebooks for Calcutta and Amsterdam were built with mercantile interests in mind (Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). Greenhalgh treats the tea-room as part of the commercial side of displays of peoples and judges the ploy a success (Greenhalgh 1988p. 104). Rydell reads colonial expositions as instruments of imperial policy at home and in the colonies (Rydell 1993p. 61). Coté places the Dutch exhibits at fairs, Amsterdam among them, within the Netherlands' effort to keep its international standing (Cotép. 2). Prina places Amsterdam in the politics of Italian state sponsorship (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55).

The texts agree on the date, the colonial theme, the model villages and the tea-room. They differ on emphasis: Findling and Gregory stress ethnological display, Hoffenberg stresses trade and knowledge, Greenhalgh stresses money, and Rydell stresses imperial propaganda. Geppert and Lawn list the fair without argument (Geppert 2010p. 251; Lawn 2009p. 13).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India and Ceylon

India is the Asian presence the texts document best. The Indian government and private parties sent extensive commercial displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). The Revenue and Agriculture Department issued a classified list of Indian produce for the fair, compiled by T. N. Mukharji as officer in charge of the Indian exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 307). Hoffenberg calls Mukharji a South Asian expert and quotes Mukharji's aim for the Amsterdam catalogue: "the object... is to interest European visitors at the Exhibition in the old Indian arts" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54). Hoffenberg adds that the official guidebooks for Calcutta and Amsterdam were built with mercantile interests in mind (Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). The Statesman and Friend of India, published in Calcutta, praised the Amsterdam catalogue's scientific and vernacular names and its notes on uses (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53).

Mukharji displayed the manufactures of British India to strengthen trade links and open markets, and the Bombay Chamber of Commerce sent goods, seeing Amsterdam as a market for growing exports from Bombay (Hoffenberg 2001p. 100).

The tea-room was the other Indian presence. Greenhalgh counts the Amsterdam teahouse among the Indian and Ceylonese teahouses the British erected, paid for mainly by the Indian Tea Syndicate. Greenhalgh says dozens of Indian and Ceylonese people were brought to Europe to work in these teahouses, but does not say this of Amsterdam (Greenhalgh 1988p. 104; Greenhalgh 1988p. 104). Findling calls the Amsterdam teahouse the Indian tea house (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73). Carritt, the tea syndicate's agent, displayed tea samples, working models and gardens where visitors could sit and drink tea at Amsterdam and Calcutta (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114).

China, Japan and Siam

China, Japan and Siam are named among the participants (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). The texts also record a Japanese bazaar and a Chinese junk on the canal, but say nothing about who organised or paid for them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). They describe no displays from these countries in further detail.

The Dutch East Indies

The Dutch East Indies took part as a colony (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 78). The model villages were brought from Dutch colonies in the Caribbean and Southeast Asia, and the texts do not say which Southeast Asian peoples or islands these were (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79).

Interpretation

Findling and Gregory read the native villages as foreshadowing later anthropological displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 73; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 79). Hoffenberg reads the Indian exhibits as part of imperial knowledge organised for markets (Hoffenberg 2001p. 111). Greenhalgh judges the teahouse ploy a success (Greenhalgh 1988p. 104). The texts name no Asian visitor or observer apart from Mukharji, whose catalogues, Hoffenberg says, became common reference points (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53; Hoffenberg 2001p. 55).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial format — the Dutch chose a colonial theme because the Netherlands was little known apart from its empire; Rydell and Della Coletta call the fair Europe's first international exhibition devoted primarily to colonialism (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Rydell 1993; Della Coletta 2006).
  • Model villages and living exhibits — natives from Dutch colonies were shown in model villages; Hollengreen dates official living exhibits to this fair, and Della Coletta describes routine native villages after 1883 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Della Coletta 2006).
  • Trade and tea — the free tea-room was subsidised by the tea trade, and India's catalogues were tied to markets (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • State support and reputation — no national funding, and city land for the site (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990); Coté ties Dutch fairs to the country's international position (Coté), and Prina ties Italian participation to state sponsorship and criticism (Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Legacy for later fairs — influence on Antwerp in 1885, and a site comparison with Liberec in 1906 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Filipová 2015; Allwood et al. 2001).

Questions for study

  1. Why did a country known mainly for its empire choose a colonial theme, and what did that choice ask visitors to think about the Netherlands?
  2. The texts date the start of official living exhibits to this fair. What evidence would a historian need to test that claim?
  3. Did the free tea-room aim mainly to sell tea, or to persuade Europeans about the empire? Which texts support each reading?
  4. The fair had no national funding but was built with municipal land and a Belgian contractor. What does that suggest about who controlled the displays?
  5. Hoffenberg treats Indian officials as producers of knowledge about India. How far do the texts let us hear Indian views of the fair?
  6. Who served the tea at Amsterdam? The association used French-speaking attendants at Paris in 1889 and Indian staff at Chicago, but the texts do not say which it did at Amsterdam. What evidence would show whether the Amsterdam tea-room was run by Indians or by Europeans?
  7. Who chose the Amsterdam villagers, and what authority did their manager have? The Javanese village Greenhalgh describes had one manager who selected the villagers, but no text names a manager for the Amsterdam villages.
  8. Greenhalgh says the Dutch focused on the history of their empire and its trade (Greenhalgh 1988p. 64), while Findling says the Dutch chose a colonial theme because the country was little known otherwise (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 72). Does the trade emphasis explain the tea and Indian catalogues better than it explains the model villages?

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
Women and the Semarang International Colonial Exhibitionp. 28Asia

Coté treats the women's pavilion as the one place where colonial and indigenous interests seemed to meet. The European section reused exhibits from the Amsterdam exhibition De Vrouw 1813-1913, and the pavilion also honoured Kartini and the teacher Dewi Sartika and included a Chinese girls' school. Coté shows, however, that the souvenir commentary by Maria Kooy-van Zeggelen treated indigenous women as needing awakening by Western ideas, so that progressivism shaped even this display.

Della Coletta, Cristina. World’s Fairs Italian-Style: The Great Expositions in Turin and Their Narratives, 1860–1915. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2006.
3Emilio Salgari: Writing Exposition Stylep. 123Asia

Della Coletta reads Emilio Salgari's popular adventure novels as 'exposition narratives', texts that carry the encyclopedic and classifying habits of the world's fairs into fiction. The chapter opens with the 1883 publicity stunt that launched Sandokan, the Malayan Tiger, and shows how Salgari assembled his jungle cycle from borrowed travel and scientific passages taken from periodicals and illustrated volumes. It then examines the ethnic inventories in I pirati della Malesia and Le pantere di Algeri, which rank peoples against a white European baseline. Sandokan's exoticism, the chapter argues, is only skin-deep: he is a Risorgimento hero modelled on Garibaldi as Dumas presented him in the Memoirs of Garibaldi, and the novels' anti-British plots leave their colonial stance unresolved. The final section turns to Le meraviglie del Duemila, a 1907 novel set in a technocratic New York of 2003, where Della Coletta pits the natural sublime against the technological sublime and reads the protagonists' madness and the fall of the utopia as a victory for entropy. The chapter concludes that Salgari's faith in science heralds no rebirth.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
3Commissioners and the State: Power and Controversy at the Exhibitionsp. 63Asia

This chapter asks how exhibition commissioners justified their authority as a new cultural elite, and why that authority drew criticism. Hoffenberg argues that commissioners presented themselves as disinterested experts serving the state, yet their work of selecting, pricing and displaying objects exposed them to charges of self-interest and commerce. The main evidence is the Crystal Palace of 1851, where the executive commissioners forbade price tags, and the London International of 1862, which radical papers denounced as a jobbery run by a 'Science and Art clique'. Colonial cases, including the Melbourne Centennial Exhibition, the Adelaide Jubilee show of 1887 and the sales rules at Calcutta in 1883-84, show how on-site selling set commissioners against local merchants. Hoffenberg then compares state funding in Australia and India, which was direct and generous, with England's ad hoc royal commissions, and traces critics from Babbage to the radical press who attacked the commissioners from opposite sides. The chapter ends by treating exhibitions as contests for public opinion, in which the shows revealed the tensions they were meant to conceal.

In the contents of this work

4Consumers, Producers, and Markets: The Political Economy of Imperial Federationp. 99Asia

Hoffenberg examines how Australian and Indian commissioners, merchants and critics used international exhibitions to argue about the economics of empire. The displays of wool, coal, wine, cotton, silk and tea are read as political economies in their own right rather than neutral reflections of trade. Hoffenberg follows the Australian and Indian commodity courts from the Great Exhibition of 1851 through the London International Exhibition of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, with further material from Sydney in 1879 and Calcutta in 1883. Tea provides the chapter's clearest case: the Indian Tea Districts Association sold samples in London and then in Australia and North America, and the chapter traces how James Inglis and Jules Joubert encouraged direct trade between Australia and India without English middlemen. The argument is that colonial producers pursued independent commerce within an imperial framework, and that critics such as Carlyle and the radical press rejected the market ideals the shows celebrated. The chapter ends by noting that the displays both reinforced imperial economic unity and exposed its strains, which opens onto the question of colonial trade policy.

In the contents of this work

7Imperial and National Taxonomies: Entertaining and Policing Exhibition Visitorsp. 203Asia

Hoffenberg turns from exhibits to the people who visited exhibitions and to the ways commissioners classified, entertained and policed them. Visitors to the Great Exhibition of 1851, the London International of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 are treated as a visible social mass that organisers sorted by nation, race, class and gender. The evidence includes reduced fares for London workers, the appeal to guilds from neighbouring colonies for the Melbourne Intercolonial of 1866 to 1867, and the controversial zenana day at the Calcutta International of 1883 to 1884, when women in purdah were admitted to the grounds. Maori and Fijian performers at Sydney in 1879, and Aboriginal visitors from mission stations at Adelaide in 1887, are set against the static ethnographic models shown elsewhere, and the separate entrances at Nagpore in 1865 show how race and rank were policed at the door. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions offered social integration and imperial federation in economic and cultural terms, a citizenship that was real but not political. The chapter closes by linking these practices of ordering visitors to later management of migration in the twentieth century.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Amsterdam 1883 entry by Michael L. Gregory, printed p. 79 onward (dictionary entry; bibliography at its end)

Gregory's dictionary entry gives a compact narrative of the 1883 Amsterdam fair, from Agostini's approach and the decision to present it as a colonial exhibition, through its buildings and its model villages of colonial peoples, to its closing. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.

On the web

Wikipedia

The International Colonial and Export Exhibition (Dutch: Internationale Koloniale en Uitvoerhandel Tentoonstelling; French: Exposition Universelle Coloniale et d'Exportation Générale) was a colonial exhibition (a type of World's Fair) held in Amsterdam from May 1 to October 1, 1883. The event drew at least a million visitors and was the first international colonial exhibition, with 28 different nations presenting their colonial trade and wealth. The event was the brainchild of Edouard Agostini, a French entrepreneur. Agostini, who had previously been involved in organizing the 1878 Exposition Universelle in Paris, presented his plans to the city of Amsterdam and King William III of the Netherlands in 1880. The Dutch government was initially hesitant, but Agostini managed to secure funding from Belgian and French investors.
