Greater Britain Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Greater Britain Exhibition was an imperial show held at Earl's Court, London, in 1899, staged by the impresario Imre Kiralfy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Geppert 2010p. 252). Australian officials reported that about 2,500,000 persons visited it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2). It included a Queensland court and an African display with re-enacted colonial wars (Hoffenberg 2001p. 290; Roche 2000p. 84). Greenhalgh links it to a jingoistic climate and to the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Organisers and site

Imre Kiralfy ran his shows at Earl's Court through a London company. Greenhalgh says he formed 'London Exhibitions Ltd.' in 1895 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91), and Hotta-Lister says he registered a company called 'London Exhibition Ltd.' in the same year (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39). Geppert dates the company's takeover of Earl's Court to 1894 (Geppert 2010p. 105). Geppert says that Kiralfy, with Harold T. Hartley as managing director and Paul Cremieu-Javal as chairman, staged nine expositions at Earl's Court over the following nine years (Geppert 2010p. 105), and names Hartley as a director of the Greater Britain Exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 269).

Empire and colony

Geppert says that every exhibition at Earl's Court was devoted to imperial themes, and that some of them, the Greater Britain Exhibition among them, showed "typical products of single colonies" (Geppert 2010p. 105). Hoffenberg says that Queensland's commissioners applied lessons from the show to Queensland's displays at Selfridge's in London (Hoffenberg 2001p. 79). A Victorian grower sent more than thirty cases of fruit a week to London for sale in the colony's court at the exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 106). Roche says that Kiralfy staged re-enactments of the Matabele War of 1893 and the Rhodesia revolt of 1896–7, and a 'Kaffir Kraal' of 174 Africans from various tribes, organised into four 'villages' (Roche 2000p. 84). Roche also says that European organisers used comparable displays to popularise and legitimise 'the scramble for Africa' (Roche 2000p. 84). The texts do not say who governed the place, or what the organisers hoped the show would do for empire, trade or settlement. Geppert says that Switzerland and Belgium had separate sections in 1899, and that most Earl's Court shows had no official participants from abroad (Geppert 2010p. 105).

Exhibits and visitors

Hoffenberg gives a visitor figure of "about 2,500,000 persons", reported by Australian officials, and cites a Queensland report for it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2; Hoffenberg 2001p. 290). The Graphic's visual report placed scenes from the opening, which the Duke of Cambridge attended, beside vignettes of the adjacent show (Qureshi 2011p. 256). The Illustrated London News printed a 'corner in Egypt', with Arabs and Victorian visitors at the Greater Britain Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 256). Greenhalgh says that after 1889 an Egyptian-themed 'Rue' was a feature of several fairs, including the Greater Britain Exhibition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 103).

An adjacent show on the same grounds

Qureshi treats Frank Fillis's Savage South Africa show as separate from the fair. Kiralfy owned Earl's Court, and Fillis managed the show (Qureshi 2011p. 222). It was held at the Empress Theatre, virtually alongside the fair, on the same grounds (Qureshi 2011p. 222). Qureshi says the show was "an unofficial adjunct to, not a component of, the Greater Britain Exhibition of 1899", and that its organisers distanced themselves from it (Qureshi 2011p. 256). The two were nevertheless conflated in advertising and the press (Qureshi 2011p. 256). Greenhalgh credits Kiralfy with the Savage South Africa Spectacle (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91), and Roche credits him with the African re-enactments (Roche 2000p. 84). Qureshi credits Fillis's show with its Ndebele war theme (Qureshi 2011p. 232). Geppert lists an Olympia show, Briton, Boer and Black in Savage South Africa, as organised without Kiralfy's direct involvement (Geppert 2010p. 103).

Its place in the series

Roche places the Greater Britain Exhibition among the imperial expos and festivals held in London in 1886 and 1899 (Roche 2000p. 42), and says that Kiralfy led the series of British imperial expos from 1899 to 1914 (Roche 2000p. 54). Greenhalgh says that the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 moved from complacent pride in empire to a propagandistic defence of it (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). By the time of the Greater Britain Exhibition, Greenhalgh writes, "a climate of jingoism had evolved" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). Greenhalgh also says that the Savage South Africa and Greater Britain events prepared the ground for Kiralfy's Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh (1988) reads the show as part of a jingoistic exhibition culture, and as a step towards the Franco-British Exhibition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). Roche (2000) reads comparable African displays as a way to popularise and legitimise the scramble for Africa (Roche 2000p. 84). Hoffenberg (2001) argues that colonial exhibitions linked producers and consumers in an imperial market (Hoffenberg 2001p. 21). Geppert (2010) stresses that the Earl's Court shows, the Greater Britain among them, were devoted to imperial themes and had little official foreign participation (Geppert 2010p. 105; Geppert 2010p. 105). Qureshi (2011) argues that Savage South Africa drew little interest from anthropologists (Qureshi 2011p. 256).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial spectacle — Geppert describes the Earl's Court shows as devoted to imperial themes, and cites the 1899 show among displays of colonial products (Geppert 2010p. 105).
  • Displayed peoples — Roche's account of a 'Kaffir Kraal' with 174 Africans is the main description of African displays in the texts (Roche 2000p. 84); Qureshi describes the separate Savage South Africa show on the same grounds (Qureshi 2011p. 222).
  • Colonial commerce — Hoffenberg shows colonial courts selling produce and applying lessons from the show in London (Hoffenberg 2001p. 79; Hoffenberg 2001p. 106).
  • Jingoism — Greenhalgh reads the show as part of an 1899 climate of jingoism (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58).
  • Attribution and conflation — the texts disagree over who ran the African shows, and the press conflated them (Qureshi 2011p. 256; Roche 2000p. 84; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the organisers of the Greater Britain Exhibition distance it from Savage South Africa, and what did press conflation do for each show? (Qureshi 2011p. 256)
  2. Did colonial courts such as Queensland and Victoria aim at sales or at display, and what can the texts tell us about each? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 79; Hoffenberg 2001p. 106)
  3. Who staged the African re-enactments: Kiralfy, as Roche and Greenhalgh say, or Fillis, as Qureshi says? (Roche 2000p. 84; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Qureshi 2011p. 232)
  4. How far did the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition continue the imperial themes of 1899, as Greenhalgh suggests? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91)

Qureshi, Sadiah. Peoples on Parade: Exhibitions, Empire, and Anthropology in Nineteenth-Century Britain. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2011.
4Recruiting Entertainersp. 126Asia

Qureshi asks how foreign peoples were recruited into the shows and how far they consented, arguing that neither victimhood nor simple agency captures their situation. The chapter covers the San children Flora and Martinus, taken in Africa by a merchant and later raised by a Croydon family, and the Aztec children Maximo and Bartola, who were probably sold by a trader. It then turns to Sara Baartman, who chose to sail to London, the Bakhoje and Anishinabe groups recruited through missionaries, the Zulus gathered by A. T. Caldecott in 1853, and prisoners drawn from Indian and Cape jails. Wild West and 1899 Savage South Africa performers appear under contracts that became standardised. Qureshi tests these arrangements against Herzog and Gerber's consent criteria and finds them largely unmet, but court cases involving the Zulu chiefs Manyos and Larcher show performers pressing their own demands. The chapter concludes that performers resisted, formed relationships and kept some dignity, while the consent obtained mainly served managers and officials.

In the contents of this work

5Interpreting Exhibitionsp. 155

Qureshi asks how audiences made sense of the London shows of displayed peoples, a question the chapter admits is hard to answer because most spectators left no records. Drawing on press reviews, satirical periodicals, images and travel literature, it maps a range of responses to performers including San, Zulu, Bakhoje and Anishinabe groups. Its main cases are the San children at the Egyptian Hall in 1845, the Zulu shows of 1853 and 1879, and Savage South Africa at Earl's Court in 1899, where a press campaign over women touching performers led the organisers to exclude women from the kraal. Patrons shook hands, gave gifts and questioned performers, while critics disputed whether they were authentic, and reviewers read the displays through travel accounts such as Lichtenstein's on the San and Isaacs's on the Zulus. Satirical papers turned the shows against fashionable society and humanitarian campaigners. The chapter concludes that spectators were critical interpreters rather than passive viewers, and that the shows' receptions were too varied to reduce to a single racist gaze.

7The End of an Affairp. 222Asia

Qureshi uses Frank Fillis's Savage South Africa, staged at Earl's Court in 1899, to show how African and Afrikaner performers were displayed in late Victorian London. The argument rests on the show's mock kraal, its reenactments of the Ndebele wars and the press coverage that framed them. Qureshi sets these against earlier British fairs, above all the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, where performers worked as attendants and craftsmen inside the main displays rather than in segregated villages. Paris in 1889 and Chicago in 1893 supply the contrasting model of the native village and the Midway. The chapter then turns to anthropologists such as Galton and Rivers, who used performers and collections at the 1886 exhibition, and argues that British researchers exploited displayed peoples less systematically than their German counterparts. It ends by suggesting that by 1900 the shows had become detached from anthropology and tied to entertainment and imperial spectacle.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Mandell, Richard D. Paris 1900: The Great World’s Fair. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1967.
FiveThe Affair and the Fair: Politics and the Expositionp. 89

Mandell argues that the Exposition of 1900 cannot be understood apart from the Dreyfus Affair, which was at its height as the fair was being prepared. The chapter traces how French diplomatic humiliations of the late 1890s, including Fashoda, and the Rennes retrial of September 1899 provoked a wave of foreign boycott threats against the fair, from Chicago and London to Calcutta and Melbourne. It then shows how President Loubet and the Waldeck-Rousseau ministry secured Dreyfus's pardon, and how a campaign of official silence, the truce of the exposition, was pressed on journalists and politicians alike, with Reinach agreeing to keep the peace and Clemenceau refusing. The evidence is mainly from newspapers, parliamentary debates and the published inquiry into appeasement. The chapter ends by suggesting that the fair served as a political tranquilliser, so that the amnesties of 1900 went through quietly and Dreyfus was declared innocent only in 1906.

“Lessons learned at the Greater Britain Exhibition in 1899” p. 79
About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Qureshi's book studies the display of living foreign peoples in nineteenth-century London, from the San exhibition at Exeter Hall in 1847 to Savage South Africa at Earl's Court in 1899. Its argument is that these shows were a large commercial entertainment, shaped by London's streets, printed promotion, managers and performers who consented on their own terms, and that they fed into ethnology and the scientific study of race rather than being only racist spectacle. Part One covers the streets, Part Two the promotion, the managers and the recruitment of performers, and Part Three the audiences' interpretations, the natural history court at the Crystal Palace and the learned societies, ending with the shift toward entertainment and imperial spectacle. The scope is London, with comparisons to Paris in 1889, Chicago in 1893 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present in this book as one group among many displayed peoples and as a frequent comparison, but not as a central subject. The text names them in about 74 of its 17,790 lines, mostly in Chapters Six and Seven and the notes. Chinese people appear in Chapter One as physiognomic types in Eliza Lynn's 'Passing Faces' and as settlers stranded in the London docks, and Japanese and Chinese shows are named in the introduction. Chapter Six gives the most sustained attention: Latham's natural history court at the Crystal Palace displayed Chinese, Tibetans, Malaysians and Javanese, and the guidebook's classification of the Chinese as the most civilized of the Mongolians is discussed. Chapter Seven adds Asian displays at other fairs, used as comparisons with London: the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition's 'Malay' and Straits Settlements performers and Chinese junks from Hong Kong, the Cochin-China settlement and Kampong-Javanese camp of Paris 1889, and the Chicago 1893 Midway with its Chinese and Javanese villages and Japanese bazaar. The book says little about the Asian performers themselves or their experiences in London, and South Asia, through the Empire of India exhibition of 1895, receives more attention as a contrast.

Geppert places the Greater Britain Exhibition at Earl's Court within the run of shows that Kiralfy and his London company staged there from the mid-1890s. About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.

Coté's footnote on colonial exhibitions lists the Greater Britain Exhibition, 1899, among exhibitions that focused on the colonial world. The note gives no place, dates or content beyond the year, so the article does not use the show in its argument.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
