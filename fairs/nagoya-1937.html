Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition

Nagoya Hantaiheiyō Heiwa Hakurankai
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition (Nagoya Hantaiheiyō Heiwa Hakurankai) ran from 15 March to 31 May 1937 in Nagoya and drew over 4.8 million visitors (Hopsonp. 139). It was meant to promote Nagoya as an industrial and cultural centre of the empire (Hopsonp. 137). The Imperial Japanese Army extracted concessions, among them a Hall of National Defense and Aviation and live-fire demonstrations (Hopsonp. 137; Hopsonp. 145). Nathan Hopson argues that even on the eve of all-out war, Nagoya's elites still favoured peace through trade rather than autarky (Hopsonp. 137; Hopsonp. 137).

Background and aims

The expo was first proposed in 1934 as a 30th anniversary celebration for the Port of Nagoya (Hopsonp. 139; Hopsonp. 139). By early 1935 its planners had widened it to the pan-Pacific region and added peace (Hopsonp. 142). Its declared purposes were to contribute to peace, culture and trade, to strengthen local and national industry and prestige, and to improve relations across the pan-Pacific region (Hopsonp. 140). Hopson calls the vision behind it a capitalist peace of stability and prosperity through free trade in a new Pacific Age (Hopsonp. 140).

Nagoya had its own stake. By 1934 it had a population above one million and had been known since the 1910s as Japan's Central Capital (Chūkyō), and its boosters were rebranding it as Great Nagoya in opposition to Tokyo (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Between 1907 and 1937 the city built a port, a canal, a municipal hospital, a new city hall, an airstrip, and a new rail station costing 25 million yen (Hopsonp. 141). Industrial output rose by nearly 40 percent from 1920 to 1925 (Hopsonp. 142). One expo official described the event as an opportunity to promote Aichi, a leader of industrial Japan, to the world (Hopsonp. 142).

In a February 1937 radio address, Mayor Ōiwa Isao (1867–1955, mayor 1927–38) said that the Japanese Empire reigned in the Pacific and had a duty to keep peace among nations (Hopsonp. 142). Hopson says the expo's goals were tied to "a sacred national mission" in a new Pacific Age (Hopsonp. 142). He argues that this was less a vision of autarky, with Japan heading a yen bloc, than a vision of capitalist peace based on the open trade that had made Nagoya a boomtown (Hopsonp. 142).

Organisers and the mayor

Mayor Ōiwa dealt personally with the army's pressure, and Hopson draws on the biography of him by Kameda Takao (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 144). In 1935 Ōiwa prayed at Ise, Japan's holiest shrine, for an expo that would dispel what he called international suspicions after the establishment of Manchukuo (Hopsonp. 144). Unless otherwise noted, the article's data come from the three-volume official record of the expo (Hopsonp. 140).

The mayor's main move against army pressure was to recruit Prince Higashikuni Naruhiko as chairman (Hopsonp. 145). The prince was a senior member of the imperial family and a high-ranking army officer, and with him as figurehead the expo was shielded from direct military criticism (Hopsonp. 146; Hopsonp. 146). Hopson notes that Higashikuni had been stationed in Nagoya in the early 1930s, and Ōiwa's biographer suggests that the mayor and the prince got on well (Hopsonp. 146; Hopsonp. 146).

Site and buildings

The expo stood on about 500,000 square metres of newly reclaimed land just north of the port (Hopsonp. 139; Hopsonp. 139). Hopson says the expo gave Nagoya the excuse to drain this area, which connected port and city, and to zone it for heavy industry (Hopsonp. 139). The Peace Tower was the centrepiece (Hopsonp. 140). After the close the site was torn down, and Hopson says the expo contributed more to the city through the empty space this left than Paris did through its surviving buildings (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147). Hopson also reports that an architect judged a peace tower at the 1937 Paris exposition absurd, and that the same would be true of Nagoya's tower (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147).

Exhibits and attractions

Exhibits on modern science and industry dominated the expo, rather than any clear programme for peace (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Over two dozen pavilions housed prefectural exhibits, colonial displays, foreign trading partners and local firms such as Toyota, Mitsubishi, Noritake and Morimoto (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Other halls covered education and sanitation, and attractions included a children's play area, an American circus, a German-made Transparent Man and Peruvian alpacas (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140).

The Hall of National Defense and Aviation was the first stop on a radio tour broadcast on 25 April 1937 (Hopsonp. 144). Visitors were urged to pray at a shrine for the war dead by the entrance. The first room held a real bomber and a reconnaissance plane, and the next a panorama of modern warfare with sound effects of air-raid sirens and cannon fire (Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144). Another panorama showed Nagoya under attack from enemy aircraft (Hopsonp. 144). A guidebook described the purpose of the hall as disseminating information about national security and the might of the Imperial Army (Hopsonp. 145).

A pavilion on liquid, solid and gas fuel production, which had obvious military significance, was probably a further concession to the army (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145). The education hall was part of the original plan, but it was dedicated to Japan in a time of crisis, with twenty themes that included the role of women as wise mothers. The records do not show when this theme was adopted (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145).

Visitors could also see taxi boats, dog, pickle and pesticide days, an America Day and an International Peace Day (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149). Hopson suggests that for many visitors the Hall of National Defense and Aviation, "naval torpedoes in the canal" and live-fire drills around the Peace Tower may have left a more lasting impression than peace (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149).

Who took part

Most Western government invitees declined because of international tensions, and the United States refused to take part (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Business associations from San Francisco, Los Angeles, Seattle and Hawaii came, as did the Dallas cotton exporters' association, drawn by trade links with Nagoya (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Many pavilions held exhibits from the Asian colonies of other imperial powers, such as Ceylon and the Dutch Indies (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Of the fourteen South and Central American representatives, all but Brazil were placed in one America Latina hall (Hopsonp. 140).

The expo drew 4.8 million visitors, mostly from Japan and its colonial possessions (Hopsonp. 139; Hopsonp. 139). Hopson suggests that a great number of visitors, perhaps the majority, enjoyed the attractions rather than the peace programme (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149).

Politics and controversy

Tokyo seems to have treated Nagoya as a useful, if idiosyncratic, satellite (Hopsonp. 144). Hopson says the mayor kept the scale limited out of deference to the 1940 Olympic Games and the 2600th anniversary of the empire (Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144). By calling the expo pan-Pacific rather than international, Nagoya avoided seeming to steal Tokyo's thunder (Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144).

The army's pressure transformed the expo more fundamentally (Hopsonp. 144). The army objected to a peace expo and extracted compromises by threatening closure (Hopsonp. 145). Hopson says the Hall of National Defense and Aviation was the most prominent of these, and the mayor intended it to deflect the antagonism (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145). The threat was real: the same antagonism could have closed the expo, as it had closed the planned 1940 Tokyo and Sapporo Olympics the following year (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145). Even after accepting the hall, the army delayed installations past the deadline and endangered the opening (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145). A footnote reports Kameda's view, on scant evidence, that the navy was more supportive of the expo than the army (Hopsonp. 137).

Nagoya and Paris

Hopson compares Nagoya with the Paris exposition of 1937. The two were remarkably similar in content, context and outcome: both were spectacles of self-promotion in industry, trade, science and empire, and both were integrated into their cities (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147). Both went through at least two planning stages, and both were overtaken by rising tensions (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147). In Nagoya the main trouble came from the army, and in Paris from Germany (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147). Hopson applies to Nagoya the phrase that James Herbert used for Paris, a combination of audacity and uncertainty (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148). Neither expo could defuse the international situation or change the course towards war (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148).

How Hopson reads the expo

Hopson cites Ha Bae-Song, one of the few scholarly studies of the expo, who described its conception of peace as weak (Hopsonp. 148). The article gives this author as Ha Bae-Song in the text but as Ha, Sae-Bong in the references (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 150). Hopson argues that the idea of capitalist peace was serious in its time. Theories of capitalist peace were influential in the interwar years, and Schumpeter argued that capitalism offered no fertile soil for imperialist impulses (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148). Hopson suggests that historians see the mid-1930s as the edge of a dark valley, which makes the plans look empty and cynical. He suggests reading the period instead as a contest between programmes and visions (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148). The expo's bylaws and promotional materials point to peace, friendship, progress and prosperity in the Pacific (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148). Hopson notes that the promoters set out no point-by-point programme for peace, which he says is not unusual for an industrial and commercial fair (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149). He concludes that the expo, like Japan itself, was "massive and diverse" (Hopsonp. 149).

Aftermath and legacy

The expo closed a few months before Japan went to war with China, and Higashikuni, who had been its chairman, then headed the Military Aviation Department and then the Second Army (Hopsonp. 146; Hopsonp. 146). Hopson, citing Rivoirard's view of Paris, suggests that the legacy of both exhibitions may lie less in their failure to prevent war than in their being part of the last stand of peace (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Colonial and puppet pavilions

Japan's own colonies and puppet regimes were represented, including Manchukuo and the short-lived East Hebei regime in the buffer zone between Manchukuo and Beijing (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 140). Hopson names Taiwanese, Korean and Manchukuo pavilions among the attractions that some visitors may have preferred, but he does not describe their contents (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149).

East Hebei and Jidong Day

Hopson suggests that some visitors were proudest of the evidence of Japan's imperial ambitions in China, such as the East Hebei Hall and Day (Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149). Citing Nishio Rintarō, he notes special celebrations of Jidong (East Hebei) Day on 25 April and Tianjin Day on 26 May. On Jidong Day the national flag flew on public transport in Nagoya, and the council chair Yin Ju-keng, educated at Waseda, addressed Japanese radio listeners (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 148). Hopson reports that the major local newspaper, the Nagoya Shinbun, did not cover Jidong Day, which he calls a virtual non-event (Hopsonp. 148).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Capitalist peace — Hopson reads the expo's peace as open trade and prosperity in a Pacific Age, set against the army's militarised idea of peace through force (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 142; Hopsonp. 148)
  • Military pressure and concessions — the army forced a defence hall and live-fire drills into the programme and delayed installations, and the mayor used a prince as figurehead to protect the expo (Hopsonp. 137; Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145)
  • City branding and rivalry with Tokyo — Nagoya's self-promotion implied a rivalry with Tokyo, and calling the expo pan-Pacific avoided direct confrontation (Hopsonp. 137; Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144)
  • Mega-event diversity — Hopson uses Roche's account of mega-events to argue that the expo held conflicting meanings, from the Hall to the attractions (Hopsonp. 138; Hopsonp. 138; Hopsonp. 149)
  • Colonial and imperial display — the expo included Manchukuo and East Hebei exhibits, and visitors were drawn to Taiwanese and Korean pavilions (Hopsonp. 140; Hopsonp. 149; Hopsonp. 149)
  • Comparison with Paris 1937 — Hopson treats the two expos as similar spectacles of self-promotion disrupted by tensions (Hopsonp. 147; Hopsonp. 147)

Questions for study

  1. How far did the army's demands change the content of the expo, and which additions can be traced to those demands? (Hopsonp. 145; Hopsonp. 145)
  2. Why did Nagoya call the expo pan-Pacific rather than international, and what did that wording allow the organisers to avoid? (Hopsonp. 144; Hopsonp. 144)
  3. Does the expo's official bylaw language about peace and friendship match what visitors remembered of it? (Hopsonp. 148; Hopsonp. 149)

Introductionp. 137Asia

Hopson introduces the 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition as a city-branding event. Although army concessions made it look like a cynical misuse of capitalist peace, Hopson argues that Nagoya's elites still clung to a 1920s vision of peace through trade rather than autarky. Drawing on mega-event theory and regional histories of interwar Japan, the introduction presents the expo as evidence of the diversity of Japanese politics before the war with China.

Local Promotion and Capitalist Peace in the Pacific Agep. 139Asia

Hopson shows how the Nagoya expo, planned in 1934 as a port anniversary and widened into a pan-Pacific peace event, was shaped by compromise between Nagoya's city-branding ambitions, its trade-based prosperity and the need to avoid friction with Tokyo. Its vision was a capitalist peace of free trade in a new 'Pacific Age', yet exhibits of modern science and industry dominated, with the Peace Tower as centrepiece, pavilions from Japan's colonies and puppet regimes, and local firms such as Toyota. Most Western governments stayed away, though business groups from San Francisco, Seattle and Dallas came. The article traces Nagoya's rise from castle town to industrial hub and Mayor Ōiwa's hope of rebranding the city as 'Great Nagoya'. It ends with the planners' anxieties about autarky and the distrust that followed Manchukuo.

Accommodation and Survivalp. 144Asia

Hopson shows how the expo survived pressure from Tokyo and the Imperial Japanese Army. Concern not to rival Tokyo or upstage the 1940 Olympics led the mayor to keep the scale modest and call the event pan-Pacific. The army's demands produced a Hall of National Defense and Aviation, with a panorama of modern warfare and a radio tour in April 1937. The mayor's defence was to recruit Prince Higashikuni as chairman, which shielded the expo from direct military criticism.

Nagoya and Parisp. 147Asia

Hopson compares the Nagoya expo with Paris 1937, arguing that both were spectacles of self-promotion in industry, trade and science, both were integrated into their cities, and both were shaped by rising tensions, the Japanese army in Nagoya and Germany in Paris. Both went through two planning stages and both added peace themes that could not stop the war. Herbert's phrase 'strange marriage of audacity and uncertainty' fits Nagoya as well as Paris.

Conclusionp. 148Asia

Hopson concludes that the expo's idea of peace, though weak, belonged to a serious interwar doctrine of capitalist peace, and that its promoters' bylaws and publicity promised amity and prosperity around the Pacific. In practice, Hopson suggests, visitors remembered the Hall of National Defense, live-fire drills and naval torpedoes more than peace, and the expo, like Japan itself, was massive and diverse.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 5

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
Introduction. Assimilation and Space: Toward an Ethnography of Japanese Rulep. 1Asia

Henry opens in 1925 with the Korea Shrine on Namsan, built by the colonial government in Keijo, and with an official's report of Koreans who looked around but did not worship there. This failure of assimilation frames the book, which treats the city's public spaces as a crucible for Japanese rule. Henry reads assimilation as a set of contested projects in spiritual, material and civic life, and argues against top-down accounts by looking at how shrines, expositions and neighborhoods were used by settlers, Korean elites and poor residents. The introduction sets out three periods, 1910–25, 1925–37 and 1937–45, and the five chapters that follow. It draws on Mary Louise Pratt's idea of contact zones and explains that the sources are limited by the colonial archive, so the book reads Japanese-language newspapers and Korean papers against the grain.

5Imperial Subjectification: The Collapsing Spaces of a Wartime Cityp. 168Asia

Henry examines how the late colonial state, after war with China began in 1937, tried to make Koreans in Keijo (Seoul) loyal subjects of the emperor, a process the chapter calls imperial subjectification. Its evidence comes from the Keijo nippo's coverage of the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition, the torch relay from Ise to Korea Shrine and a school tour of Japan's sacred sites, and from the Namsan shrines, the militarised autumn festival, Seoul Nation-Protecting Shrine and the campaign to install Ise amulets and miniature shrines in homes. Henry sets the official claim that rising shrine visits proved growing reverence against the complaints of Shinto priests about coercion and formality, and against photographs that show Koreans bowing in a Confucian manner or keeping their hats on. The chapter argues that loyalty was performed under observation and that the state could measure it only by counting. It ends by showing how the cult fell short in the home, where amulets were discarded and shrines misused, and by noting a Christian seminary professor who visited Namsan while keeping his own faith.

Kargon, Robert H., Karen Fiss, Morris Low and Arthur P. Molella. World’s Fairs on the Eve of War: Science, Technology, and Modernity, 1937–1942. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2015.
1Introduction: World’s Fairs, Modernity, and the Demand for Authenticityp. 1Asia

The introduction sets out a comparative study of five fairs planned in the 1930s, three built (Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939) and two planned but never opened (Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942). Its argument is that these fairs became arenas of ideological struggle over modernity, as liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define the future. It traces how world's fairs since the Crystal Palace of 1851 taught populations to accept industrial change as progress, and how after the First World War, with the older empires gone, nations sought a unifying authenticity to animate modern technology. It then sketches the international crises of 1931 to 1936, from Manchuria to the Rhineland, which made the fairs' messages more confrontational. The national messages are outlined in turn: imperial Rome, racial unity in Germany, Japan's pan-Asian mastery, and the democratic displays of France and the United States. The introduction ends by describing the fairs as propaganda contests that preceded a war of armies, and by noting that the war of ideas proved significant for its outcome.

In the contents of this work

2Modernity à la française: The 1937 Paris Expositionp. 7Asia

The chapter examines the 1937 Exposition internationale des arts et techniques dans la vie moderne in Paris and how it defined a French version of modernity. It argues that the fair, planned under the Third Republic, presented France as a third way between subsistence production and American standardised mass production, with human creativity and artisanship at its centre. The evidence comes from the fair's official magazine, the commissioners' reports and a planning history that began in 1928 and was reshaped after the 1934 Stavisky riots. Edmond Labbé and Paul Léon are the main planners, and the chapter works closely through Jean Perrin's Palais de la Découverte, with its Léger mural, and the modernist commissions the Blum government added. It then turns to the foreign pavilions, where the Soviet and German buildings drew the largest crowds and the Italian and Japanese pavilions blended modernism with tradition, while the American building was judged weak. It concludes that France's claim to be a rational middle way between ideologies looks, in retrospect, fragile.

In the contents of this work

4Whose Modernity?: Utopia and Commerce at the 1939 New York World’s Fairp. 57Asia

The chapter examines the 1939 New York World's Fair as the site of a debate over what progress and modernity should mean for the United States during the Depression. It argues that the official theme, Building the World of Tomorrow with the Tools of Today, was contested between New Deal reformers who wanted public education about planned cities and corporate sponsors who wanted to sell products and free enterprise. The author traces the fair's origins in the Fair of the Future committee, which included Lewis Mumford, and the shift towards corporate pavilions after the renewed economic stress of 1937. The main cases are three visions of the American city: Democracity in the Perisphere, General Motors' Futurama, and the film The City, scripted by Mumford. Foreign pavilions, including those of Britain, Japan, Italy and the Soviet Union, are read as propaganda that the approaching war increasingly overshadowed. The chapter concludes that the fair's uneasy mix of public planning and private enterprise was a compromise, and that by the 1964 fair the corporate path to modernity had become dominant.

whole article (journal issue 38:2, 2018, pp. 137-151); sections at l. 77 Introduction, l. 166 Local promotion and capitalist peace in the Pacific Age, l. 354 Accommodation and survival, l. 470 Nagoya and Paris, l. 537 Conclusion

The most detailed study of the 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition in the collection, covering its origins, pavilions, army interference and comparison with Paris 1937. The author argues that the expo should not be dismissed as a cynical use of the word peace: Nagoya's business and government elites still held a 1920s vision of capitalist peace through free trade, and the city's stance shows the diversity of Japanese politics in the late 1930s. Drawing on Roche's idea of mega-events, he stresses that such exhibitions are large enough to hold alternative and even conflicting meanings. Students should weigh this regional argument against the visible role of the army in the event.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. The article concerns a Japanese exhibition in Nagoya, so Japan is its subject throughout. The chapter 'Local Promotion and Capitalist Peace in the Pacific Age' carries most of this, covering the prefectural, colonial and firm pavilions such as Toyota, Mayor Ōiwa's 'Great Nagoya' campaign and the expo's place in a new Japan-led 'Pacific Age'.
