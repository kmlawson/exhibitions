Portland · 1905

Lewis and Clark Centennial Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Lewis and Clark Centennial and American Pacific Exposition and Oriental Fair ran in Portland, Oregon, from 1 June to 15 October 1905 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190). Its ostensible purpose was to memorialise the explorers, but its backers aimed it at trade across the Pacific (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The texts use it to show how commercial boosterism, colonial displays and Asian markets were presented to American visitors as one story of progress (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181). Its Filipino village and its labour conflict make it a case for both empire and class (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The commemorative purpose hid a commercial project. Dan McAllen first proposed a fair in 1895, drawing on San Francisco's Midwinter Fair and the hard times of the 1890s (Rydell 1984). Bankers, brokers and the Board of Trade supplied the impetus (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181). J. M. Long of the Board of Trade held meetings from 1900, and the Oregon Historical Society chose the date and the Lewis and Clark theme (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell, Findling and Pelle state the aim plainly: a fair could promote commercial expansion across the Pacific and "to assure visitors of the moral correctness of America’s imperial design" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The corporation filed its articles on 12 October 1901 under a long name that joined commemoration and boosterism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181).

Organisation, finance and labour

State and federal money paid for much of the fair. The Historical Society endorsed the idea on 15 December 1900, and the Oregon legislature followed two months later and pledged aid (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181). In 1903 it appropriated $450,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 189). Congress first considered more than $2 million for federal participation, but the sum fell to $475,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Harvey Scott's influence with Theodore Roosevelt was, according to the official account, decisive for the federal building (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182). Private backers subscribed the $300,000 stock issue within two days (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 189).

Labour was the most contested matter. Unions had subscribed to the stock on promises of union-only work. In April 1903 the management adopted an open shop, meaning it did not confine jobs to union members (Rydell 1984). A referendum drive led by Socialists and farmers split the unions, the Board of Trade backed the fair, and the drive failed to qualify the appropriation for a vote (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A strike in March 1905 forced the directors to make concessions, including an eight-hour day, and the management blamed outside agitators from St Louis (Rydell 1984).

Opposition spread beyond the unions. Farmers in Albany wanted the referendum applied to the appropriation bill, and the carpenters' business agent, O. N. Pierce, insisted on a vote (Rydell 1984). The Board of Trade, whose members included most of the trustees, declared that a resolution asking union members to burn down the public buildings "would be quite as justifiable" (Rydell 1984). The text does not explain the comparison. Property owners and service workers opposed the referendum, and the management's publicity campaign helped split the labour movement (Rydell 1984).

The directors answered with job advertisements in daily papers across the country. The Labor Press reported that not a brick was being laid on the grounds, though one advertisement had prompted a letter from a Canadian brick contractor about a hundred bricklayers (Rydell 1984).

The sums disagree. Rydell gives the 1903 state appropriation as $500,000 (Rydell 1984), against the $450,000 above. Badger's table of United States appropriations lists $5,000 for Portland in 1905, against the $475,000 federal figure above, and cites a Treasury letter of 29 December 1904 as its source (Badger 1979p. 132).

Site and architecture

The fair stood on Guild's Lake, a 400-acre tract on the northwest edge of the city that had been pasture and marsh (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Dredging and water pumped from the Willamette made a shallow lake at its centre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). John Olmsted of Olmsted and Sons received $5,000 for a 1903 site plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190).

Most buildings were in Spanish Renaissance style, with domes and red tile, on light wood frames covered with lath and plaster, and mostly by Portland architects (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190). The federal building was by James Knox Taylor (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190). The only truly distinctive building was the Forestry Building, a log cabin 105 by 209 feet with a portico of tree trunks (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

A special smoke filter was designed for the city crematorium, less than a thousand feet from the grounds. The Oregon Daily Journal wrote that where wilderness had been, a monument to American energy now stood (Rydell 1984). Visitors on Lakeview Terrace could see Mount St Helens on clear days (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190).

Exhibits and attractions

Much of the American display was regional: forestry, farm produce, irrigation models of the Salt River and Palouse projects, Alaskan totem poles and a fish hatchery (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The entrance motto, which the texts read as a claim that empire was moving west, appeared over the colonnade (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Rydell 1984).

The Trail, a centrally located midway (Rydell 1984) built on pilings over the lake (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182), held an Igorot village (Igorots were one of several peoples the texts group as Filipinos (Rydell 1984)), a Fair Japan, a Streets of Cairo, an Eskimo village, an Old Plantation and a vaudeville theatre with blackface minstrels (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The directors set aside 2 August as Workingmen's Day and cut admission to twenty-five cents (Rydell 1984). A note in the same text gives the date as 3 August (Rydell 1984).

Peoples on display

Peoples were shown in private villages and in federal exhibits. The Igorot village was a concession, first planned by the International Anthropological Exhibit Company and then run by Felder and Schneidewind, while the War Department sent only artifacts (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984). Goode, the fair's president, praised the village as an educative display (Rydell 1984). Federal photographs and exhibits presented overseas peoples as potential customers, with Filipinos as one group (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The texts say Negritos and Igorots were shown as peoples on the verge of extinction (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Abbott calls the Filipino exhibit on the Trail a "deeply racist exhibit" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184).

Two rival firms bid for the Igorot show. Truman K. Hunt offered Igorots alone, while Edmund Felder and Richard Schneidewind offered Visayans, Negritos and Igorots. Goode doubted that the Negritos and Visayans would be "moneymakers" like the Igorots (Rydell 1984). Hunt then signed with Elks' carnivals, and Felder and Schneidewind won the Portland contract with a promise of at least twenty-five Igorots (Rydell 1984). Clarence Edwards of the Bureau of Insular Affairs wrote "I really see nothing against it", but disliked taking the performers to Coney Island, where Americans might think most Filipinos were like Igorots and Negritos (Rydell 1984).

The later tours show the cost to the performers. In 1906 Felder and Schneidewind took the village to Los Angeles and Chicago. When a federal recruiting officer questioned the Igorots, they said they wanted to go home, and the partners raised wages they held in trust to prevent a mutiny (Rydell 1984). The following year Hunt was in a Memphis jail, charged with embezzling Igorot wages (Rydell 1984).

Visitors, reception and aftermath

The reference works give 1,588,000 paid admissions, 540,000 of them from Portland and 160,000 from east of the Rockies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 190). Rydell, Findling and Pelle give a total of 2.6 million visitors without saying whether it counts paid entries (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The crowd was mostly regional (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

National newspapers and magazines were generally complimentary, and Walter Hines Page wrote that "the enterprise has from the beginning been managed with modesty, good sense, and good taste" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). The fair made an operating profit and returned 21 percent of capital to the investors, against the state commission's wish that the profit go to Oregon (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). Rydell, Findling and Pelle put the profit at nearly $85,000 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Businessmen credited the fair with a real estate boom, which Abbott places within wider growth in railways, farming and stock raising (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). The fair also launched the Rose Festival (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The buildings were dismantled, and only the Forestry Building survived until a fire in the early 1960s (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). Guild's Lake silted up in the 1910s and became an industrial district, and the Forestry site is now upscale housing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191).

On Workingmen's Day, 2 August, 10,282 people passed the turnstiles before noon, the largest morning crowd to that date (Rydell 1984). The Oregon Daily Journal put the day's total at 27,420 (Rydell 1984). A woman recorded in her diary that she had enjoyed the Igorot village (Rydell 1984).

The people who ran the fair

Henry W. Corbett subscribed a tenth of the $300,000 stock, nominated the first directors and became board president. He died in 1903 (Rydell 1984). The official list also names Theodore Hardee as assistant to the president, John Wakefield for concessions and admissions, Ion Lewis for architecture and Henry Reed as secretary (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420). Wakefield ran the Trail, and a note says the idea of Workingmen's Day seems to have started with him (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

Publicity and the special agent

The directors hired a special agent to tour the West for the fair (Rydell 1993p. 24). Rydell quotes one historian on the job: the agent shared worn-down hotels with travelling salesmen and "glad-handed editors of the weekly papers" (Rydell 1993p. 24). The local post office was persuaded to postmark outgoing mail with the fair's name (Rydell 1993p. 24). Goode donated an electric sign showing the year, visible for over thirty miles (Rydell 1984).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell reads the Portland and Seattle fairs as imperial theme parks that tied trans-Pacific markets to visions of racial progress (Rydell 1984). He ends the Portland story with the industrial future of the site, which he calls "the consummation of empire" rather than the destruction of Eden (Rydell 1984). Abbott, in both encyclopedias, stresses boosterism and economic effect (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). Abbott sets the fair's technological optimism against its racism on the Trail (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184), and finds its physical legacy slight (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). Rydell, Findling and Pelle place the fair among the imperial fairs of the period, with federal displays aimed at overseas markets (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Maloney reads the fair through horticulture: its horticulture was subsumed by the Forestry Building and industry, and its Olmsted grounds were lost (Maloney 2012p. 117). Daykin's thesis, named in a contributor note, makes Japanese participation the question and compares it with Osaka in 1903 (Filipová 2015).

The three main accounts agree that the fair was commercial boosterism and that its Filipino displays were framed in racial terms (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They differ on emphasis. Labour is central in Rydell's chapter and in Rydell, Findling and Pelle, and the Abbott entries omit it (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Abbott stresses the local economy and the site's fate (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184). Visitor figures and profit also disagree, and the texts do not reconcile them (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191).

Allwood, Allan and Reid group the Portland fair with Dublin in 1907 and Seattle in 1909 as smaller international events of more local character (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). Their table gives it no attendance or profit figures (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Abbott sets technological progress against social progress. Moving pictures, motorised blimps, the first transcontinental auto race and electric lighting stood for the first (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184). He sets the Trail's Filipino exhibit against speeches at the National American Woman Suffrage Association convention (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts describe three Asian presences at Portland: a Japanese display in the Oriental Exhibits Building, a poorly filled Chinese space, and a Filipino village. None of the texts describes South Asian or Dutch East Indies participation.

Japan

Japan had the most extensive display in the Oriental Exhibits Building, the counterpart of the Foreign Exhibits Building for Asian displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 191). Henry Dosch had gone to Osaka in 1902 with a display organised by Portland businessmen, and he urged Asian exhibitors to show working exhibits of natives in their own dress (Rydell 1984). After St Louis, Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged by their treatment there (Rydell 1984). Private exhibitors moved their St Louis displays to Portland's Oriental Exhibits Building, which was painted red, white and blue and draped with bunting (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Oregon Daily Journal read the building as America looking to the Orient and extending its civilisation through education (Rydell 1984). A Japanese vice-consul, Tsuneji Aiba, wrote on Japan's relations to the fair (Rydell 1984). Daykin's thesis on Japanese participation is named in a contributor note (Filipová 2015).

China

The Chinese government did not send a display, so the space went to a few merchants selling ornaments and trinkets. Merchants in Shantung province organised their own display and sent it (Rydell 1984). A Chinese boycott of American goods began in July 1905 (Rydell 1984). D. R. Francis, head of the St Louis fair, conceded that the Chinese there had suffered many affronts (Rydell 1984). Benjamin Ide Wheeler told the Lewis and Clark Educational Congress that an industrially awakened China would be an industrial cataclysm for the world, and that Japan was the model China should follow (Rydell 1984).

The Philippines

The Philippines was the most prominent Asian presence, and it was a private concession. The War Department sent only artifacts (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and a Philippine collection from St Louis reached Portland despite objections from William P. Wilson (Rydell 1984). The International Anthropological Exhibit Company agreed in November 1904 to show Igorot, Visayan and Negrito villages on the Trail (Rydell 1984). The company split in early 1905, and Felder and Schneidewind's Igorot village opened six weeks before the fair closed (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984). Taft allowed it to proceed after Goode cabled him (Rydell 1984). The village was popular, and Goode praised it (Rydell 1984). Abbott calls the Trail's Filipino exhibit "deeply racist" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184). Rydell argues that the two Pacific fairs made the development of an American commercial empire in Asia seem inevitable (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commercial boosterism — the fair was built to attract investors, immigrants and trade, with the commemoration as a cover (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 181; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Open-shop labour conflict — unions were promised union work, the management went open shop, and the referendum drive failed (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Filipino villages — private concessions, popular with fairgoers, and read as racially framed (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Federal colonial displays — federal photographs and exhibits presented overseas peoples as potential customers, and some groups as near extinction (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984).
  • Asian markets — Japan's display, the Chinese space and Asian exhibitors' treatment (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184).
  • Site and landscape — Guild's Lake, the Olmsted grounds and their later loss (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Maloney 2012p. 117).
  • Economic legacy — the real estate boom, the Rose Festival and the industrial district that replaced the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Rydell 1984).

Questions for study

  1. How did the open-shop dispute shape the way the fair presented its Filipino and Asian displays to workers, and did it matter to the fair's audience? (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984)
  2. Rydell reads the fair as imperial, while Abbott reads it as boosterism with a racialised exhibit on the Trail. Which reading fits the evidence on the federal buildings, and what would test it? (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184)
  3. Who gained most from the Igorot village: the concessionaires, the fair, or the federal government? (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984)
  4. Why do the sources give different visitor and profit figures, and what would a historian need to reconcile paid admissions with total visits? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 182; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  5. What survives of a fair's site, and what does the loss of Guild's Lake suggest about the relation between expositions and city planning? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 184; Maloney 2012p. 117)
  6. Why did the Board of Trade, the Socialists and the property owners read the referendum so differently, and which union records would show how far the members themselves agreed? (Rydell 1984)
  7. The 1903 appropriation is $450,000 in some texts and $500,000 in Rydell's chapter. Which legislative or treasury records would settle the sum, and would the difference change the account of the labour dispute? (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  8. The concessionaires held the Igorots' wages in trust, and Hunt was charged with embezzling them. Who was answerable for the performers' pay, and why did the government take no action on the conditions reported for the Jamestown village? (Rydell 1984)

Rydell argues that the Lewis and Clark Centennial in Portland (1905) and the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition in Seattle (1909) were built to link the Pacific Northwest to Asian markets and an American commercial empire, and that organized labor contested both. The chapter traces the closed-shop dispute, the referendum drive and the open-shop hiring that led to the Labor Day protest at Woodland Park. Its evidence is the exhibits and villages: the Igorot village on Portland's Trail and Seattle's Pay Streak, the Smithsonian and War Department displays of the Pacific possessions, the Oriental Exhibits Building, and the Japanese delegation's visit to Seattle under the slogan hands across the sea. Rydell also follows the concessionaires Felder and Schneidewind and their Igorot troupe through amusement parks, where a complaint that the performers wanted to go home was answered with higher wages. The chapter ends with the fairs' legacies in Portland's Guild Lake industrial district and the University of Washington campus.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
2Fairs of the Imperial Erap. 45Asia

Rydell, Findling and Pelle examine how American fairs from 1898 to 1915 presented the new overseas empire as natural and the racial hierarchy that justified it. They begin with the Trans-Mississippi Exposition in Omaha, where an Indian Congress, staged battles and an Old Plantation Village taught visitors about conquest and slavery, and move to Buffalo in 1901, where a color scheme ranked races from dark to light and a Filipino village cast American rule as uplift. St. Louis in 1904 receives the most space, for its anthropology displays and the Olympic events held alongside the fair. The authors then cover the Portland and Seattle fairs, which relied on Igorot and Eskimo villages, and the 1915 fairs in San Francisco, with its eugenics congresses, and San Diego, which reinvented the city's Spanish past and displayed human evolution. The evidence comes from fair records, newspapers and visitors' recollections. The chapter argues that these fairs were designed to show the desirability of colonialism and to justify racial hierarchy, and it closes by noting that the war which followed would reshape the next generation of fairs.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Wikipedia

The Lewis and Clark Centennial Exposition, commonly also known as the Lewis and Clark Exposition, and officially known as the Lewis and Clark Centennial and American Pacific Exposition and Oriental Fair, was a worldwide exposition held in Portland, Oregon, United States in 1905 to celebrate the centennial of the Lewis and Clark Expedition. While not officially considered a World's Fair by the Bureau of International Expositions, it is often informally described as such; the exposition attracted both exhibits and visitors from around the world. During the exposition's four-month run, it attracted over 1.6 million visitors, and featured exhibits from 21 countries. Portland grew from 161,000 to 270,000 residents between 1905 and 1910, a spurt that has been attributed to the exposition.
