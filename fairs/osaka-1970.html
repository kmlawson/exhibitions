Expo '70, the Japan World Exposition, ran from 15 March to 13 September 1970 on an 815-acre site in the Senri Hills of Osaka Prefecture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340). It was the first world's fair held in Asia, and it drew 64,218,770 visitors, the largest attendance of any world's fair up to that time (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). Historians use it to study Japan's arrival as an economic power, the superpower rivalry staged in its pavilions, and nuclear power presented as progress (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 53; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50).

Origins and aims

Japan wanted a world's fair long before 1970. It first showed a pavilion at the Paris exposition of 1867, and an early plan for 1877 "received little encouragement" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). A fair planned for 1912 was cancelled after the death of Emperor Meiji, and the 1940 Tokyo exposition was abandoned when war broke out; its tickets were honoured at Expo '70 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 326).

The 1970 proposal came from Osaka. In April 1964 the governor of Osaka Prefecture, the city's mayor and the chamber of commerce president petitioned the prime minister, and the cabinet agreed in June to study the request (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). The Bureau of International Expositions (BIE), the Paris body that chose hosts and registered fairs, gave two dates for its decision: 12 May 1965 in one text (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346) and September 1965 in another (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). The fair was registered on 11 May 1966 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339).

Japanese planners tied the fair to the 2600th anniversary of Japan, to Osaka's planning and to the centenary of Japan's modernisation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). Its theme, Progress and Harmony for Mankind, was meant to stress what people share rather than the difference between East and West (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). The 1964 Tokyo Olympics strengthened confidence that Japan could stage a large international event (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346).

Organisation, finance and participation

The Japanese government organised and financed the fair with Osaka Prefecture and the city of Osaka. The Japan Association for the 1970 World Exposition prepared and ran it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). Kenzo Tange, probably Japan's best-known architect at the time, planned the whole site but designed no individual building (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340).

Japan departed from normal BIE practice. Usually the host's commissioner general, a senior diplomat, has overall charge of the fair, but here day-to-day administration went to the Japan Association, "for whatever bureaucratic reason" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). The host commissioner general, Toru Haguiwara, was left as an intermediary and twice threatened to resign (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146).

The texts give three different costs. One gives $2 billion for transport and site development (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340). Another gives 87,800 million yen for construction and operation, about $1.25 billion in 2006 dollars, excluding city infrastructure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). A third gives $2.9 billion (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50). The profit was 19,439,402,017 yen, or $146,160,918 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345).

Participation was large but counted differently. Japan invited 126 countries, of which 77 accepted, and 21 international bodies (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340). One text counts 76 countries and a territory, Hong Kong (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345); a table gives 78 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192). The texts count 90 official foreign pavilions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141). Japanese firms built many more: 24 private pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341), or 28 private companies among 32 domestic pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345).

Site, architecture and the Tower of the Sun

Tange planned the site as a tree: a central Symbol Zone, the main north-south axis that split the site in two, was the trunk, roads and moving walkways the branches, and the pavilions the blossoms (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346). The Festival Plaza roof covered 340,000 square feet on six pillars and weighed 6,000 tons (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341; Allwood et al. 2001p. 142). The Tower of the Sun, designed by Taro Okamoto, rose through the plaza (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341). The texts disagree on its height: 230 feet (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346) or 400 feet (Allwood et al. 2001p. 142). One essay credits the Festival Plaza to Arata Isozaki and Atsushi Ueda rather than to Tange (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 315).

Critics found the architecture cautious. Allwood and colleagues read the restrained palette as a concern not to upset anyone, possibly reflecting Tange's design philosophy of neutrality (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141). They add that the Festival Plaza tended to cut the saucer-shaped site in two and lacked human scale (Allwood et al. 2001p. 143). After the fair most pavilions were demolished and the site became a national cultural park (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 344). The Tower survives (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 349).

Exhibits and the Cold War

Japan's own pavilion had five cylindrical halls shaped like cherry-blossom petals (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341). Its exhibits moved from the past in Hall 1, through the present in Halls 2 and 3, to the future in Halls 4 and 5 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Hall 4 held two atomic towers, one showing the destruction of Hiroshima and one celebrating peaceful nuclear power (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Low argues that Expo '70 carried the Atoms for Peace idea of civilian nuclear power into everyday life, turning nuclear power into a friendly energy source, with Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy as an emblem (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 52). Electricity from the Mihama reactor lit the site (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50).

The United States and the Soviet Union competed for attention. The US pavilion was an air-supported fibreglass dome, and its moon rock became the must-see item of the whole fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 342; Allwood et al. 2001p. 145). The Soviet pavilion, at 357 feet, was the tallest building on site; Cuba and the Soviet Union mounted the most overtly political displays (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341). Low's survey found that Japanese visitors were most interested in the moon rock and the Apollo 8 spacecraft (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 53), and that 22 percent of those who had seen both pavilions read the Soviet message as Russian ideology (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 53).

The site also offered art and performance. The Expo Museum of Fine Arts, under the title Discover the Harmony, set Japanese artists beside Western ones (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). Across the site, 440 companies gave 2,880 performances (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146).

Visitors, crowds and reception

Attendance was 64,218,770 over 183 days. Of these, 62 million were Japanese and 1.7 million were foreign (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). Allwood and colleagues put the Japanese share at 98 percent (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141). Peterson gives the total as 66.2 million (Peterson 2020p. 289), against 64.2 million elsewhere in the same book (Peterson 2020p. 144).

Crowds were the chief problem. Peak days averaged 641,000 visitors and reached 835,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). The record day, later known as Black Saturday, left hundreds stranded overnight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). Private cars jammed the main gate, and foreign participants threatened to close their pavilions when the organisers let in a million people on a Saturday (Allwood et al. 2001p. 143; Allwood et al. 2001p. 143).

The opening was also disrupted. Eighty fairgoers were trapped for hours on a ferris wheel, and leftist demonstrators protested against the fair and the US-Japan security treaty (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345).

Allwood says that at half a dozen favoured pavilions, admission was at timed intervals, either because the exhibit sequence required it or because the crowd pressure was too great. These were Japan, the USSR, Britain, the United States, France and Canada (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). Visitors came with a list of pavilions to see and wanted the pavilions' passport stamps, and Japanese media surveys had guided them to the favourites (Allwood et al. 2001p. 145). The Scandinavian pavilion's message was read by holding a card printed with the five flags, against projections from above (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 344). Allwood's point is that most visitors had already decided where to go, so the message was lost on them (Allwood et al. 2001p. 145). The Canadian pavilion, with 34,000 mirrors, kept its queues in a courtyard with a pool, folk singers, dancers and Shinto wedding ceremonies (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). Anderson also records the "Buffalo Run", the daily rush through the gates at opening (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348).

Expoland and the rides

Expo '70 kept its amusements in Expoland, a 42.5-acre section with a giant ferris wheel, a fun house, a minirail and a water park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). Its most popular ride was the Daidarasaurus, a roller coaster about three-quarters of a mile long that reached 40 mph (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). Benedict gives the rider count: nearly three million people rode it, against a little over nine million visitors to the Theme Pavilion and about 1.75 million to the Expo Museum (Benedict 1983p. 54). The fair's ferris wheel was forty metres high, with twenty gondolas of five people each, much smaller than Mr Ferris's original (Benedict 1983p. 55). Benedict argues that amusement-zone methods had moved into the main fair, so that at Osaka and at Expo '67 performers and moving displays were spread across the grounds (Benedict 1983p. 59). The Expo Tower in Expoland was 417 feet high, with nine observation cabins, and was demolished in 2002 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). It is a separate structure from the Tower of the Sun, so it does not settle the height dispute above.

Pavilions built to impress

Some pavilions had more presence than contents. Allwood says many participants built impressive pavilions that had "little or nothing inside" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 144). France and Germany repeated the geodesic structures of Expo 67, while the American pavilion was, in Allwood's words, a hole in the ground under a flat tent held up by air pressure (Allwood et al. 2001p. 144; Allwood et al. 2001p. 145). The Fuji Group pavilion used sixteen inflated vinylon tubes, each 13 feet across and 263 feet long, and housed a light, sound and movement show (Allwood et al. 2001p. 145). The Swiss Tree of Light carried 32,000 light bulbs on aluminium branches, and its designer, Willi Walter, won an American Institute of Architects award (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 342). Australia's circular roof hung from a tower modelled on Hokusai's The Wave. Manning gives the roof as 260 tons (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 342), but Anderson gives it as 240 tons, 158 feet across (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346). Technology was on show too. Anderson lists "dream telephones" for calling anywhere in Japan, and cashless shopping at the Furukawa Pavilion, which used customers' voice prints (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 347). Massidda counts maglev trains, mobile phone technology and the first IMAX film among Osaka's innovations (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 186). Peterson's source says the IMAX format was first demonstrated in the Fuji Pavilion (Peterson 2020p. 154).

Art and the time capsule

Anderson names the European artists in the Expo Museum of Fine Arts, among them Dalí, Picasso, Gauguin, Renoir, Rubens, Van Gogh and Cézanne, and gives Sesshu and Kano as the Japanese highlights (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). The Matsushita Pavilion held the "Expo ’70 Time Capsule", with 2,008 objects that included Hiroshima mementos and a list of endangered species, to be opened in the year 6970 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348).

Money and the balance sheet

The Japanese government spent $17 million (6.1 billion yen) on its own pavilion, the largest of the government pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341). Anderson says the fair left a credit balance of about $100 million in Japan's international balance of payments, mainly by boosting the trade surplus during its run (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348). Badger's appendix gives Osaka's cost as $220,000,000 (Badger 1979p. 131). That is well below the essay's figures, and the table's "Cost" column does not say what it includes. Roche's table of expo results also lists Osaka as a profit, with 64.2 million visitors (Roche 2000p. 43).

How historians have interpreted it

The texts agree on the scale of the fair but differ on what it meant. Anderson, in the encyclopedia entry, presents the fair as a sign of Japan's new prosperity and a likely cause of it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346). Manning, in the dictionary, reads Prime Minister Sato's claim of national strength as Japan's growing economic power (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345). Low argues that the fair helped sell civilian nuclear power to the Japanese public and led the government to choose the United States as its partner (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 54). Spero argues that Singapore's pavilion stressed multiracial harmony and non-alignment, and that Japan may have used the fair to assert its strength in the region against China (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 164; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 163). Peterson argues that the fair brought the West to Asia and proclaimed an Asian modernity (Peterson 2020p. 144), and that Asia has since taken over from the West as the great host exhibitor (Peterson 2020p. 280). Massidda argues instead that Osaka was the last universal exposition for twenty-two years, and ties that break to postmodern culture (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183). Benedict contrasts Osaka's confidence with the doubts about consumer society expressed at Montreal in 1967, and says Osaka showed none (Benedict 1983p. 36). Hollengreen and colleagues place it among mega-expositions that served industrial growth (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Rydell and colleagues say it held out hope for progress despite nuclear and ecological fears (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Massidda draws on David Anderson and Hiroyuki Shimizu's study of visitor memories to argue that Osaka was a rite of passage in which Japanese society was reborn (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185). Benedict, reading the fair as a trade fair, notes that Osaka replaced the congresses of earlier fairs with "commemorative lectures", set pieces rather than discussion groups (Benedict 1983p. 60). Geppert counts Osaka as the best-attended exhibition ever, and one of the non-Western expositions that succeeded in the preceding forty years (Geppert 2010p. 8).

Asian participants and visitors

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan was the host, and the only Asian state whose organisation the texts describe in detail. The texts repeat that Osaka was the first world's fair held in Asia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183). The Japanese government, with Osaka Prefecture and the city, organised and financed the fair, and the Japan Association ran it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). Japanese companies built 24 private pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341), and about 30 domestic pavilions whose budgets exceeded most foreign ones (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141). The government pavilion told the story of Japan in three periods, past, present and future (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Japanese gardens covered 64 acres and recreated four periods of Japanese garden history (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346). Of the visitors, 62 million were Japanese (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339).

Japanese organisers set their fair against Japan's new standing. Sato told the opening that Japan had acquired sufficient national strength to sponsor a world exposition, and the dictionary entry reads that strength as Japan's growing economic power, which made it the first Asian nation rich enough to sponsor such an exposition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345). Peterson describes Japan's national displays as presenting tradition within modernity (Peterson 2020p. 289). Low argues that the Japanese government chose the United States as its partner in civilian nuclear power (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 54).

Singapore and Malaysia

Singapore first declined the invitation. Goh Keng Swee, then minister of finance, recalled that the Japanese would not take a refusal and urged a rethink (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 162). The Tourist Promotion Board hoped to reach an estimated 30 million visitors, 29 million of them expected to be Japanese (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 163). Singapore's pavilion was a tropical garden of about 3,000 square metres beside the West Gate, with two huts built in the Malay manner, an aquarium and an aviary (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 165). A Shell-donated film of about fourteen and a half minutes opened with a legend of the city's founding in 1392 and left out the colonial period and the Japanese occupation (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 165; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 165). Its message was non-alignment and regional co-operation through ASEAN (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 164). Spero argues that the emphasis stayed on Southeast Asia and Japan rather than the wider world (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 164). Expo stamps, printed in Japanese and English for the fair, were partly meant to recover costs (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 169). Malaysia's pavilion, built in traditional Malay style, showed a multiracial society (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 168).

Other participants

Six communal pavilions called International Place housed smaller nations. Its Asian section held Afghanistan, Cambodia, Laos, Nepal, the United Arab Republic and Vietnam (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 344). Afghanistan joined after its king inspected the site during a 1969 state visit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340). The Vietnamese pavilion avoided politics and showed the country's history and culture, without mention of war (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341). The texts name the Burmese dragon-king boat and Hong Kong's junk sails among pavilions marked by nationalist display (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 342). Hong Kong was one of the territories that took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). The organisers helped developing countries gain a significant standing at the fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). North Korea, North Vietnam and the People's Republic of China were not invited, because they had no diplomatic relations with Japan (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340). South Korea followed Canada in the opening parade (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345).

Asian visitors and reception

Of the 1.7 million foreign visitors, 7 percent were Indian, 5 percent Chinese, 4 percent Filipino, 3 percent Korean, 2 percent Thai and 2 percent from Hong Kong (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). The texts say little about how these visitors responded. Low's survey covers the reactions of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 53). Peterson places Osaka in a longer story in which the West was brought to Asia and Asia later became the main host of expositions (Peterson 2020p. 144; Peterson 2020p. 280).

Themes in the scholarship

  • National standing and prosperity — The texts read the fair as a statement of Japan's position, from the opening speech to the 1910 London exhibition, which Hotta-Lister treats as a precedent (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 186; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346).
  • Nuclear power as progress — Low reads the nuclear towers, Tezuka's robot and the reactors that powered the site as one effort to make nuclear power seem friendly (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 52; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50).
  • Cold War display — The US and Soviet pavilions competed on size, space exhibits and politics, and Low's survey shows how visitors read them (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 341; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 53; Allwood et al. 2001p. 144).
  • Architecture and site — Tange's plan, the Festival Plaza and the restrained palette are described in detail, though the texts disagree on who designed what and how high the Tower stood (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 315; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346; Allwood et al. 2001p. 142).
  • Crowds and logistics — Peak-day figures, the crush of Black Saturday and the closures show the limits of the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348; Allwood et al. 2001p. 143).
  • Participation and absence — Counts of countries and pavilions, and the states that did not attend, show politics shaping the guest list (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340; Allwood et al. 2001p. 141; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192).
  • Commerce and publicity — Profit, trade gains and the publicity strategy of participants show the fair as a commercial venture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 345; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348; Allwood et al. 2001p. 200).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the Japanese organisers place day-to-day operations under a Japan Association rather than the host commissioner general, and how far did that split of authority shape the disputes the texts describe? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146)
  2. The texts give attendance as 64.2 million and 66.2 million, and costs from $1.25 billion to $2.9 billion. What explains the differences, and which figures should students rely on? (Peterson 2020p. 144; Peterson 2020p. 289; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50)
  3. Did the nuclear messages of the Japanese pavilions persuade visitors, or did they reflect a policy the government had already chosen? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 52; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 54)
  4. Singapore's film left out its colonial past and the occupation. What do national pavilions leave out, and how can students recover what is missing? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 166)
  5. Was Osaka the start of Asia's place as host of expositions, as Peterson argues, or the end of the universal form, as Massidda suggests? (Peterson 2020p. 280; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183)
  6. Which states stayed away from Osaka, and how did the rules on recognition shape the guest list? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 340)
  7. Badger gives Osaka's cost as $220 million, while the essay's figures run from $1.25 billion to $2.9 billion. Which costs did each text count, and what did each leave out? (Badger 1979p. 131; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 348; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50)
  8. Benedict says Osaka replaced congresses with set-piece lectures. What evidence would show how visitors used the fair's programme beyond the spectacle of the pavilions? (Benedict 1983p. 60; Allwood et al. 2001p. 146)
  9. Manning and Anderson give different roof weights for the Australian pavilion. What should a student check to decide between two reference entries? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 342; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 346)

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
12Madison Avenue, the Computer and the Rising Sunp. 132Asia

Allwood traces how exhibitions of the late 1960s and early 1970s moved from national statement towards commercial showmanship. The main case is New York 1964-65, where Robert Moses's fair, without major foreign exhibitors, filled its grounds with company pavilions, among them IBM's building by Eero Saarinen, Ford's Disney-designed display and the Johnson Wax film To Be Alive, and was hit by protests, poor site planning and a financial loss. Montreal's Expo 67 is presented as a high point, with Moshe Safdie's Habitat 67, Buckminster Fuller's geodesic American pavilion and the Labyrinth film. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event of record attendance, organised around Kenzo Tange's Festival Plaza and the Tower of the Sun, and remembered for the rush to see the American pavilion's moon rock. The chapter ends with a run of picture captions on later fairs and legacies, including Expo 98, Hanover 2000 and the London Dome, which carry its argument forward without comment.

Molella, Arthur P., and Scott Gabriel Knowles, eds. World’s Fairs in the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of Progress. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2019.
3Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka: World’s Fairs and the Shaping of Japanese Attitudes to Nuclear PowerMorris Lowp. 46Asia

Low asks how world's fairs helped sell the peaceful atom to Japanese audiences during the Cold War. He begins with the Atoms for Peace campaign and a 1956 US Information Service survey in which many Japanese expected atomic energy to become a curse, and then covers the Japan Pavilion at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors before the exhibits of instruments and crafts. The main case is Expo '70 in Osaka: Low describes Kono's tapestries in the atomic towers, the moon rock, and the Fujipan Robot Pavilion, where Tezuka's Astro Boy helped to counter Japan's 'nuclear allergy'. He also draws on exit surveys of the American and Soviet pavilions, which show Japanese visitors preferring the American display and reading the Soviet one as propaganda. The conclusion is that the fairs helped build a nuclear partnership with the United States, since the Japanese government chose American reactors and most Japanese supported building new plants.

13A Garden City for Progress and Harmony: Singapore at the Osaka 1970 ExpoEllan F. Sperop. 161Asia

Spero examines Singapore's pavilion at Expo '70 in Osaka, a tropical garden with a film and a series of postage stamps, shown by a country that had been independent for only five years. The argument is that the pavilion presented Singapore as a multicultural, non-aligned and prosperous state, and that this was a deliberate choice which played down colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. The chapter draws on the planning papers of the Ministry of Culture and the Tourist Promotion Board, the Shell-produced film Singapore Island City, and the stamps' design history. It recounts the government's initial reluctance to take part, the Japanese invitation, and the tourism and political aims considered for the pavilion. Spero describes the aviary, aquarium and two stand-alone huts, the film's origin myth and its treatment of trade, housing and diversity, and the four stamp designs. The chapter ends by suggesting that the garden is best understood through what it hides, the labour and planning behind its apparent naturalness.

15The Cold War, a Cool Medium, and the Postmodern Death of World ExposLuca Massiddap. 183

Massidda asks why world expositions disappeared after Osaka 1970, with no BIE-recognised universal exposition until Seville in 1992. The argument is that the expo's cultural form was structurally incompatible with late capitalism, the crisis of the city and the dominance of television, which made the Olympics a more effective 'media event'. The chapter has two parts. The first examines Brussels 1958, Montreal 1967 and Osaka 1970 at their peak, with attendances of 41 to 64 million, Cold War superpower pavilions, and new technology such as the Bell Pavilion's 360-degree screen. The second, covering 1970 to 1992, draws on McLuhan and Meyrowitz to argue that television dissolved the physical public space on which expos depended, and it presents a table of smaller specialised fairs in the period. Massidda concludes that the digital and networked media of the 1990s returned the city to the centre of the social system and gave the expo a new opportunity, as Seville showed.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 3

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
10World’s Fair Competitionp. 116

Allwood compares the New York World's Fair of 1939-40 with the Golden Gate International Exposition on Treasure Island in San Francisco, which opened in the same year. The New York fair, with its theme of 'The World of Tomorrow', the Trylon and Perisphere, its planned sectors and the reclaimed Corona Dumps site, is described first, along with the Aquacade, Railroads on Parade and the Swedish and Finnish pavilions. The chapter argues that the ambition for planned sectors and a unified architectural style was undone by exhibitors who ignored the plan, by loud flags and murals, and by an organizing body with little authority. The San Francisco fair is then set against it: an island built for the Bay Bridge and an airport, a site walled against the wind, a colour scheme that combined Guerin's 1915 palette with Urban's 1933 colours, and the Tower of the Sun. The chapter ends as war closes the fair and Treasure Island becomes a naval station.

EAppendix E: Fairs That Never Werep. 428

Wikipedia

The Japan World Exposition, Osaka, 1970 (日本万国博覧会, Nihon Bankoku Hakuran-kai) or Expo '70 was a world's fair held in Suita, Osaka Prefecture, Japan, between 15 March and 13 September 1970. Its theme was "Progress and Harmony for Mankind." In Japanese, Expo '70 is often referred to as Ōsaka Banpaku (大阪万博). It was the first world's fair held in Japan as well as in Asia. The Expo was designed by Japanese architect Kenzō Tange, assisted by 12 other Japanese architects. Bridging the site along a north–south axis was the Symbol Zone. Planned on three levels, it was primarily a social space with a unifying space frame roof. The Expo attracted international attention for the extent to which unusual artworks and designs by Japanese avant-garde artists were incorporated into the overall plan and individual national and corporate pavilions.
