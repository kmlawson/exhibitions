International Exhibition of 1862

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Exhibition of 1862 was held at South Kensington, London, from 1 May to November that year, eleven years after the Great Exhibition of 1851 and under the same Royal Commissioners, the body that had run the 1851 show (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). It drew 6,211,103 visitors, only slightly more than in 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001), and it was measured against its predecessor almost without exception (Findling & Pelle 1990). Historians use it to show how international exhibitions became arenas for national rivalry, imperial display and class conflict (Hoffenberg 2001; Kaiser; Della Coletta 2006). The texts also tie it to the shift of leadership in the genre away from Britain (Roche 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Henry Cole, who had been central to 1851, was the key figure behind the second London international exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh describes a show that grew out of paperwork rather than parliamentary argument, with more than 250 official documents and 84,000 items of correspondence (Greenhalgh 1988). In March 1861 the commissioners and the Royal Society announced a show at South Kensington from 1 May 1862, with possibly further shows at ten-year intervals (Greenhalgh 1988). The fair opened in the shadow of a death. Prince Albert had died of typhoid in December 1861 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). The temporary throne stood empty between busts of Albert and Victoria, and every speech referred to him (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Organisation and finance

The commissioners raised guarantees of £450,000 by advance subscription (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The commissioners spent more than that sum on the fair, and Prasch's dictionary entry says they barely broke even (Findling & Pelle 1990). The commission was financially secure within weeks, with 9,800 applications for space, about 8,000 from industrial sectors (Greenhalgh 1988). The ten-point plan that served as the official charter set rules that included no rents and a bar on machinery made before 1850 (Greenhalgh 1988). Francis Philip Cunliffe-Owen directed the foreign sections, and Cole acted as general adviser (Geppert 2010; Hoffenberg 2001).

The texts disagree about the outcome. Prasch's 2008 encyclopedia entry reports that the ledgers showed a minimal profit when the fair closed. Adrian Duncan estimated it at £790, and the profit appeared only after Kelk and Lucas gave up some claims to clear the deficit (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid give a loss of £11,783 in an appendix table (Allwood et al. 2001). Their account adds that Sir John Kelk, a partner in the main contractors Lucas and Kelk, took over building materials and personally gave £11,000 to balance the books (Allwood et al. 2001). Badger's appendix gives receipts of £459,631 against costs of £460,000 (Badger 1979), which leaves a gap of £369 on those figures. Benedict, Roche and Mandell all record a loss (Benedict 1983; Roche 2000; Mandell 1967).

Site and architecture

Captain Francis Fowke of the Royal Engineers designed the building (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001). Its facade ran for 1,250 feet, and its two glass domes were each greater in diameter than St Paul's Cathedral (Findling & Pelle 2008). Critics were unkind. The Quarterly Review condemned it as a failure (Findling & Pelle 2008), and the Art Journal dismissed it as a shed (Allwood et al. 2001). Planned mosaic pictures by leading British artists were never made (Allwood et al. 2001). Cole argued in a pamphlet that the building was designed for all future exhibitions, but the government refused to buy it, and it was demolished after the close (Findling & Pelle 2008). Geppert records that many regarded the building as a national disgrace (Geppert 2010).

The site later gained the South Kensington Museum, the Natural History Museum and the Royal Albert Hall, and later fairs were pushed to the periphery (Geppert 2010). Roche calls the resulting museum district Albertopolis (Roche 2000).

Exhibits and attractions

The fine art section held 6,529 works by 2,305 English and European artists, and the wall space was twice that of the Manchester show (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The British display covered half the fine art space and traced a naturalist line from Hogarth to the Pre-Raphaelites (Findling & Pelle 2008). The British galleries began with Hogarth (1762), whose 33 works anchored that line, while the French showed only recent work (Findling & Pelle 1990). Each nation chose its own artists and period, which Prasch reads as encouraging national schools of art (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh calls the show the nearest Britain came to the French model of fine art display (Greenhalgh 1988).

The industrial exhibits included the Bessemer process for making steel (Badger 1979; Mandell 1967), the calculating machine of Babbage (Findling & Pelle 1990), and the Crampton steam locomotive and mechanical typesetting (Roche 2000). Allwood, Allan and Reid also list aniline dyes, which they trace to a distillation by Béchamp in 1856, photography by the London Stereoscopic Company, steam power and the electric telegraph (Allwood et al. 2001). Victoria's Gold Trophy was a gold obelisk nearly 70 feet high, showing 800 tons 17 cwt of gold sent from the Australian colony in 1851–61 (Allwood et al. 2001). Benedict cites the obelisk as an example of sheer quantity used to impress (Benedict 1983).

Nations, empire and peoples on display

Thirty-five colonies and dominions exhibited (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008). Colonial display was kept apart from the rest. Fine art from New South Wales and India sat in national courts rather than among the other fine arts, and only goods made for Western consumption won praise (Findling & Pelle 1990). The British colonies covered over 13,000 square feet, with Victoria taking over 5,660 against New South Wales's 2,500 (Hoffenberg 2001). The Indian court covered 10,000 square feet, against 30,000 in 1851, and Greenhalgh calls it a smaller but more extravagant affair (Greenhalgh 1988).

Colonial displays also carried a hierarchy of peoples. Robert Hunt's official Synopsis treated Australian Aborigines, Indians and settler colonists as successive levels of evolutionary development (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Esquiros's guided tour moved from Black peoples, through Asian peoples he judged stagnant, to Europe, ending with France and England (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh argues that empire was displayed from 1851 onwards and that the record shows no anti-imperial phase in the period, saying that "empire was proudly and exhaustively displayed" (Greenhalgh 1988). Hoffenberg shows that colonial commissioners used the show to build permanent collections in their home museums, exchanging exhibits with English and European ones (Hoffenberg 2001). Organisers of the cotton machinery placed a sign naming Surat beside a self-acting mule, making the imperial supply of raw cotton explicit (Hoffenberg 2001).

Japan's first large appearance came through individuals rather than the state. The Tokugawa shogunate declined to take part, so Rutherford Alcock, the British Consul-General in Japan, sent lacquer, colour woodblock prints and cloisonné himself (Hur). Hur reports Alcock's own explanation that the ruling class wished to keep the country isolated (Hur). Hotta-Lister argues that these goods had been made for export to Western tastes, yet they still influenced British designers, and that Farmer and Rogers bought some of them (Hotta-Lister 1999). William Burges, an architect who had never been to Japan, admired them (Hotta-Lister 1999). Hur says the exhibit is believed to have started Japonism, the European taste for Japanese art (Hur). Members of the Japanese embassy then in Europe also visited. Fuchinobe Tokuzō, an embassy member spelled Fuchibe by Hotta-Lister, judged the display harshly: "Japan’s collection consists of sundries like an antique store." (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999). Fukuzawa Yukichi, who was in the embassy, saw an international exhibition for the first time (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999). Bakufu officials, the government of the shogunate, reported on the fair on their return (Hotta-Lister 1999). Peterson's footnote, citing Mizuta, dates Japan's presence at the expositions from 1862 (Peterson 2020).

Other exhibitors appear mostly as reference points. Chinese merchants, not the state, submitted silk, vases, ivory carvings and raw silk (Hur). The Ottoman Empire took part in London in 1851 and 1862 (Çelik 1992). Viceroy Muhammad Sa'id Pasha of Egypt attended, and his banker Henry Oppenheim met the Prussian architect Carl von Diebitsch, who had shown a zinc vase and won a medal (Raizman & Robey 2018). Hungarian sections were organised by the council of the governor-general in Buda and shown within the Austrian section (Filipová 2015). German states contributed separately, and the Zollverein states, a German customs union, exhibited together while dividing their area (Lawn 2009; Findling & Pelle 1990). Spain did not take an official part in the education section, which the chapter says frustrated educators and damaged the country's international prestige (Lawn 2009).

Visitors, workers and politics

The fair drew 6,211,103 visitors, against 6,039,195 in 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Crowds came, but not in substantially larger numbers (Findling & Pelle 1990). Shilling days, when admission cost a shilling and a tradition carried over from 1851, were meant to draw working-class visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008). Commentators noted few workers and few textile displays, because the textile economy was depressed (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Hoffenberg records that the Working Man attacked the celebration of capital and technology rather than labour, and that the Leisure Hour said workers were kept in the background. Even so, working-class visitors felt the hall was theirs (Hoffenberg 2001). The Bee-Hive called the fair a failure and blamed the commissioners' jobbery, meaning the corrupt use of office for favours (Hoffenberg 2001). Della Coletta says that the workers' delegations to London in 1851 and 1862 evolved into Marx's International Workingmen's Association (Della Coletta 2006). Roche cites Benjamin's view that the 1862 fair was directly important for the founding of that association, and he records visits by Ferdinand Lassalle and trade unionists from France, Germany and England (Roche 2000).

The Duke of Cambridge formally opened the fair with two thousand choristers and four hundred musicians. The Queen's expected presence had been cancelled after the Prince Consort's death (Hoffenberg 2001). The Royal Gun Factories displayed a trophy of guns that celebrated the union of manufacturing and war, and a contemporary critic protested: "No wonder the ghost of 1851 is so restless." (Hoffenberg 2001). Giberti records that Prince Napoléon's proposal to abolish juries and awards, meaning the panels that judged exhibits and gave prizes, was ignored, and that similar systems were adopted at London in 1862 and Paris in 1867 (Giberti 2002).

How historians have interpreted it

The main reading of the fair comes from Thomas Prasch, who wrote two reference entries on it, one in 1990 and one in 2008 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). He argues that the fair failed to match its predecessor, yet it confirmed international exhibitions as an institution and replaced the 1851 vision of universal progress with competitive nationalism (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid argue that the fair had far more and often better exhibits than 1851 but no conceptual advance, and that international exhibitions before Paris in 1867 merely tried to be bigger or better (Allwood et al. 2001). They conclude that the exhibition "never really caught the public imagination, and today is almost forgotten" (Allwood et al. 2001).

Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions were sites where imperial and colonial identities, economic networks and social order were built and contested, and not simply celebrations of progress (Hoffenberg 2001). For him the 1862 show illustrates that tension: the radical press called it a failure, its military trophies were read against the idea of peace, and working-class visitors were both courted and sidelined (Hoffenberg 2001). Hur argues that the first Chinese and Japanese appearances came through merchants and individuals, not state representation (Hur).

Geppert argues that complaints of exhibition fatigue began here, in the diaries of the engineer Max Eyth, long before the term was coined in the early 1880s (Geppert 2010). Roche argues that after 1862 Britain's approach changed and France, then the Americans, took the lead (Roche 2000). Giberti calls the 1862 fair a partial world's fair with no impact on the design of the 1876 Centennial in Philadelphia (Giberti 2002). Burris treats the fair briefly, arguing that it gave more attention to aesthetics after Paris in 1855 (Burris 2001). Kaiser argues that the United States' Civil War delayed its entry as a great exhibitor until Philadelphia in 1876 (Kaiser).

The texts agree that the fair was larger than 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001) and that it is little remembered (Burris 2001; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001). They disagree on whether it failed. Prasch treats it as a shortfall (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Hoffenberg reports that the radical press called it a failure (Hoffenberg 2001), while Burris sees it as slightly larger and more ambitious in aesthetics (Burris 2001). They also disagree on its effect. Roche sees it as a turning point in the genre (Roche 2000), but Giberti finds it had no impact on later design (Giberti 2002). On the finances, the accounts conflict and cannot be settled from the notes (Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). The texts say little about what ordinary visitors saw beyond the fine arts and industrial exhibits, or about how colonial peoples themselves responded to the displays.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan's goods reached the 1862 fair without the Tokugawa government. The shogunate declined to take part, and Alcock, the British Consul-General in Japan, personally sent lacquer, colour woodblock prints and cloisonné, which Alcock attributed to the ruling class's wish to keep the country isolated (Hurp. 36). Hotta-Lister argues that these articles had been made for Western export tastes, yet they still influenced designers, and that Farmer and Rogers bought some of them (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 204). Burges, who had never been to Japan, admired them (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 204). Hur says the exhibit is believed to have started Japonism (Hurp. 37), and Greenhalgh says Japanese art's discovery in the West followed its constant presence in exhibitions from 1862, with a marked impact in London that year (Greenhalgh 1988p. 148).

The texts differ on when Japan's own presence began. Peterson, citing Mizuta, dates it from London 1862 (Peterson 2020p. 40). Hur gives the Paris exposition of 1867 as Japan's debut at a world exhibition (Hurp. 38).

Members of the Japanese embassy then in Europe also saw the fair. Fuchinobe Tokuzō called the collection "sundries like an antique store" (Hurp. 37). Fukuzawa Yukichi and colleagues saw an international exhibition for the first time and was impressed by its scale, paid admission and trade benefits (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 203). The Illustrated London News praised the Japanese "court" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 204), so it and the embassy gave opposite verdicts. Bakufu officials, Fuchibe and Masugashira, reported to the government on their return. Fuchibe lamented the poor Japanese exhibits and blamed feudalism and seclusion (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 205). Hur notes that a shogunate newspaper had carried an article on the London exhibition that year, but Fukuzawa's book reached more readers (Hurp. 38).

China

Chinese goods also came through merchants. After declining the New York (1853) and Paris (1855) exhibitions, a few Chinese and foreign merchants from mainland China submitted silk, vases, ivory carvings, silkworms and raw silk to London in 1862 (Hurp. 12). Hur dates the Imperial Maritime Customs' management of Chinese exhibits from Vienna in 1873 (Hurp. 12).

India

India was shown mainly through colonial display. Fine art from New South Wales and India sat in the national courts, not among the other fine arts, and only goods made for Western consumption were praised. The Times approved of Bengali ivory carvers who introduced perspective into their landscapes under Western influence (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 28). Prasch reads the Synopsis, the official handbook by Robert Hunt, as treating Indians, Australian Aborigines and settler colonists as successive levels of development (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 28). Greenhalgh says the Indian court covered 10,000 square feet, against 30,000 in 1851, and calls it "a smaller but more extravagant affair" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Watson was commissioner and editor of the official catalogues for India at this exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285). Hoffenberg describes a sign reading "Surat" beside a self-acting mule, naming the South Asian source of the raw cotton and making the imperial supply network explicit (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104).

Interpretation

Hur argues that the first Chinese and Japanese appearances at world's fairs came through merchants and individuals, not through state representation (Hurp. 12). Greenhalgh argues that from 1851 onwards empire was "proudly and exhaustively displayed" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). Prasch's ethnographic reading arranges colonies and non-Western nations as stages of development (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 28). The texts say little about how South or Southeast Asian peoples themselves responded to the displays, and nothing about Southeast Asian exhibitors.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Bigger, not better — the texts measure the fair against 1851 and find more exhibits but less lasting impact and public affection (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Mandell 1967).
  • Finance and the guarantors — the guarantees, the commissioners' spending and the closing accounts are reported with conflicting figures, and the rescue by the contractors is a recurring detail (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Badger 1979).
  • Empire and colonial display — colonies were given large areas, arranged in an ethnographic hierarchy and used to build colonial collections at home (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Japan's first presence — Japanese goods came through a British consul and individuals, and they fed later enthusiasm for Japanese art (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999; Peterson 2020; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Competitive nationalism and national art — each nation chose its own artists and period, and the texts read the fair as a contest between national schools (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Filipová 2015).
  • Workers and internationalism — working-class visitors were courted and sidelined, and the texts link the fair to labour delegations and the socialist internationals (Hoffenberg 2001; Della Coletta 2006; Roche 2000; Kaiser).

Questions for study

  1. The texts give different accounts of whether the fair made a profit, and the figures do not reconcile. What evidence would a historian need to settle the question, and which kinds of source, such as ledgers, appendices or press reports, should carry most weight? (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Badger 1979)
  2. Almost every text measures the fair against 1851. Does being bigger but less remembered describe a failure, or does it measure a different thing from what the organisers intended? (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001)
  3. Greenhalgh finds no anti-imperial phase in the displays between 1851 and 1862, while Hoffenberg stresses how the displays were contested. What would count as evidence of opposition to empire at an exhibition? (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001)
  4. Japan's first presence came through a consul and individual travellers rather than the state. How far does that change the story of Japonism, the European taste for Japanese art, which Hur links to this fair? (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999)
  5. Geppert links the origins of exhibition fatigue to this fair, and Roche links it to the First International. Why do historians attach such different long-term meanings to a single exhibition? (Geppert 2010; Roche 2000)
  6. Working-class visitors had cheaper admission days, yet the texts describe them as largely absent or sidelined. What does this suggest about who exhibitions were built for? (Findling & Pelle 2008; Hoffenberg 2001)
