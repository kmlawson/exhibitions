Vienna World Exhibition

The Vienna World Exhibition (Wiener Weltausstellung) ran in the Prater from 1 May to 31 October 1873 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Austria organised it alone to show that the Habsburg state had recovered from its defeat by Prussia in 1866 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). It was the first international fair held in a German-speaking region (Findling & Pelle 2008), and it is one of the great financial failures of the nineteenth century (Allwood et al. 2001). Historians use it to study national display, imperial rivalry and the first official Japanese and Chinese showings at world fairs (Lawn 2009; Hur; Giberti 2002).

Origins and aims

The fair was meant to restore Austria's standing. The monarchy had lost its war with Prussia in 1866, and the Austro-Hungarian arrangement that followed had strengthened Hungarian influence (Findling & Pelle 2008). The Lower Austrian Trade Association proposed the exhibition in 1870 and offered a pledge of 6 million gulden to the government (Findling & Pelle 1990). Rottau says the emperor agreed in May and that 1873 was chosen as the twenty-fifth anniversary of his coronation (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk reads the aim as restoring the image of an empire that had lost territory and prestige since the mid-1850s (Findling & Pelle 1990). Filipová's volume adds a second purpose: to confirm a common state identity and present Vienna as a modern capital of the monarchy (Filipová 2015). The Austro-Hungarian government invited China, and the Austrian minister Heinrich von Calice persuaded the Meiji government in Japan to take part (Hur).

Organisation and finance

Wilhelm von Schwarz-Sendborn, who had organised Austria's participation in earlier fairs, was named general director (Findling & Pelle 1990). The texts disagree about the money. Rottau gives expenditure of 19 million gulden, the Austrian currency both Rottau and Sirk use, against an income of roughly 4.3 million (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk gives the overall loss as 15 million gulden, citing Franz Herre (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the final accounts showed an income of only one-sixth of the cost (Allwood et al. 2001). Badger's appendix gives a cost of £2,200,000 and receipts of £206,477 in pounds (Badger 1979).

The texts also disagree about who paid the shortfall. Rottau says the Austrian state covered it, unlike the London exhibition of 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh says the fair almost bankrupted the city, and that attendance decided whether each fair paid (Greenhalgh 1988).

Site and architecture

The fair stood in the Prater, the park created when the Danube canal was moved. Its ground was five times the size of the Paris site of 1867 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the ground was made from 500,000 cubic feet of gravel removed when the Danube was realigned (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts give different site sizes. Giberti says 280 acres (Giberti 2002), while Badger's appendix gives 40 acres (Badger 1979). Our texts do not explain the difference.

Austria built separate Palaces of Industry, Machinery, Agriculture and Art in an Italian Renaissance style, among Swiss-style chalets and ethnic buildings (Findling & Pelle 1990). Karl von Hasenauer designed the Palace of Industry (Findling & Pelle 2008). Its centre was a rotunda, realised by John Scott Russell, which Rottau gives as 354 feet across and 284 feet high, the largest rotunda ever built (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the same width (Allwood et al. 2001). The Palace used a herringbone system of galleries, which Rottau calls the definitive symbol of the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The texts disagree about the rotunda's later use. Giberti says it was to become a corn exchange (Giberti 2002). Rottau says it held events and exhibitions until a fire destroyed it in 1937 (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Nations on show

The Palace of Industry was arranged by country. Benedict says the national exhibitors were placed by Mercator's projection, a way of drawing the world map, so that each country sat beside its geographical neighbour (Benedict 1983). Giberti says critics read the result as a series of isolated national fairs, with Austria and Germany at the centre and the United States at the western end (Giberti 2002). Austria exhibited 9,000 of the 53,000 exhibitors, and the German Empire 8,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk notes that the fair claimed to show the cultures of the world, but German-speaking states dominated it (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Japan's showing recurs throughout the texts. It was the Meiji government's first official participation (Lawn 2009; Hotta-Lister 1999). Most exhibits were traditional craft objects that promoted Japonisme, the European taste for Japanese art (Lawn 2009). Japan sent about 6,000 objects, including a reproduction of a Kyoto temple and a Japanese garden (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hur says a 72-member commission under Sano Tsunetami went partly to show that Japan was independent of China, a confusion that Europeans seemed to make (Hur). Other texts give 66 engineers and craftsmen (Allwood et al. 2001) or more than sixty (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Japanese report on the fair ran to 96 volumes (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Burris 2001; Lawn 2009).

China's displays were run by foreigners. From Vienna onward the Imperial Maritime Customs Service, under Robert Hart, supplied the Chinese exhibits, and its staff were European and American (Hur; Raizman & Robey 2018). Hur argues that the samples from each port with a customs office reflected the service's priorities rather than China as a whole (Hur). Hart's diary called the collection "a decided success" (Hur).

Other displays appear too. The Indian Court covered 10,000 square feet at a cost of £12,342 (Greenhalgh 1988), and Watson coordinated the Indian Department (Hoffenberg 2001). Pottery from Alexander Hunter's Madras school won a Medal of Good Taste (Hoffenberg 2001). Çelik describes the Ottoman pavilion as a full-scale copy of the Sultan Ahmed Fountain near Hagia Sophia, built with Ottoman artisans and materials brought from Turkey (Çelik 1992). The Italian government sent a large model of the Mont Cenis tunnel entrance, with rail lines and a full-size train (Della Coletta 2006; Allwood et al. 2001).

Peoples on display

The Ethnographic Village drew attention in several texts. Székely says the Hungarian committee showed Hungary as a multi-ethnic region, with peasant houses for Germans, Hungarians, Szeklers, Romanians and Croatians (Filipová 2015). Houze lists the furnished wooden farmhouses as Hungarian, Saxon, Romanian, Szekler and Serbian, and says they delighted visitors (Raizman & Robey 2018). Rottau reads the village and the oriental quarter as a success for a multi-ethnic state (Findling & Pelle 2008). Houze argues that the village shaped later Hungarian displays of a modern yet culturally distinct nation (Raizman & Robey 2018).

Greenhalgh says the Austrians were the first to create an old city, a reconstructed eighteenth-century Vienna peopled with figures, and adds that Austria had no consistent policy of imperial display (Greenhalgh 1988).

Visitors and reception

The texts give different attendance totals. Rottau gives 7,255,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Filipová's volume gives 7,250,400, lower than Paris in 1867 (Filipová 2015). Badger's appendix gives 6,740,000 (Badger 1979). Rottau says attendance was only half the number Paris had counted in 1867 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rottau gives the length as 184 days (Findling & Pelle 2008), while the Filipová volume gives 176 (Filipová 2015).

The opening was rushed. Sirk says 4,000 soldiers helped finish the grounds (Findling & Pelle 1990). Nine days after the opening the Viennese stock market crashed (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Sirk says the crash caused a depression and suicides among speculators (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid link it to the 1872 financial crises in New York and London (Allwood et al. 2001). Cholera in the summer of 1873 killed an estimated 2,000 people, according to Sirk, and scared visitors away (Findling & Pelle 1990). Rottau also lists a cholera epidemic among the problems (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Hotel prices were a further deterrent. Sirk, and Allwood, Allan and Reid, blame the greed of hotel keepers, restaurant owners and cab drivers (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Attendance rose when the weather improved during the summer (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Freud visited the fair twice as an ordinary visitor and judged it a display for the aesthetic and superficial world. Roche uses the episode to show that expos were popular events mixing information with entertainment (Roche 2000).

Models and legacy

Philadelphia's planners studied Vienna closely. Goshorn, the Centennial's director-general, visited in 1873 (Rydell 1984; Hollengreen et al. 2014), and Maloney says the Machine Hall, Art Hall, two Agriculture halls and central rotunda made Vienna a better model for the Centennial than Paris 1867 (Maloney 2012). Giberti says the Centennial's classification committee judged that Vienna's geographic plan made comparison impossible, and that the Centennial's jury system was a deliberate reaction to it (Giberti 2002).

Badger says Paris 1867 and Vienna 1873 gave the United States its first impressive material proof of expansion (Badger 1979). Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that the successes of 1867 and 1873 encouraged American nationalists to seek federal backing (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Berlin's critics pointed to Vienna's deficits and ruined hotel prices as a warning, and Berlin's planners borrowed its central rotunda with fishbone galleries (Geppert 2010). In Japan, Hur argues that the Vienna experience led leaders to plan domestic exhibitions, the first held in Tokyo in 1877 (Hur).

How historians have interpreted it

Most texts agree that the fair was a financial failure. Rottau (Findling & Pelle 2008), Sirk (Findling & Pelle 1990), Allwood, Allan and Reid (Allwood et al. 2001) and Greenhalgh (Greenhalgh 1988) all record a loss, and Roche calls it a financial disaster that was nonetheless the most popular expo to date (Roche 2000). They differ on the causes. Allwood, Allan and Reid blame outside shocks: the 1872 crises, the collapse of the Vienna stock exchange, fear of cholera, and hoteliers who priced out tourists (Allwood et al. 2001). Sirk adds the greed of hotel keepers and cab drivers (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh gives a simpler account in which attendance decided the outcome (Greenhalgh 1988).

The texts read the fair's meaning differently. Rottau calls it "a turning point in the history of the world’s expositions", when organisers moved from mainly industrial display towards a more entertaining experience for a broad public (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rottau sees a success for a multi-ethnic state in the village and oriental quarter (Findling & Pelle 2008), while Sirk sees a tension between the fair's claim to show all world cultures and the dominance of German-speaking states (Findling & Pelle 1990). Filipová treats the fair as a benchmark for marginal cities (Filipová 2015).

Benedict argues that competition bred uniformity at fairs (Benedict 1983). Giberti treats Vienna as the foil for Philadelphia's jury system (Giberti 2002). Geppert reads Vienna as both a warning and a model for Berlin (Geppert 2010). Çelik reads the pavilions as statements about how Western powers mapped the world and how Muslim nations sought a modern image (Çelik 1992), while Hur reads the Chinese and Japanese displays as attempts to stage modern statehood (Hur).

The texts agree that the fair failed financially and that it became a model for later fairs. They differ on whether the peoples on show made a plural empire look secure or exposed its limits. Several texts mention Vienna only in passing (Peterson 2020; Shepherdson-Scott; Kaiser; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Mandell 1967).

Japan

Vienna in 1873 was the Meiji government's first official participation in an international fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208; Lawn 2009p. 74). The government allotted 600,000 yen and made Okuma Shigenobu Sōsai, or general commissioner (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208). Hur says Heinrich von Calice, the Austrian minister in Japan, persuaded the government to take part (Hurp. 40). Hotta-Lister gives Japan's expenditure as 520,858 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221).

Most Japanese exhibits were traditional craft objects that promoted Japonisme, and the government also hoped to enhance national dignity and promote exports (Lawn 2009p. 74). Rottau says Japan sent some 6,000 objects, including a reproduction of a Kyoto temple and a Japanese garden (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 49). Sigur describes the garden complex with a scale-model shrine, temple and market structures (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 93). Hotta-Lister adds a golden Shachi Hoko from Nagoya Castle (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208). The delegation's size differs by text: 72 members in Hur's account (Hurp. 40), 66 technical engineers in Allwood, Allan and Reid's (Allwood et al. 2001p. 38), and more than sixty in the 1990 dictionary (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51). The report ran to 96 volumes (Lawn 2009p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51).

Visitors drew their own conclusions. Hirayama Narinobu wrote that Europeans thought Japan a subject state of China and whispered "it's Chinese" at the sight of Japanese visitors (Hurp. 40). Hur argues that proving Japan's independence mattered more than prestige (Hurp. 41). Kaiser says the Japanese sections of 1867 and 1873 raised European interest in Japanese history and art (Kaiserp. 48). Cunliff P. Owen, the British commissioner, advised a Japanese commissioner to show authentic goods rather than Western imitations (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 51). Hur sees Vienna as the start of Japan's domestic exhibitions, the first held in Tokyo in 1877 (Hurp. 41). Hur counts 24 people selected from the commission and left in Europe to study technology (Hurp. 42), while Hotta-Lister counts 27 students sent abroad (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208).

China

From Vienna onward the Imperial Maritime Customs Service, under Robert Hart, supplied the Chinese exhibits [16, l. 592-594; 760-762; 04, l. 3874-3878]. Its staff were European and American (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). Hart's circular of 3 August 1872 asked each customs office to send samples and trade statistics, at the special invitation of the Austro-Hungarian government (Hurp. 13). Hur argues that the service focused on ports with a customs office rather than on China as a whole [16, l. 845-851; 5248-5254]. Hart's diary called the Chinese collection "a decided success" (Hurp. 17). Hur says the foreign staff's Orientalist taste shaped the selection of objects, including some that Chinese observers found problematic, and the circular listed "Models of Junks" (Hurp. 20).

India

The Indian Court covered 10,000 square feet and cost £12,342 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Watson coordinated the Indian Department as chief commissioner and director (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Hunter's Madras school pottery won a Medal of Good Taste, and the Indian collection excited the jurors' admiration (Hoffenberg 2001p. 194). The 1990 dictionary says that British India, with China and Japan, dazzled the fair more than Britain's own exhibits did (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51).

Layout

Findling and Pelle's encyclopedia says the 35 participating countries were arranged from east to west, with Austria as the gateway between them, placed in the middle (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 49). Giberti says Pettit described the eastern transepts as given to China and Japan, at the far end of a plan built as a world map centred on Austria and Germany (Giberti 2002).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Austrian prestige and the Habsburg state — the fair was staged to repair Austria's image after 1866, and it was read both as a statement of unity and as a display of imperial tension (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Filipová 2015).
  • Japan's first official showing — craft objects, Japonisme, a 72-member commission and a 96-volume report make Japan's debut a recurring case in the texts (Lawn 2009; Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • China and the customs service — the Imperial Maritime Customs Service supplied China's exhibits from Vienna onward, and Hur argues that its port samples gave a partial picture (Hur; Peterson 2020; Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Peoples on display — the Ethnographic Village and the reconstructed old city presented peoples and social life as part of the fair's appeal (Filipová 2015; Raizman & Robey 2018; Greenhalgh 1988; Benedict 1983).
  • Financial failure and attendance — the figures for cost, loss and visitors differ across the texts, and attendance is treated as the key to the fair's finances (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Greenhalgh 1988; Roche 2000).
  • Classification and national juries — the geographic arrangement and the national juries became the points of comparison with later fairs (Benedict 1983; Giberti 2002).
  • Models for Philadelphia and beyond — Vienna shaped planning for the Centennial and for later fairs in Berlin and Tokyo, and Helsinki's 1876 pavilion drew on it (Rydell 1984; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Maloney 2012; Giberti 2002; Geppert 2010; Filipová 2015; Hur).

Questions for study

  1. Should a fair's arrangement of exhibits by country be read as a neutral map of the world, as Benedict suggests (Benedict 1983), or as a set of isolated national displays that make comparison hard, as Giberti argues (Giberti 2002)?
  2. Who should carry the financial risk of a world fair: a city, a state, or private backers? The texts give different answers for Vienna (Findling & Pelle 2008; Greenhalgh 1988).
  3. Did the Ethnographic Village show a successful multi-ethnic state, or did it expose the tensions within the empire? How would you decide between Rottau's reading (Findling & Pelle 2008) and Sirk's (Findling & Pelle 1990)?
  4. Japan's display was meant to prove independence from China as much as to win prestige (Hur). What does this suggest about how a national display can have audiences beyond the fair's visitors?
  5. The texts give different attendance and cost figures for the same fair. What does this tell us about how the statistics of world fairs were produced and reported?
  6. How far did one fair shape later exhibitions in other cities, such as Philadelphia, Berlin and Helsinki? Which lessons do the texts say were carried forward, and which were rejected (Rydell 1984; Giberti 2002; Geppert 2010; Filipová 2015)?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
4The Big Step Forward and the American Centennial Expositionp. 31

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the shift from the London exhibitions to the wider fairs of the 1860s and 1870s. The main case is Paris in 1867, which introduced national pavilions, Frédéric Le Play's concentric galleries including the History of Work, and a building by Gustave Eiffel, and which was overshadowed by the Tsar's near-assassination and news of Maximilian's execution. The London International Exhibitions of 1871 to 1874 failed as a thematic series, while the Vienna exhibition of 1873 was undone by a stock-market collapse and high hotel prices, its income reaching only a sixth of its cost. Philadelphia's Centennial Exhibition of 1876, whose grounds were planned by Herman Schwarzmann, is presented as the first properly landscaped international exposition, with the Corliss engine and Bell's telephone among its displays. The chapter ends with the Centennial's funding from public money and concessions, and with the later demolition of its main buildings.

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
2The Growth and Character of the Institutionp. 7

Badger examines the world's fair as an institution, which between 1851 and 1925 became a standard part of Western culture. Each fair claimed to display the latest stage of progress, from British machinery and Bessemer steel in London to German motors at Paris in 1867 and the telephone and typewriter at Philadelphia in 1876. Architecture and the arts had to show refinement, which gave them a largely functional role, and the congresses held alongside the fairs seldom did more than reflect current thought. National purposes shaped many fairs, from Austria's recovery in Vienna in 1873 to Napoleon III's rivalry with London in 1855 and 1867. Badger then turns to finance, showing that governments subsidised fairs and that few made a profit, and to the city, which he treats as the greatest exhibit of any fair. The chapter closes by arguing that the claimed economic gains to host cities, hard to measure, were used to win public money from city governments.

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
1A Capital in the Margins: Concepts for a Budapest Universal Exhibition between 1867 and 1917Miklós Székelyp. 23

Székely argues that the proposals for a universal exhibition in Budapest between 1867 and 1917 show how a semi-sovereign state presented itself at home and abroad. Within the Dual Monarchy Hungary had its own government but no independent foreign or military policy, so its exhibitions asserted a 'sovereign' and 'Hungarian' identity through pavilion architecture, the revival of historic styles and vernacular art, and commemoration. The chapter follows three planning efforts: Szilárd Blána's utopian permanent exhibition of 1874, Count Jenő Zichy's proposal to mark the 1896 Millennium with a universal exhibition, and Leo Lánczy's 1911 scheme for 1917, the fiftieth anniversary of Franz Joseph's coronation. Its evidence comes from Hungarian displays at Paris in 1867 and 1900, the forestry pavilion and ethnographic village at Vienna in 1873, and the Turin pavilion of 1911. Székely concludes that none of the Budapest universal exhibitions was realised, and that the plans show a modernising nation looking to its glorious past while displaying its present achievements abroad.

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
2Berlin 1896: Wilhelm II, Georg Simmel and the Berliner Gewerbeausstellungp. 16

Geppert asks why Germany never hosted an international exposition like those in London, Paris or Vienna, and what the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung of 1896 was in their place. He traces three failed phases of the 'exhibition question' (1878-82, 1891-92, 1907-10), in which businessmen's associations and government debated and dropped plans, and attributes the final failure largely to Wilhelm II's personal opposition, with cost and site problems behind it. The chapter then turns to the privately financed fair: its layout, the colonial section with its 'native villages', the Egyptian quarter Kairo and Alt-Berlin. Reading Georg Simmel's 1896 review beside the Kaiser's letters and the press, Geppert argues that the fair tested Berlin's claim to be a world city and found it wanting next to Paris. It concludes that the fair left few urban traces but was publicised as a rehearsal for a later world exhibition.

In the contents of this work

7Conclusion: Exhibition Fatigue, or the Rise and Fall of a Mass Mediump. 201

Geppert's concluding chapter asks why world and imperial exhibitions, at their height around 1900, also drew sustained criticism. It traces the German term Ausstellungsmüdigkeit ('exhibition fatigue') from the diaries of the engineer Max Eyth, who worked at fairs in London, Vienna and Paris, through German debates from the 1880s to similar complaints in France and Britain. It then follows the institutional response: a German permanent exhibition commission, a British Board of Trade branch, and the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions. A proposed Anglo-German exhibition at the White City, which German officials blocked, shows how these fears worked in practice. The second half turns to sites. Geppert compares the Eiffel Tower, the Ferris wheels at Earl's Court and Paris, and the Berlin Funkturm, and treats the grounds in London, Paris and Berlin as 'cities within the city' that were absorbed into urban life in different ways. The chapter closes by proposing three elements of a theory of exposition practice: transience, the movement from space to place, and the chronotope of time and space.

Giberti, Bruno. Designing the Centennial: A History of the 1876 International Exhibition in Philadelphia. Lexington: University Press of Kentucky, 2002.
1The Order of the Exhibition

Giberti argues that the organisers of the 1876 Centennial were preoccupied with classification, and that this preoccupation shaped the whole exhibition. The chapter traces the classificatory logic from the French national expositions of 1798-1849, through London in 1851, where Playfair's thirty classes gave way to a geographic installation, to Paris in 1855 and 1867, where Napoleon's dual system of country and type produced a wheel-like plan. Vienna in 1873 is the counter-case, a developmental order realised as national courts along one nave. The Philadelphia scheme, drafted by a committee that included William Blake, combined these models in ten departments and a decimal notation modelled on Philadelphia's street numbers. The classification was then cut to seven departments to fit the buildings, and the official catalogue was late and disordered, which Giberti presents as evidence that the exhibition was never fully ordered. The chapter ends by arguing that the organisers had to keep revising their project in the face of conditions they could not foresee.

In the contents of this work

2The Architecture of the Exhibition

Giberti argues that the architecture of the Centennial was shaped by the split between architecture and engineering, and that engineers had more influence on the fair than architects did. The chapter takes three precedents, the Crystal Palace of 1851, the elliptical hall of Paris in 1867 and Vienna's industrial palace of 1873, and reads each for its vista, its plan and its flaws. The central case is the 1873 competition, which drew forty-three entries and whose four prizes all went to Philadelphia firms, though the committee then declined to endorse any winner. Giberti follows the unpremiated Pavilion Plan of Vaux and Radford, a timber-and-iron hall of uniform bays that the chapter treats as the most modern scheme entered, and then the process by which Pettit and Wilson, both engineers, produced a rectangular iron-and-brick building with shedlike wings. The chapter concludes that the Centennial was an engineer's landscape, whose utilitarian buildings anticipated twentieth-century modernism while architecture was still weak as a profession.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
OneChina and the World Exhibitions before 1903p. 11Asia

Chapter One traces China's presence at world exhibitions from London in 1851 to the Paris exposition of 1900, and argues that the foreign-staffed Imperial Maritime Customs shaped how China was shown. The Customs took charge of Chinese exhibits from Vienna in 1873, and the foreign commissioners' selections of local curiosities, including bound-feet shoes and opium tools, later became a target for Chinese critics. Early travelogues by Bin Chun, Li Gui, Guo Songtao and Zeng Jize describe Philadelphia in 1876 and Paris in 1878, and reformers such as Zheng Guanying argued from the 1890s that China should hold its own exhibitions. The chapter also compares China with Japan, whose exhibits from Philadelphia onwards outshone China's, including at Chicago in 1893, and it ends with the Qing court's return to Beijing in 1902, when the reformers' hold on the debate began to change.

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Formation and Transformation of Education in Japan through Exhibitions: focused on the Educational Museum founded in 1877Kayoko Komatsup. 73Asia

Komatsu asks how international and domestic exhibitions helped to modernise education in early Meiji Japan, using the Educational Museum that opened in Ueno Park in 1877 as the main case. Japanese participation in the Vienna (1873) and Philadelphia (1876) fairs and the first Domestic Exhibition supplied the museum with foreign apparatus and books, many bought at fairs and displayed beside Japanese copies made from them. Komatsu uses the museum's guidebook, visitor figures and departmental reports to show how this arrangement pointed Japan towards European materials, and how private makers were encouraged to imitate European physics and chemistry machines for sale to schools. The second half traces the museum's decline after 1885, when it was incorporated into the Tokyo Library and its building passed to the Tokyo Art School, and relates this to the replacement of the Pestalozzian 'Developmental Mode of Teaching' by Herbartian method. The conclusion presents the fairs and the museum as two 'nodes' in a network that carried modern educational materials into Japanese schools.

The Bull and the Book: images of Spain and Spanish education in the World Fairs of the nineteenth century, 1851-1900Maria del Mar del Pozo Andrésp. 153

Del Pozo Andrés asks how Spain presented itself through education at the nineteenth-century world fairs, from London in 1851 to Paris in 1900. The argument is that Spanish participation was driven by a wish to restore national pride after imperial decline, and that the fairs staged a contradictory nationalism, boastful about prizes yet anxious about backwardness. The evidence comes from official catalogues, commissioners' reports and the press. Cases include the absence of Spanish teaching materials in London in 1862, the Paris pavilion of 1867 with its stuffed bull, the Philadelphia display of some 715 deluxe books that won ninety-three prizes in 1876, the dispute over an illiteracy chart at Paris in 1878, the pedagogic congress held in Barcelona in 1888 and the Paris pavilion of 1900. The chapter also follows Martin de Martin y Ruiz, a deaf, dumb and blind student shown in Vienna in 1873. It concludes that the fairs supplied new ideas, but that Spanish schools adopted them slowly, and graded schools took hold only after the loss of the colonies in 1898.

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
Introductionp. 1–2

Celik introduces a study of how Islamic architecture was displayed at nineteenth-century world's fairs, focusing on the Paris expositions of 1867, 1878, 1889 and 1900, Vienna in 1873 and the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893. He argues that the fairs claimed scientific accuracy while indulging fantasy, and that their Islamic pavilions show both how Europeans constructed the Orient and how Muslim states tried to define a modern cultural identity. Celik gives three reasons for choosing Islam: it stood as Europe's binary opposite, it raises a debate about modernity that is still live, and non-Western perspectives reveal a more complex nineteenth-century world. He surveys the political history of the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco and Iran to show how colonial control varied, and sketches the thought of Ibrahim Sinasi, al-Tahtawi and Khayr al-Din, who saw European progress as compatible with Islam. The chapter ends by previewing the book's chapters on site plans, pavilion styles, Ottoman and Egyptian displays and Western architects' use of Islamic styles.

2Islamic Quarters in Western Citiesp. 51

Celik's fourth chapter examines the Islamic quarters built as street-level spectacle in Western cities, from the Ottoman and Egyptian sections at Paris in 1867 to the Cairo Street and Ottoman village at Chicago in 1893. Celik argues that the designers pursued authenticity through collected fragments and irregular plans, yet their settings were ahistorical, collapsing periods and regions into single structures that made Islamic cultures appear incapable of change. The chapter follows how the layouts moved from the picturesque, as in the 1867 Egyptian and Tunisian pavilions and Vienna in 1873, to the regularised and axial Tunisian village of 1900. Its key case is the Rue du Caire that Delort de Gleon built in 1889 from recycled Cairo fragments, which Celik reads as a blend of archaeological claim and commerce, and whose mosque was used as a coffeehouse according to one Egyptian visitor. The chapter concludes that after four decades the Islamic world no longer seemed exotic, and that with colonial expansion the exotic increasingly belonged to the Western powers.

3Search for Identity: Architecture of National Pavilionsp. 95

Çelik asks how the national pavilions of Islamic states and colonies at nineteenth-century world's fairs were designed, and what image of Islamic architecture they gave Western visitors. The chapter moves roughly chronologically through the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Iran, Tunisia, Morocco and Algeria, beginning with the Ottoman buildings in Paris in 1867, where Leon Parvillee used measured drawings of Bursa to argue that Ottoman architecture followed geometric rules. It then follows replicas of Istanbul monuments, such as the Sultan Ahmed Fountain at Vienna in 1873, the Egyptian temple modelled on Philae, and the Persian and Tunisian palaces, to the colonial palaces of Algeria and Tunisia at Paris in 1878, 1889 and 1900. Çelik shows that most of the architects were French and academically trained, and that they drew on publications more than firsthand knowledge. The conclusion is that regional differences at the fairs were stylistic rather than a matter of building type, and that in the colonies France used Islamic forms to display its own power.

3The Installation in the Main Building

Giberti examines how the Centennial's Main Building was planned, built and filled with exhibits. The chapter starts with the 1873 site plan and the formal axial layout of the grounds, which drew on the Vienna and Paris fairs and set buildings across the fairground in the new pavilion pattern. It then turns to the Main Building itself, a prefabricated iron-and-wood shed that Giberti reads as a rationalised industrial loft, and to the two schemes for installing displays inside it. The first, a dual grid of nations and departments taken from Paris, was abandoned; the second, an installation by races centred on the four industrial powers, was only partly realised. Giberti argues that the organisers wanted the arrangement to make the classification visible, yet the last-minute scramble for space and the demand for striking displays along the avenues undid that transparency. The chapter closes by suggesting that the exhibition's ideal of legibility gave way to the glance of a mobile crowd.

Benedict, an anthropologist, reads world's fairs as symbolic structures that stood for the host nation's view of the world. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

Wikipedia

The 1873 Vienna World's Fair (German: Weltausstellung 1873 Wien) was a large world exposition that was held from 1 May to 31 October 1873 in the Austria-Hungarian capital Vienna. Its motto was "Culture and Education" (German: Kultur und Erziehung).
