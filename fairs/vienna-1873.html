Vienna · 1873

Vienna World Exhibition

Wiener Weltausstellung

The Vienna World Exhibition (Wiener Weltausstellung) ran in the Prater from 1 May to 31 October 1873 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Austria organised it alone to show that the Habsburg state had recovered from its defeat by Prussia in 1866 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). It was the first international fair held in a German-speaking region (Findling & Pelle 2008), and it is one of the great financial failures of the nineteenth century (Allwood et al. 2001). Historians use it to study national display, imperial rivalry and the first official Japanese and Chinese showings at world fairs (Lawn 2009; Hur; Giberti 2002).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair was meant to restore Austria's standing. The monarchy had lost its war with Prussia in 1866, and the Austro-Hungarian arrangement that followed had strengthened Hungarian influence (Findling & Pelle 2008). The Lower Austrian Trade Association proposed the exhibition in 1870 and offered a pledge of 6 million gulden to the government (Findling & Pelle 1990). Rottau says the emperor agreed in May and that 1873 was chosen as the twenty-fifth anniversary of his coronation (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk reads the aim as restoring the image of an empire that had lost territory and prestige since the mid-1850s (Findling & Pelle 1990). Filipová's volume adds a second purpose: to confirm a common state identity and present Vienna as a modern capital of the monarchy (Filipová 2015). The Austro-Hungarian government invited China, and the Austrian minister Heinrich von Calice persuaded the Meiji government in Japan to take part (Hur).

Organisation and finance

Wilhelm von Schwarz-Sendborn, who had organised Austria's participation in earlier fairs, was named general director (Findling & Pelle 1990). The texts disagree about the money. Rottau gives expenditure of 19 million gulden, the Austrian currency both Rottau and Sirk use, against an income of roughly 4.3 million (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk gives the overall loss as 15 million gulden, citing Franz Herre (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the final accounts showed an income of only one-sixth of the cost (Allwood et al. 2001). Badger's appendix gives a cost of £2,200,000 and receipts of £206,477 in pounds (Badger 1979).

The texts also disagree about who paid the shortfall. Rottau says the Austrian state covered it, unlike the London exhibition of 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh says the fair almost bankrupted the city, and that attendance decided whether each fair paid (Greenhalgh 1988).

Site and architecture

The fair stood in the Prater, the park created when the Danube canal was moved. Its ground was five times the size of the Paris site of 1867 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the ground was made from 500,000 cubic feet of gravel removed when the Danube was realigned (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts give different site sizes. Giberti says 280 acres (Giberti 2002), while Badger's appendix gives 40 acres (Badger 1979). Our texts do not explain the difference.

Austria built separate Palaces of Industry, Machinery, Agriculture and Art in an Italian Renaissance style, among Swiss-style chalets and ethnic buildings (Findling & Pelle 1990). Karl von Hasenauer designed the Palace of Industry (Findling & Pelle 2008). Its centre was a rotunda, realised by John Scott Russell, which Rottau gives as 354 feet across and 284 feet high, the largest rotunda ever built (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the same width (Allwood et al. 2001). The Palace used a herringbone system of galleries, which Rottau calls the definitive symbol of the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The texts disagree about the rotunda's later use. Giberti says it was to become a corn exchange (Giberti 2002). Rottau says it held events and exhibitions until a fire destroyed it in 1937 (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Nations on show

The Palace of Industry was arranged by country. Benedict says the national exhibitors were placed by Mercator's projection, a way of drawing the world map, so that each country sat beside its geographical neighbour (Benedict 1983). Giberti says critics read the result as a series of isolated national fairs, with Austria and Germany at the centre and the United States at the western end (Giberti 2002). Austria exhibited 9,000 of the 53,000 exhibitors, and the German Empire 8,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Sirk notes that the fair claimed to show the cultures of the world, but German-speaking states dominated it (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Japan's showing recurs throughout the texts. It was the Meiji government's first official participation (Lawn 2009; Hotta-Lister 1999). Most exhibits were traditional craft objects that promoted Japonisme, the European taste for Japanese art (Lawn 2009). Japan sent about 6,000 objects, including a reproduction of a Kyoto temple and a Japanese garden (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hur says a 72-member commission under Sano Tsunetami went partly to show that Japan was independent of China, a confusion that Europeans seemed to make (Hur). Other texts give 66 engineers and craftsmen (Allwood et al. 2001) or more than sixty (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Japanese report on the fair ran to 96 volumes (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Burris 2001; Lawn 2009).

China's displays were run by foreigners. From Vienna onward the Imperial Maritime Customs Service, under Robert Hart, supplied the Chinese exhibits, and its staff were European and American (Hur; Raizman & Robey 2018). Hur argues that the samples from each port with a customs office reflected the service's priorities rather than China as a whole (Hur). Hart's diary called the collection "a decided success" (Hur).

Other displays appear too. The Indian Court covered 10,000 square feet at a cost of £12,342 (Greenhalgh 1988), and Watson coordinated the Indian Department (Hoffenberg 2001). Pottery from Alexander Hunter's Madras school won a Medal of Good Taste (Hoffenberg 2001). Çelik describes the Ottoman pavilion as a full-scale copy of the Sultan Ahmed Fountain near Hagia Sophia, built with Ottoman artisans and materials brought from Turkey (Çelik 1992). The Italian government sent a large model of the Mont Cenis tunnel entrance, with rail lines and a full-size train (Della Coletta 2006; Allwood et al. 2001).

Peoples on display

The Ethnographic Village drew attention in several texts. Székely says the Hungarian committee showed Hungary as a multi-ethnic region, with peasant houses for Germans, Hungarians, Szeklers, Romanians and Croatians (Filipová 2015). Houze lists the furnished wooden farmhouses as Hungarian, Saxon, Romanian, Szekler and Serbian, and says they delighted visitors (Raizman & Robey 2018). Rottau reads the village and the oriental quarter as a success for a multi-ethnic state (Findling & Pelle 2008). Houze argues that the village shaped later Hungarian displays of a modern yet culturally distinct nation (Raizman & Robey 2018).

Greenhalgh says the Austrians were the first to create an old city, a reconstructed eighteenth-century Vienna peopled with figures, and adds that Austria had no consistent policy of imperial display (Greenhalgh 1988).

Visitors and reception

The texts give different attendance totals. Rottau gives 7,255,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Filipová's volume gives 7,250,400, lower than Paris in 1867 (Filipová 2015). Badger's appendix gives 6,740,000 (Badger 1979). Rottau says attendance was only half the number Paris had counted in 1867 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rottau gives the length as 184 days (Findling & Pelle 2008), while the Filipová volume gives 176 (Filipová 2015).

The opening was rushed. Sirk says 4,000 soldiers helped finish the grounds (Findling & Pelle 1990). Nine days after the opening the Viennese stock market crashed (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Sirk says the crash caused a depression and suicides among speculators (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid link it to the 1872 financial crises in New York and London (Allwood et al. 2001). Cholera in the summer of 1873 killed an estimated 2,000 people, according to Sirk, and scared visitors away (Findling & Pelle 1990). Rottau also lists a cholera epidemic among the problems (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Hotel prices were a further deterrent. Sirk, and Allwood, Allan and Reid, blame the greed of hotel keepers, restaurant owners and cab drivers (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Attendance rose when the weather improved during the summer (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Freud visited the fair twice as an ordinary visitor and judged it a display for the aesthetic and superficial world. Roche uses the episode to show that expos were popular events mixing information with entertainment (Roche 2000).

Models and legacy

Philadelphia's planners studied Vienna closely. Goshorn, the Centennial's director-general, visited in 1873 (Rydell 1984; Hollengreen et al. 2014), and Maloney says the Machine Hall, Art Hall, two Agriculture halls and central rotunda made Vienna a better model for the Centennial than Paris 1867 (Maloney 2012). Giberti says the Centennial's classification committee judged that Vienna's geographic plan made comparison impossible, and that the Centennial's jury system was a deliberate reaction to it (Giberti 2002).

Badger says Paris 1867 and Vienna 1873 gave the United States its first impressive material proof of expansion (Badger 1979). Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that the successes of 1867 and 1873 encouraged American nationalists to seek federal backing (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Berlin's critics pointed to Vienna's deficits and ruined hotel prices as a warning, and Berlin's planners borrowed its central rotunda with fishbone galleries (Geppert 2010). In Japan, Hur argues that the Vienna experience led leaders to plan domestic exhibitions, the first held in Tokyo in 1877 (Hur).

How historians have interpreted it

Most texts agree that the fair was a financial failure. Rottau (Findling & Pelle 2008), Sirk (Findling & Pelle 1990), Allwood, Allan and Reid (Allwood et al. 2001) and Greenhalgh (Greenhalgh 1988) all record a loss, and Roche calls it a financial disaster that was nonetheless the most popular expo to date (Roche 2000). They differ on the causes. Allwood, Allan and Reid blame outside shocks: the 1872 crises, the collapse of the Vienna stock exchange, fear of cholera, and hoteliers who priced out tourists (Allwood et al. 2001). Sirk adds the greed of hotel keepers and cab drivers (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh gives a simpler account in which attendance decided the outcome (Greenhalgh 1988).

The texts read the fair's meaning differently. Rottau calls it "a turning point in the history of the world’s expositions", when organisers moved from mainly industrial display towards a more entertaining experience for a broad public (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rottau sees a success for a multi-ethnic state in the village and oriental quarter (Findling & Pelle 2008), while Sirk sees a tension between the fair's claim to show all world cultures and the dominance of German-speaking states (Findling & Pelle 1990). Filipová treats the fair as a benchmark for marginal cities (Filipová 2015).

Benedict argues that competition bred uniformity at fairs (Benedict 1983). Giberti treats Vienna as the foil for Philadelphia's jury system (Giberti 2002). Geppert reads Vienna as both a warning and a model for Berlin (Geppert 2010). Çelik reads the pavilions as statements about how Western powers mapped the world and how Muslim nations sought a modern image (Çelik 1992), while Hur reads the Chinese and Japanese displays as attempts to stage modern statehood (Hur).

The texts agree that the fair failed financially and that it became a model for later fairs. They differ on whether the peoples on show made a plural empire look secure or exposed its limits. Several texts mention Vienna only in passing (Peterson 2020; Shepherdson-Scott; Kaiser; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Mandell 1967).

Japan

Vienna in 1873 was the Meiji government's first official participation in an international fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208; Lawn 2009p. 74). The government allotted 600,000 yen and made Okuma Shigenobu Sōsai, or general commissioner (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208). Hur says Heinrich von Calice, the Austrian minister in Japan, persuaded the government to take part (Hurp. 40). Hotta-Lister gives Japan's expenditure as 520,858 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221).

Most Japanese exhibits were traditional craft objects that promoted Japonisme, and the government also hoped to enhance national dignity and promote exports (Lawn 2009p. 74). Rottau says Japan sent some 6,000 objects, including a reproduction of a Kyoto temple and a Japanese garden (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 49). Sigur describes the garden complex with a scale-model shrine, temple and market structures (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 93). Hotta-Lister adds a golden Shachi Hoko from Nagoya Castle (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208). The delegation's size differs by text: 72 members in Hur's account (Hurp. 40), 66 technical engineers in Allwood, Allan and Reid's (Allwood et al. 2001p. 38), and more than sixty in the 1990 dictionary (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51). The report ran to 96 volumes (Lawn 2009p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51).

Visitors drew their own conclusions. Hirayama Narinobu wrote that Europeans thought Japan a subject state of China and whispered "it's Chinese" at the sight of Japanese visitors (Hurp. 40). Hur argues that proving Japan's independence mattered more than prestige (Hurp. 41). Kaiser says the Japanese sections of 1867 and 1873 raised European interest in Japanese history and art (Kaiserp. 48). Cunliff P. Owen, the British commissioner, advised a Japanese commissioner to show authentic goods rather than Western imitations (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 51). Hur sees Vienna as the start of Japan's domestic exhibitions, the first held in Tokyo in 1877 (Hurp. 41). Hur counts 24 people selected from the commission and left in Europe to study technology (Hurp. 42), while Hotta-Lister counts 27 students sent abroad (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 208).

China

From Vienna onward the Imperial Maritime Customs Service, under Robert Hart, supplied the Chinese exhibits [16, l. 592-594; 760-762; 04, l. 3874-3878]. Its staff were European and American (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). Hart's circular of 3 August 1872 asked each customs office to send samples and trade statistics, at the special invitation of the Austro-Hungarian government (Hurp. 13). Hur argues that the service focused on ports with a customs office rather than on China as a whole [16, l. 845-851; 5248-5254]. Hart's diary called the Chinese collection "a decided success" (Hurp. 17). Hur says the foreign staff's Orientalist taste shaped the selection of objects, including some that Chinese observers found problematic, and the circular listed "Models of Junks" (Hurp. 20).

India

The Indian Court covered 10,000 square feet and cost £12,342 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Watson coordinated the Indian Department as chief commissioner and director (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Hunter's Madras school pottery won a Medal of Good Taste, and the Indian collection excited the jurors' admiration (Hoffenberg 2001p. 194). The 1990 dictionary says that British India, with China and Japan, dazzled the fair more than Britain's own exhibits did (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 51).

Layout

Findling and Pelle's encyclopedia says the 35 participating countries were arranged from east to west, with Austria as the gateway between them, placed in the middle (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 49). Giberti says Pettit described the eastern transepts as given to China and Japan, at the far end of a plan built as a world map centred on Austria and Germany (Giberti 2002).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Austrian prestige and the Habsburg state — the fair was staged to repair Austria's image after 1866, and it was read both as a statement of unity and as a display of imperial tension (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Filipová 2015).
  • Japan's first official showing — craft objects, Japonisme, a 72-member commission and a 96-volume report make Japan's debut a recurring case in the texts (Lawn 2009; Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • China and the customs service — the Imperial Maritime Customs Service supplied China's exhibits from Vienna onward, and Hur argues that its port samples gave a partial picture (Hur; Peterson 2020; Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Peoples on display — the Ethnographic Village and the reconstructed old city presented peoples and social life as part of the fair's appeal (Filipová 2015; Raizman & Robey 2018; Greenhalgh 1988; Benedict 1983).
  • Financial failure and attendance — the figures for cost, loss and visitors differ across the texts, and attendance is treated as the key to the fair's finances (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Greenhalgh 1988; Roche 2000).
  • Classification and national juries — the geographic arrangement and the national juries became the points of comparison with later fairs (Benedict 1983; Giberti 2002).
  • Models for Philadelphia and beyond — Vienna shaped planning for the Centennial and for later fairs in Berlin and Tokyo, and Helsinki's 1876 pavilion drew on it (Rydell 1984; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Maloney 2012; Giberti 2002; Geppert 2010; Filipová 2015; Hur).

Questions for study

  1. Should a fair's arrangement of exhibits by country be read as a neutral map of the world, as Benedict suggests (Benedict 1983), or as a set of isolated national displays that make comparison hard, as Giberti argues (Giberti 2002)?
  2. Who should carry the financial risk of a world fair: a city, a state, or private backers? The texts give different answers for Vienna (Findling & Pelle 2008; Greenhalgh 1988).
  3. Did the Ethnographic Village show a successful multi-ethnic state, or did it expose the tensions within the empire? How would you decide between Rottau's reading (Findling & Pelle 2008) and Sirk's (Findling & Pelle 1990)?
  4. Japan's display was meant to prove independence from China as much as to win prestige (Hur). What does this suggest about how a national display can have audiences beyond the fair's visitors?
  5. The texts give different attendance and cost figures for the same fair. What does this tell us about how the statistics of world fairs were produced and reported?
  6. How far did one fair shape later exhibitions in other cities, such as Philadelphia, Berlin and Helsinki? Which lessons do the texts say were carried forward, and which were rejected (Rydell 1984; Giberti 2002; Geppert 2010; Filipová 2015)?

A general history of exhibitions by Allwood, Allan and Reid. Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
Entry 'Vienna 1873' by Nadine Rottau, pp. 45-52; ends with a bibliographical note

A reference entry on the 1873 Vienna fair by Nadine Rottau. Rottau presents the fair as a turning point in the history of world's fairs, when the organisers moved from a mainly industrial and technical display towards a more entertaining experience for a broad public, and notes that it was the first international fair in a German-speaking region. She links the project to Austria's need for prestige after its defeat by Prussia in 1866 and the new Austro-Hungarian arrangement, and argues that the fair's architecture, ethnographic village and oriental sections were a success for a multi-ethnic state. She explains its financial failure through the shortfall, weather, the stock-market crash, cholera and empty hotels, and ends with a contemporary critic's view that the fair showed no individual could any longer take in the whole of modern society.

A dictionary entry on the Vienna fair by Leila G. Sirk. Sirk presents the fair as an effort to restore the image of the Austro-Hungarian Empire after its losses of the 1850s and its defeat by Prussia in 1866, and describes its festive program of Strauss concerts, ethnic restaurants, medals and a monumental rotunda. Her argument turns on a tension between the fair's claim to represent the cultures of the world, when German-speaking states in practice dominated it, and an exhibit so large that no single visitor could take it in. She explains the failure through the stock-market crash, cholera, a flood, and the greed of hotel and restaurant keepers, and closes by treating the fair as the height of Vienna's cultural grandeur before its decline early in the next century.

Giberti's book on the Philadelphia Centennial uses Vienna 1873 as the main foil for the Centennial's design, in a section headed 'Vienna, 1873'. Giberti describes Vienna's industrial palace as a geographic map of the world, arranged by country, which critics said read as a series of isolated national fairs and lacked the unified classification of Paris 1867. He traces the consequences: Vienna's juries returned to graduated medals with national judging, and the Centennial's classification committee judged that the geographic plan made comparison impossible. The Centennial's jury system is presented as a conscious reaction to earlier experience, and Vienna's national juries are among the cases cited.

Çelik's study of how Islamic cultures were displayed at world's fairs names the 1873 Vienna exposition among the six fairs discussed at length. For Vienna, Çelik describes the Ottoman pavilion as a faithful full-scale copy built with Ottoman artisans and Turkish materials, and a Sultan's Treasury whose display cases were later reused in schools, museums and public buildings. Moderate discussion 6

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
Sections on the symbolic structure of fairs (PDF p. 31), on layout and Mercator's projection (PDF p. 37), and on classification (PDF p. 47); Table 1 (printed p. 22).

Benedict, an anthropologist, reads world's fairs as symbolic structures that stood for the host nation's view of the world. Vienna 1873 is one of his recurring examples: its huge main building with a dome is cited as the fair's symbol, and its national exhibitors were arranged by Mercator's projection so that each country sat beside its geographical neighbour. He also notes that its 26 groups gave more attention than earlier fairs to domestic life, instruction and social life, while the fair carried a strong progressive evolutionist bias. His main argument is that competition bred uniformity, and he illustrates it with Boito's 1873 remark that national arts were becoming assimilated.

“The art of the different nations is becoming more and more assimilated; in everyday occupations, in things that concern the mind, too, the spirit of nationality and the influence of tradition is receding.” — Boito (1873), quoted by the author p. 15

In this edited volume on exhibitions in marginal cities, Vienna 1873 serves as a benchmark rather than a case study. Filipová's introduction describes it as an Austria-only event whose secondary aim was to confirm a common state identity and present Vienna as a modern capital of the monarchy. Miklós Székely shows how Hungary used the fair to appear as a sovereign country, with a state forestry pavilion and an ethnographic village in which peasant houses represented the monarchy's peoples, presenting Hungary as multi-ethnic. Muñoz Torreblanca compares Barcelona's visitor and day figures with Vienna's, Syrjämaa argues that Helsinki's 1876 central rotunda drew on the Vienna pavilion, and Neale notes Melbourne's local exhibitions that came before Vienna.

The 1873 Vienna World's Fair (German: Weltausstellung 1873 Wien) was a large world exposition that was held from 1 May to 31 October 1873 in the Austria-Hungarian capital Vienna. Its motto was "Culture and Education" (German: Kultur und Erziehung).
