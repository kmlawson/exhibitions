San Francisco · 1915

Panama-Pacific International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Panama-Pacific International Exposition opened in San Francisco on 20 February 1915 and closed on 4 December that year (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 216). It celebrated the opening of the Panama Canal and the city's recovery from the 1906 earthquake and fire (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 219). It drew about 18.9 million admissions and, unlike most world's fairs, made a profit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221). Historians use it to study civic promotion, eugenics, colonial and ethnic display, and Asian self-representation at Western fairs (Rydell 1984; Peterson 2020p. 39).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of a San Francisco proposal made before the 1906 earthquake. In 1904 Reuben Brooke Hale proposed an international exhibition to celebrate the Panama Canal (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89). The earthquake and fire destroyed 28,000 buildings, and within six months a company was formed to hold a world's fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89). Benedict's entries say the fair's purpose then became showing the world that the city had recovered (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 219). Rydell says the fair became the rallying point for rebuilding (Rydell 1984).

Dobkin reads the fair as a booster project, one that promotes a city's growth and standing. In Dobkin's account the fair was an advertisement for the city, and after the fire the reform campaign for Burnham's plan collapsed (Benedict 1983p. 67).

Organisation and finance

Charles C. Moore, an engineer, headed the exposition (Rydell 1984). In 1906 Hale and Julius Kahn introduced a bill for $5 million. Once New Orleans bid for a 1915 fair, Congress wanted about $7.5 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89; Allwood et al. 2001p. 91). Federal support was voted in February 1911 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and President Taft signed the Act of National Recognition on 15 February 1911 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 91).

Private money came first. On 28 April 1910 businessmen raised over $4 million in two hours, and municipal and state bonds of $5 million each guaranteed the rest (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 219). Labour was won by a deal. The labour leader P. H. McCarthy persuaded unions to buy stock in return for union labour on the excavations, while the grounds accepted open-shop conditions, meaning workplaces that did not require union membership (Rydell 1984). Franklin D. Roosevelt had been a commissioner of the fair (Kargon et al. 2015p. 61).

Site and architecture

The site was reclaimed land on the south bank of the bay, with some private property and part of the Fort Mason lands (Allwood et al. 2001p. 91). Golden Gate Park was first favoured, and Taft broke ground there in 1911, but an undeveloped bay site was chosen instead (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 220). Dredging created about 70 acres of solid ground (Maloney 2012p. 137).

Burnham's plans for a remodelled city were abandoned by 1912 as too expensive and impractical (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 216). An architectural commission headed by George Kelham gave the main buildings a harmonious design (Allwood et al. 2001p. 91). Jules Guerin's pastel palette broke with the white of the Chicago fair of 1893 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 91). Greenhalgh places the fair among the two most aesthetically resolved American exhibitions, in a classicism tinged with romantic nostalgia (Greenhalgh 1988p. 46).

Courts designed by different architects surrounded the 432-foot Tower of Jewels, the most visible feature (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 220). The encyclopedia gives the same height (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 217), but Benedict's book gives 435 feet in the preface and 450 feet in the essay (Benedict 1983; Benedict 1983p. 13). The fair relied on indirect lighting, with more than 370 searchlights and 500 projectors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 218). John McLaren, appointed to the architectural commission in 1912, imported 70,000 rhododendrons and moved mature trees from gardens and cemeteries (Maloney 2012p. 138). The courts were planted as garden rooms, a first at a world's fair (Maloney 2012p. 141).

Exhibits and attractions

The amusement area east of the main palaces was called the Joy Zone (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 221). Its most important exhibit was a working model of the Panama Canal, which covered nearly five acres. Visitors rode a moving platform while telephone receivers played recorded lectures (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 225).

The Transportation Pavilion ran a working Model-T assembly line, which introduced millions of visitors to mass production (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 181). The sources differ on its output: eighteen to twenty-five cars a day (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 178), and 4,400 cars over the run of the fair (Samuel 2007). Sobe and Rackers describe a giant Underwood typewriter, 21 feet wide and 15 feet high (Lawn 2009p. 97). Fairs of this period increasingly showed processes rather than only products (Lawn 2009p. 99). Over a million feet of film was shown in sixty cinemas (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 219).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle say the Joy Zone continued the commercialisation of sex begun at the Chicago fair of 1893 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A 90-foot effigy of a suffragette stood in the zone (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 219).

The Palace of Fine Arts held over 11,400 works, two-thirds of them from the United States (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 223). Benedict's book says art was limited to work made in the previous ten years (Benedict 1983p. 19). The most famous sculpture, James Earle Fraser's The End of the Trail, showed an exhausted Native American on horseback, which Benedict's entry calls sentimental (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 218). Greenhalgh places it beside Solon Borglum's The Pioneer (Greenhalgh 1988p. 128).

Nations and colonies on show

Germany and Britain agreed not to build national pavilions, though manufacturers from both countries exhibited (Benedict 1983p. 23). France built a replica of the Palace of the Legion of Honor (Benedict 1983p. 23). Its pavilion went up in nine weeks, with exhibits brought on the naval collier Jason (Allwood et al. 2001p. 93). The texts differ on the number of foreign nations: twenty-eight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 215), and pavilions of twenty-four nations on the Avenue of the Nations (Allwood et al. 2001p. 93).

Spain refused to pay for an official exhibit, so Spanish artists who came alone were placed in the international section (Boone 2019p. 164). Boone argues that the fair treated Spain as a remote past, blending its architecture into a generalised Mediterranean style (Boone 2019p. 164). Equestrian statues of Cortés and Pizarro stood at the base of the Tower of Jewels (Boone 2019p. 164). The Asian pavilions are covered in the Asia section.

Peoples on display

The Zone put living people on show. Its ethnic villages included Samoans, Hawaiians, Mexicans, Maoris, Egyptians, Filipinos, Japanese and Native Americans (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221). Rydell argues that the villages turned racial stereotypes into lessons of progress, and that the African village was deported by immigration officials within weeks (Rydell 1984).

Visitors, eugenics and controversy

Attendance was 18,876,438 admissions. The encyclopedia says these do not represent separate people, since most visitors came more than once (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 216). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the same figure (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Other texts give 18.8 or nearly 19 million (Roche 2000p. 43; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger's appendix gives a lower figure, 13,127,103 (Badger 1979p. 131). The fair paid off its mortgage by 30 August 1915 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221). The two reference works give profits of $2,401,911 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221) and $2,401,931 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 225), which differ by $20. Most texts give 4 December as the closing date, but Dobkin gives 5 December (Benedict 1983p. 67).

The fair was where American eugenicists first became active in exhibition culture (Rydell 1993p. 39). Eugenics, the movement to improve a population by controlling who breeds, was promoted explicitly there (Roche 2000p. 80). The Race Betterment Foundation ran a booth in the Palace of Education with charts, portraits and vibrating chairs (Rydell 1993p. 41). The directors set aside a week for the Second National Conference on Race Betterment (Rydell 1993p. 40), whose sessions drew 1,200 to 1,500 people each (Rydell 1993p. 42). Rydell writes that "In the context of the fair, eugenicists had become showmen" (Rydell 1993p. 41).

The women's programme received $104,000, but the money came too late for a women's building, so the women built a monument to California's pioneer mothers (Weimann 1981p. 598). Greenhalgh says women's sections at fairs were taken over by anti-suffrage groups, as at San Francisco in 1915 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). The Japanese and Chinese governments exhibited anyway, despite their opposition to California's 1913 anti-alien land law (Benedict 1983p. 76).

How historians have interpreted it

Benedict, who wrote the encyclopedia and dictionary entries and edited the 1983 book, reads world's fairs as rituals of competition, comparable to the potlatch, a Northwest Coast ceremony of rival gift-giving (Benedict 1983). Benedict calls the fair the last nineteenth-century world's fair, with the Joy Zone set apart from the main fair (Benedict 1983p. 60). Benedict's entry calls it "the last collective outburst of this sort of naive optimism" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221).

Dobkin reads a booster project in a post-earthquake city, which swapped Burnham's civic ambitions for a temporary fairyland that stressed the Zone as a segregated part of the city (Benedict 1983p. 67). Brechin reads the architecture, colour and lighting as a Byzantine fantasy of permanence that could be sustained for only nine months (Benedict 1983p. 95). Armstrong reads the sculpture as Hercules-style symbolism and as Social Darwinist imagery of racial and masculine conquest, meaning the application of evolutionary competition to human races (Benedict 1983p. 115). Starr argues that the fair's allegories asked visitors to decode edifying meanings, and that its most effective parts were those enjoyed directly (Benedict 1983p. 136).

Rydell reads the fair as civic boosterism and racial ideology, and argues that the directors leaned on eugenicists more than on anthropologists (Rydell 1984). In a later survey, Rydell argues that fair managers and eugenicists shared a plan to make America modern (Rydell 1993p. 42). Rydell, Findling and Pelle treat the Joy Zone as the commercial side of the fair, which relied on static exhibits and midway concessions (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Peterson reads the Asian displays as the start of Asian nations controlling their own image at Western fairs (Peterson 2020p. 39). Fernsebner reads the Chinese protests as the work of a transnational public (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 190), while Rydell reads the official Chinese exhibits as presenting China as a land ripe for American investment (Rydell 1984).

Boone argues that the fair used Spain as a remote past in order to ignore Mexicans (Boone 2019p. 164). Roche says the eugenic movement promoted its doctrines explicitly at the fair (Roche 2000p. 80). Kargon and colleagues see the fair as the point when major corporations began to show the processes of production (Kargon et al. 2015p. 63). Allwood, Allan and Reid treat it as an organisational triumph and are concerned with the planned event rather than its political meaning (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89).

The readings agree on the fair's origin in the 1906 disaster and its boosterism (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 219; Benedict 1983p. 67; Rydell 1984). They part company on how to read its spectacle. Benedict and Starr read it as ritual and allegory, Rydell and Armstrong as ideology, and Rydell, Findling and Pelle as commerce.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Peterson says Japan's official display had four parts: a government site, the Japan Beautiful concession in the Zone, art in the Palace of Fine Arts, and exhibits in nine palaces. Its footprint was nearly a fifth of the exhibition area, and the press reported plans to spend $1 million on its main display (Peterson 2020p. 49). Shigetō Dewa, an admiral, called the fair a Congress of International Peace (Peterson 2020p. 40). Peterson says the State Department told the fair's president to give the Japanese commissioners particular attention, and that the diplomacy was minutely stage-managed (Peterson 2020p. 46).

Japan Beautiful was a village built by Yumeto Kushibiki under a gilded Buddha (Peterson 2020p. 62). Peterson gives the Buddha's height as 109 feet (Peterson 2020p. 63); Rydell and the encyclopedia give 120 feet (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 219). Rydell reports that one official called the village a junk shop (Rydell 1984). The Japanese garden had more than 1,400 trees and 4,400 smaller plants shipped from Japan (Maloney 2012p. 150). When the fair closed, the Imperial Japanese Government gave lacquered mannequins from a silk-making exhibit to the University of California (Benedict 1983).

China

The Chinese Republic's pavilion opened in March 1915 with a model of the Qing Taihe Palace (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 184). Commissioner-general Chen Qi set China beside the United States as a parallel Pacific nation (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 183). Chinese displays filled a block of the Palace of Varied Industries (Peterson 2020p. 101), and nine provincial delegates stayed on through the fair (Peterson 2020p. 101). Zhang Jian's preface to a businessmen's report argued that both countries were sites for material exploitation and foreign investment (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 185).

The Six Companies, the Chinese community's association, and Chinese-language newspapers attacked the pavilion and a commercial teahouse. Chen Qi replied that the teahouse had been set up to recover the tea trade (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 186; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 186).

Underground Chinatown, a sub-concession in the Chinese Village run by Sid and David Grauman, showed opium users and gambling in wax (Peterson 2020p. 108). Chen Qi called it a "disgrace to the Exposition and a slander on the Chinese people" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 188). Guards closed it on 26 March 1915, and a modified Underground Slumming replaced it (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 189). Benedict says the name was changed and the Chinese characters removed (Benedict 1983p. 51).

Other Asian peoples

Siam had a palace among the pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 221). Filipinos were among the living villagers on the Zone (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221). Hotta-Lister describes a 1915 San Francisco and San Diego Exhibition, where a Chinese village's opium-den display was closed after protest while the Japanese village stayed open (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 142). That text does not name the Panama-Pacific fair, so linking it to this fair is an assumption.

Interpretations

Peterson treats the fair as the start of Asian nations controlling their own image at Western fairs, and writes that America "seemed to want Japan to represent itself as it wanted to see it" (Peterson 2020p. 76). Fernsebner reads the protests as the work of a transnational Chinese public (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 190). The texts say little about how Asian visitors reacted beyond the protests and official verdicts.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commemoration and renewal — the fair marked the canal and San Francisco's recovery from 1906, and Benedict's entry reads it as an assertion of California and the American West (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 221; Rydell 1984).
  • Eugenics and race betterment — the congresses, the booth and the pageant made race improvement a public show (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1993p. 41).
  • Asia and empire on display — national pavilions and Zone villages staged racial hierarchies, and Asian governments and communities contested them (Peterson 2020p. 39; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 188).
  • Process and industry — the Ford line and the typewriter showed how things were made, not only what was made (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 181; Lawn 2009p. 99).
  • Gender and suffrage — the women's programme had no building of its own, and women's sections were taken over by anti-suffrage groups (Weimann 1981p. 598; Greenhalgh 1988p. 183).
  • Spanish heritage — Spain's past was used in place of the present-day Mexican presence (Boone 2019p. 164).

Questions for study

  1. Does the ritual-competition model Benedict proposes explain the racial and colonial displays, or does it hide the power relations that Armstrong and Boone describe?
  2. Were the protests against Underground Chinatown about China's national image, as Fernsebner argues, or about the standing of San Francisco's Chinese community, as Peterson argues?
  3. Allwood, Allan and Reid stress the fair's planned architecture, while Benedict and Rydell focus on the Zone. Which side of the fair should a history of it put at the centre, and why?
  4. Why did the fair's managers give eugenicists a platform, and what did each side gain from the arrangement?
  5. The sources disagree on basic figures such as profit, tower height and closing date. How should a student weigh a reference work against a specialised monograph when they conflict?

Peterson devotes its first two chapters to Japan and China at the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition, which it treats as the start of Asian self-representation at Western fairs. For Japan, Peterson argues that a practised 'master of the form' presented itself to the West as a modern, civilising partner and as a pre-modern, artistic and feminine culture, while its diplomacy with the American hosts was carefully staged. For China, the author shows the new Republic presenting a long civilisation and a modern economy, but argues that the most consequential episode was the Underground Chinatown attraction in the Zone, which exposed how the local Chinese community's position in San Francisco shaped the nation's image. The fair is the opening case for the book's wider claim that Asian nations could increasingly control their own representation at expositions.

Substantial discussion 6

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
section 'The Louisiana Purchase Exposition & Two Great Fires' within the '1904-23' chapter; printed headers pp. 86-94 seen; fair-statistics table at l. 10136 (page not seen)

The authors treat the San Francisco exposition mainly as an organisational triumph over earthquake, fire, war and depression, tracing it from Reuben Brooke Hale's 1904 proposal and the 1906 rebuilding to the 1911 Act of National Recognition. They stress its unusually deliberate design: a homogeneous main group of palaces, planned by an Architectural Commission, and a colour scheme by Jules Guerin that broke with the white of the Chicago fair. The survey also records the war's effect on foreign participation, including the French pavilion, and the fair's place in the lineage of later fairs. Its concern is the fair as a planned event rather than an argument about its political meaning.

Fernsebner's chapter treats China's participation in San Francisco as a contest over who could speak for 'China'. She shows the Republic's commissioners, led by Chen Qi, presenting China as a modern market and as the Pacific partner of the United States, with the Chinese pavilion opened in March 1915 and displays of transport and commerce. Her main argument is that the fair became a focus of protest across a transnational Chinese public: the Six Companies, Chinese-language newspapers and the Chinese consul fought the Zone's Underground Chinatown and the teahouse at the China Pavilion, and the attraction's closure on 26 March 1915 shows how economic nationalism linked local and global stakes. She reads the episode as evidence of a broader, diverse community invested in images of China.

Wikipedia

The Panama–Pacific International Exposition was a world's fair held in San Francisco, California, United States, from February 20 to December 4, 1915. Its stated purpose was to celebrate the completion of the Panama Canal, but it was widely seen in the city as an opportunity to showcase its recovery from the 1906 earthquake. The fair was constructed on a 636-acre (257-hectare) site along the northern shore, between the Presidio and Fort Mason, now known as the Marina District.
