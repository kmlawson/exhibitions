New Orleans · 1884–1885

World's Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition (New Orleans)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The World’s Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition opened at New Orleans on 16 December 1884 and closed on 1 June 1885 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 81; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 88). It marked the centennial of America’s first cotton export and was meant to show the South as a centre of industry and trade (Maloney 2012p. 41; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86). Against a forecast of four million visitors it drew 1,158,840, and it ended with a debt of nearly $470,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). The texts use it to study the New South, race in exhibitions and horticulture (Rydell 1984; Maloney 2012p. 41).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The scheme began outside New Orleans. In an 1880 letter to the New York Herald, the economist Edward Atkinson proposed a cotton centennial in New York in 1884 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86). The Atlanta cotton exhibition of 1881 seemed to Morehead, Richardson and Burke to have wasted that idea, so they campaigned for New Orleans instead (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86). Congress gave the project legal form on 10 February 1883, under the joint auspices of the federal government, the National Cotton Planters’ Association and the city (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 80). The promoters belonged to the New South movement, which in the closing decades of the nineteenth century urged the region to set sectional quarrels aside and turn to commerce and industry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86). Pfeffer adds that Henry W. Grady’s New South concept shaped the promoters, who hoped northern money would follow reconciliation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 80).

Organisation and finance

Edmund Richardson presided over a thirteen-member board of management, Samuel Mullen was its secretary and E. A. Burke was director-general (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87). Federal support took the form of a congressional loan of $1,000,000, a $300,000 appropriation for the government display and $15,000 for a Woman’s Department. The state added $100,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 80). Hardy suggests the money probably contributed to the failure, since the plans were then enlarged beyond what it could cover (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87). By February 1885 the director-general reported liabilities of $369,422.95 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Pfeffer records a final grant of $350,000, with the rule that only the Treasury Secretary could pay bills, and only to non-residents (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Hardy gives the March 1885 appropriation as $335,000, made as a gift rather than a loan (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89); Pfeffer’s $350,000 includes the $15,000 for the Woman’s Department (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Rydell (1984) says a federal loan that bailed out the fair was still unpaid in 1911 (Rydell 1984). The texts do not settle which federal money was a loan. Hardy’s four federal sums add to $1,650,000, the figure Badger tabulates for New Orleans (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87; Badger 1979p. 132).

Site and buildings

The board selected 247 acres four miles upriver from the central business district, between St. Charles Avenue and the Mississippi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 80). Maloney gives 249 acres of former wilderness, pasture and marsh (Maloney 2012p. 42). The city had bought the land in 1871, and an 1883 ordinance granted its use to the exposition corporation, with the understanding that a Horticultural Hall would be built (Maloney 2012p. 42). G. M. Torgerson, a Swedish architect, designed most buildings in a Victorian eclectic style (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 88). Maloney says the layout appeared to reflect military expediency and engineering precision more than aesthetic aims (Maloney 2012p. 42). The Main Building covered over 33 acres, with only a Music Hall, seating 11,000, as its major interior space (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 80). The city paid $100,000 for Horticultural Hall, a predominantly glass building (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 81).

Exhibits and attractions

Most domestic exhibits came from private firms, and many foreign governments did not take part officially (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 88). Mexico was the exception. Pfeffer says Mexico had the largest presence of the twenty-one foreign exhibitors, and that its Mining Pavilion was the most admired structure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 81). Hardy adds that Mexico’s participation was official and extensive, with two major structures and a military band and cavalry squadron (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Maloney describes a Mexican outdoor garden with a fountain 60 feet in circumference, and cacti that introduced thousands of northern visitors to the plant (Maloney 2012p. 45). The Mississippi Valley Horticultural Society ran the horticultural department, and at its February 1885 meeting on the grounds it renamed itself the American Horticultural Society (Maloney 2012p. 43). Davis reports that several theatres offered exposition season packages, and that the Kiralfy Brothers, touring producers of large spectacles, drew crowds (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 340). Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show suffered rain and a streetcar strike, yet the Picayune wrote that “Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show is an exposition in itself” (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 340).

Peoples on display

Southern fairs gave separate displays for African Americans, called Negro departments (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The fair’s Colored Department, which Pfeffer calls the first-ever at this fair, showed the achievements of African Americans, who had been shut out of the 1876 Centennial (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Pfeffer reads it as demonstrating the New South’s racial accommodation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Rydell reads the same display differently: “At the New Orleans exposition, survival and resistance were explicitly joined” (Rydell 1984). Rydell also reports that Bishop Henry A. Turner praised the directors for welcoming black exhibitors (Rydell 1984), and that the department held sixteen thousand exhibits (Rydell 1984). Burke presented the department as a way to bring black Americans into farm and factory work; Rydell says only the directors wanted to confine them to fields and shops (Rydell 1984). The Woman’s Department was led by Julia Ward Howe, to the resentment of local women (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83). Pfeffer says it strengthened claims of sectional reconciliation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Weimann argues that women’s work could look trivial in such displays, and that the Woman’s Building planned for Chicago had to differ from the earlier women’s exhibits (Weimann 1981p. 51). The Smithsonian’s ethnological exhibit, planned by Otis T. Mason in 1884, was to set apes and human crania in a progressive series and crania of delinquent individuals beside them (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the exhibit, mostly busts of Indian chiefs and North American artefacts, was never far from a scientific rationale for government Indian policy (Rydell 1984).

Visitors, failure and aftermath

Exhibitors complained of disorganised allotment of space (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82). Bad weather, unfinished buildings and a remote site hurt attendance, Rydell, Findling and Pelle say, with losses estimated at $470,000 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Hardy is the most direct: “Measured against those promises, the exposition was a failure” (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Maloney calls the fair “hastily assembled and poorly financed” (Maloney 2012p. 41). Allwood, Allan and Reid give a deficit of $250,000 and a table figure of 3,525,000 visitors [50, l. 2888; l. 10068-10069], which conflicts with the 1,158,840 given by Pfeffer and Hardy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Maloney says a company headed by George Pullman bought the property for $175,000 and planned to reopen it in November 1885 (Maloney 2012p. 51). Allwood, Allan and Reid credit a group of patriotic New Orleans citizens with raising that sum (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). The reopened fair failed too (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83). The grounds became Audubon Park, renamed in 1886 (Maloney 2012p. 51). Hardy identifies the lasting result as the city’s tourist industry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Pfeffer ties the tourism to press attention on the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83).

How historians have interpreted it

Hardy and Pfeffer agree that the fair failed by its own promises, and both find an indirect gain: for Hardy, self-awareness and tourism (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89); for Pfeffer, tourism and some emigration to the South (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83). Pfeffer credits Hardy’s 1964 Tulane thesis with rediscovering the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84). Maloney reads the fair horticulturally: despite its financial disappointment, it offered significant horticultural milestones and a green space that became a park (Maloney 2012p. 41). Rydell (1984) reads it through race: the southern fairs gave black Americans a role in the drive for new markets, and Negro departments served as instruments of social control (Rydell 1984). Pfeffer’s accommodation reading and Rydell’s reading of resistance differ over the same display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82; Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle (2000) add that the southern fairs used Negro departments to show black people as industrial workers, and that the Smithsonian displays there carried a theory of racial hierarchy (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Davis reads the fair through its entertainments, arguing that exposition culture and popular theatre were porous to each other (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 335), and that critics saw the Kiralfy shows and the Wild West as parts of the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 340). Davis also notes that histories of American fairs usually give this one only a passing mention (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 339). Allwood, Allan and Reid place it among the exhibitions of the 1880s, which they call an epidemic (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). Badger counts it among post-Centennial fairs of purely local significance (Badger 1979p. 21). Hoffenberg reads Victoria’s displays there as part of an argument about Australian and Indian trade policy (Hoffenberg 2001p. 121). The texts agree that the fair failed commercially (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89; Maloney 2012p. 41). They differ on the Colored Department, on whether the 1885 money was a gift or a loan (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89; Rydell 1984), on who bought the grounds (Maloney 2012p. 51; Allwood et al. 2001p. 55), and on the number of foreign exhibitors, given as seventeen countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55) and twenty-one exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 81).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan’s presence is the best documented of the Asian displays. Hotta-Lister calls Japan’s appearance at the fair small, and says that at New Orleans the Japanese government seems to have changed its emphasis, featuring educational exhibits such as universities, scientific apparatus and the publishing industry (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 211). Hotta-Lister names the Japanese government as the body that shaped the presentation. Lafcadio Hearn, then working as a journalist in New Orleans, gave extensive coverage of these educational exhibits in The East at New Orleans (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 211). Hardy lists Japan among the non-European exhibitors, and says that Japan and Mexico were the best of them in completeness and quality, while most non-European exhibits were native artefacts and natural resources (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Maloney reports that Japan was well represented among the manufacturing and industry exhibits but did not create an attention-getting garden, as it had at the Centennial (Maloney 2012p. 53). Hotta-Lister’s appendix dates the New Orleans exhibition to 1884 and gives a Japanese expenditure of 15,500 yen, which conflicts with the 1885 date in the text (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221).

China and Siam

Hardy lists China and Siam among the non-European exhibitors, without describing what either showed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 89). Rydell (1984) includes a photograph captioned as a Chinese exhibit at New Orleans (Rydell 1984). Earll, who installed the Smithsonian’s display, reported that many exhibits had yielded material for the museum, but China was the exception (Rydell 1984). The Chinese collection went to the University of Michigan, to Earll’s chagrin (Rydell 1984). The texts do not say who organised or paid for the Chinese or Siamese displays.

South Asia and the wider region

The texts describe no display from South Asia at this fair. Hoffenberg links the Victorian exhibit to arguments about Indian trade policy, but describes no Indian display there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 121). Entertainment is a separate matter. The Kiralfy spectacle Around the World in Eighty Days played in New Orleans during the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 340). In its Philadelphia run it had used scenes of Calcutta, Borneo and Egypt (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 339). The texts do not say whether the New Orleans version kept those scenes.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Federal money and the deficit — Congress lent $1,000,000 and voted $300,000 for federal exhibits, and the fair closed with a debt that Hardy and Pfeffer put near $470,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83). Pfeffer’s $350,000 final grant includes the $15,000 that Hardy lists separately (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 87).
  • The New South — promoters meant the fair to show that the South was reconciled with the Union (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 79), and Rydell (1984) treats the southern fairs as a way of tying race to the drive for markets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 86; Rydell 1984).
  • The Colored Department — Pfeffer calls it the first-ever at this fair and reads it as racial accommodation; Rydell reads it as a site of survival and resistance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 82; Rydell 1984).
  • Women’s departments — Howe’s Woman’s Department and the women’s exhibits that later fairs sought to differ from (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 83; Weimann 1981p. 51; Benedict 1983p. 40).
  • Horticulture and landscape — the horticultural hall, cacti and fruit displays, and the park that became Audubon Park (Maloney 2012p. 41; Maloney 2012p. 51).
  • Spectacle and entertainment — theatre and the Wild West as parts of the fair’s experience (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 340).
  • Smithsonian ethnology — the planned exhibit of apes, crania and Indian busts (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
chapter 2, 'North Meets South in a Winter Garden' (heading 'World's Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition, New Orleans, 1884-1885'), pp. 39-53

Maloney, whose book studies world's fair gardens, treats the 1884-85 New Orleans fair as a case study in horticulture. Maloney argues that the fair was a financial disappointment that nonetheless offered significant horticultural milestones: a permanent Horticultural Hall, a new glazing method, a fruit display and a much-noticed cactus collection, and a green space that became Audubon Park. The chapter sets the fair beside the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876 and, briefly, the Chicago fair of 1893, and the new park is the legacy it stresses most.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
entry 'New Orleans 1884-1885' by Miki Pfeffer, printed pp. 79-84; the entry ends with a bibliographical essay

Pfeffer's encyclopedia entry places the fair within Henry W. Grady's New South idea and reports that its finances were strained throughout, leaving it a failure by its own promoters' measure. It treats the first Colored Department and the Woman's Department as the fair's most notable features, and reads them as displays of the New South's 'racial accommodation' and of 'sectional reconciliation'. The entry's closing argument is that the fair's few gains were indirect: sustained press attention led to later tourism and some emigration to the South. The author states these points in factual prose and gives little extended interpretation.

Moderate discussion 2

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
chapter 6.1, 'The Theatre Culture of United States', by Robert Davis, in the chapter 'International Expositions, 1876-1893'; New Orleans section printed pp. 338-344 (page markers partly visible)

Davis treats the New Orleans fair as one of three case studies, with Philadelphia 1876 and Chicago 1893, in how exposition entertainment and popular theatre fed each other. Davis argues that the New Orleans theatre community, unlike Philadelphia's, set itself up as part of the fair, and that critics saw the Kiralfy Brothers' spectacles and Buffalo Bill's Wild West as parts of the exposition experience. The wider argument is that the expositions' rhetoric of education and study was porous to popular amusement.

The World Cotton Centennial (also known as the World's Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition) was a World's Fair held in New Orleans, Louisiana, United States, in 1884–1885. At a time when nearly one third of all cotton produced in the United States was handled in New Orleans and the city was home to the New Orleans Cotton Exchange, the idea for the fair was first advanced by the Cotton Planters Association. The name "World Cotton Centennial" referred to the earliest-surviving record of export of a shipment of cotton from the U.S. to England in 1784. Gustave M. Torgerson who lived in Meridian was selected and supervising architect and designed the main building.
