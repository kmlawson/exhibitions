Dunedin · 1865

New Zealand Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The New Zealand Exhibition opened in Dunedin on 12 January 1865, while its building was still unfinished, and was the first major international exhibition in Australasia (Filipová 2015p. 231; Filipová 2015p. 235; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Commissioners planned it in 1863 to show the colony's natural wealth (Filipová 2015p. 235). John Hyde Harris, Superintendent of the Otago Province, opened it (Filipová 2015p. 235). The texts present it as New Zealand's first world's fair and as the start of Dunedin's art infrastructure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Filipová 2015p. 240).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The commissioners' first planning meeting, in 1863, proposed to show the colony's natural wealth and the adaptability of its soil and climate, and so to increase the confidence of its inhabitants in their resources (Filipová 2015p. 235). Rice reads the emphasis as lying on local industry and internal relations rather than external trade (Filipová 2015p. 235). The organisers ordered the catalogues of the London exhibitions of 1851 and 1862 and of the South Kensington Museum to guide their planning (Filipová 2015p. 235). James Hector toured the colony in 1864 to gather information on exhibitors and exhibits, and his report helped determine the scope of the show (Filipová 2015p. 235).

McCarthy's entry gives a harsher reading. It says the rationale was scientific to a degree but mainly a practical example of extractive economics and colonial boosterism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35).

The texts give the fair several names and scales. Rice calls it the New Zealand Exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 231). Hoffenberg and the Allwood table use New Zealand International Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 46; Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). The dictionary's appendix gives New Zealand Exposition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 396). The dictionary's 1889 entry calls the earlier colonial fairs small international exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117), and Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the Dunedin show as one of many small exhibitions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 30).

Organisers and finance

The officials named in the encyclopedia were John Hyde Harris, President of the Executive, James Hector, Commissioner and Juror, Alfred Eccles, Secretary, and Thomas Forrester, Superintendent (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 418). Eccles later recalled that the commissioners had tried to stimulate a love of art as well as an interest in industry and science (Filipová 2015p. 236).

McCarthy says the fair was funded partly by the general government in Wellington and partly by the Otago provincial government (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Its building was a large stone structure designed by the local architects Mason and Clayton, which McCarthy calls the most impressive structure yet built in Dunedin (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The fair opened unfinished because of construction delays, and its financial status was in doubt (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Governor Sir George Grey did not attend, partly because of the wars between the Crown and Maori tribes on the North Island (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35).

Exhibits and the fine arts

The fair was arranged in 38 classes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The first display in a prominent position in the main building was a large gilded obelisk representing the £6.7 million of gold exported from the region (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). A portion of the exhibits went to the Colonial Museum in Wellington and others to the Otago Museum at Otago University (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35).

The fine arts were housed in an annexe at the rear of the building, beside the refreshments area. Most of the loaned works came from Otago collections and were mainly by British artists (Filipová 2015p. 236). Rice notes that the catalogue named the lenders, and that this public confirmation of cultural capital helped define class in colonial society (Filipová 2015p. 236). The fine-art display held 412 works, and the Otago Daily Times reviewer described only 119 of them (Filipová 2015p. 237). McCarthy names landscapes by William Mathew Hodgkins, John Gully, J. C. Richmond, Charles Heaphy, William Fox and C. D. Barraud (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 36). Rice notes that Fox treated himself and Richmond as amateurs, but defined Gully and Barraud as professionals, although both had other jobs (Filipová 2015p. 238).

Visitors and reception

None of the passages gives an attendance figure for 1865, and the Allwood table row for the fair has no figures (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). Reports on the opening said the exhibition was "confessedly very incomplete" but that the display was much better than could have been anticipated (Filipová 2015p. 235). Reviews were largely congratulatory (Filipová 2015p. 231). Rambler, the reviewer whom Rice identifies as William Fox (Filipová 2015p. 238), wrote that the fine arts were the department in which failure might be predicted in a new colony (Filipová 2015p. 236), and then found the display creditable to the colony's artistic talent and taste (Filipová 2015p. 236).

Empire and colony

The colony had been founded as a British colony in 1840, and European settlement of Otago province began in 1848 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The general government and the Otago provincial government funded the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The entry lists exhibits from the New Zealand provinces, from Fiji and the colonies of Australia, and from Canada, America, France, Belgium, Holland, Germany and Austria (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). It says the exhibits from Great Britain included a large Indian court (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Rice reports that Tasmania put on a very good show and that Britain sent local as well as imperial exhibits (Filipová 2015p. 235).

A section for Maori and other aboriginal manufactures and implements held Maori material culture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The entry says weaving and carving were described as useful and skilfully made, and as evidence of Maori intelligence and adaptability (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The catalogue listed weapons, ceremonial objects, carving and weaving exhibited by Maori, and the entry says Maori material culture was not seen at the time in purely ethnographic terms (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). The organisers, the entry says, wanted Maori people to be included as exhibitors and visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Bell finds that the fine-art section held only two Maori subjects among its 412 works, and that Maori representation played a small part in New Zealand art in the 1860s (Bell 1992p. 99). None of the passages mentions a native village or a display of living people.

The organisers declared that a country in which they meant to erect another England must contain the natural elements needed for commercial and manufacturing prosperity (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Harris hoped that the fair would promote a generous emulation between the provinces and colonies (Filipová 2015p. 235). Rice's reading is that the fair aimed at development at home more than at trade abroad (Filipová 2015p. 235). Hoffenberg's account concerns India's contribution and is given in the Asia section below.

Aftermath and legacy

The closing report predicted that the exhibition would leave a lasting mark on industrial art in New Zealand (Filipová 2015p. 240). Rice argues that its impact is hard to measure, but that the positive reviews of colonial fine art suggest it gave confidence for building an art infrastructure (Filipová 2015p. 240). She lists an art exhibition organised in Dunedin in 1869, the Otago Society of Arts in 1876, New Zealand's first school of art in Dunedin in 1870, and a public gallery opened in 1884 in a space in the Otago Museum (Filipová 2015p. 240; Filipová 2015p. 240; Filipová 2015p. 240; Filipová 2015p. 240). Hector was appointed director of the Colonial Museum and Geological Survey in Wellington later in 1865 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). In 1889 some works from the 1865 fine-art display, such as Heaphy's sketches of Rangitoto Island, were shown in the Early History, Maori and South Seas Court for their historical interest (Filipová 2015p. 244).

How historians have interpreted it

Rice argues that the 1865 exhibition gave a boost to the development of arts infrastructure in New Zealand (Filipová 2015p. 232). She reads the fine-art displays as evidence of culture and civilisation, offered to colonists whose inferiority was debated (Filipová 2015p. 234). McCarthy reads the fair as colonial boosterism of an extractive kind (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Filipová's introduction sees exhibitions on the geographical margins as showing the ambiguities of colonial culture, combining a desire to emulate metropolitan models with an attempt to assert the colony's uniqueness (Filipová 2015p. 12). Roche, citing MacKenzie, treats colonial exhibitions as a rite of passage for responsible government, held after economic advance (Roche 2000p. 58). Roche does not discuss the Dunedin fair beyond listing it.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The entry names a large Indian court among the exhibits from Great Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35). Hoffenberg writes that John Forbes Watson, who directed the Indian Department at the London exhibition of 1862, later forwarded similar goods, which he calls Raw Products and Manufactures, to the New Zealand International Exhibition from his London office (Hoffenberg 2001p. 46; Hoffenberg 2001p. 46). The text does not say that these goods were Indian. Watson's biography lists him as commissioner and editor of official catalogues for India at the 1865 New Zealand exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285). The passages record no Japanese, Chinese, Korean or Southeast Asian presence at the 1865 fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial art and cultural standing — Rice treats the fine arts as evidence of culture and civilisation and as the basis of a local art infrastructure (Filipová 2015p. 234; Filipová 2015p. 240).
  • Progress and extraction — McCarthy reads the fair as practical colonial boosterism of an extractive kind (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35).
  • Indigenous material culture and representation — Maori objects were shown as skilled work, yet the fine-art section held few Maori subjects (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35; Bell 1992p. 99).
  • Imperial exchange — Watson's London office supplied goods to a colonial show, and Hoffenberg treats such exchanges as challenging core-periphery distinctions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 46; Hoffenberg 2001p. 47).
  • Colonial exhibitions as a type — Roche places the 1865 fair among British colonial exhibitions, with the dominion rite-of-passage argument (Roche 2000p. 58).

Questions for study

  1. Rice calls the 1865 fair the first major international exhibition in Australasia, but also says the first international exhibition in Australasia came at Sydney in 1870. How should a student reconcile these two statements? (Filipová 2015p. 231; Filipová 2015p. 235)
  2. McCarthy says Maori material culture was not seen in purely ethnographic terms, while Bell finds few Maori subjects in the art. What does each source show about how Maori were represented at the fair? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35; Bell 1992p. 99)
  3. Hoffenberg says Watson sent similar goods from London. What evidence would show whether they came from India, from the colonies, or from both? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 46)
  4. Roche's rite-of-passage argument presents colonial exhibitions as a sign of maturity. How well does this fit an exhibition that opened unfinished and whose finances were in doubt? (Roche 2000p. 58; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 35)

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

In the contents of this work

10A Red-Letter Day: Evaluating Progress in New Zealand’s Art at Dunedin’s International Exhibitions, 1865 and 1889Rebecca Ricep. 231

Rice compares the fine art sections of the two international exhibitions held in Dunedin, the New Zealand Exhibition of 1865 and the New Zealand and South Seas Exhibition of 1889-90, to ask what they show about progress in New Zealand art. Her evidence is mainly the critical reception. In 1865 reviewers, chiefly 'Rambler' (identified as William Fox), praised colonial watercolours as equal to the best work at home, and read the display as proof of colonial taste. By 1889 the verdicts were sharper: reviewers faulted New Zealand painters for relying on landscape and for having little genre or history painting. Rice explains the change through James Belich's idea of recolonization and through the new historical consciousness of 1889, shown in the Early History, Maori and South Seas Court and in a group of John Gully's works. She concludes that the 1865 exhibition helped build an art infrastructure, but that by 1889 the colonial moment was seen as past, which brought anxiety rather than triumph.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

ch. 3, 'Representations of Maori by Artists Active in New Zealand in the 1860s', running head at l. 4182 (printed page not visible in the passage)

Bell's chapter on European artists who represented Maori in New Zealand in the 1860s mentions the 1865 Dunedin exhibition in a few sentences. A painting, New Zealand Chief: Te Rangihaeata (1864), was shown in the Fine Arts section, and Bell counts it as one of only two Maori subjects among 412 fine-art exhibits, the other being William Fox's Oteriah Pah. About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Bell studies European paintings, watercolours, drawings and prints of Maori made for, exhibited in or published in Australasia and Britain, from the first organised settlement in New Zealand to the emergence of an independent nation. Its argument is that every image is a construction shaped by European conventions, patrons, racial theory and audiences, so it is better read as ideology than as a neutral record. The chapters follow artists and periods in turn: Angas and others in the 1840s and early 1850s, Gilfillan and Strutt, the artists active in New Zealand in the 1860s, the historical paintings of the 1890s and 1900s, Lindauer's pictures of customs and legend, and Dittmer's Te Tohunga of 1907. The conclusion considers later uses of these images, including their reuse by contemporary Maori artists and the 1987 Te Maori exhibition.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are slight in this book, which is about European images of Maori and the colonial settings in which they were made. No Asian display, participant or visitor is mentioned in any of the exhibitions discussed. Asia appears only as subject matter, in passing and mostly as a comparison for European painting: the Siamese twins among the Egyptian Hall's entertainments in London in the 1840s (chapter 1); the Orient of illustrated travel books (chapter 1); belles from Tahiti, the Philippines, India and China in Chevalier's 1872 London exhibition (chapter 3); Chinese heads among Hutchinson's sculptures shown at the A.S.A. in 1901 (chapter 5); and the European romanticisation of Africa, Asia and Oceania, to which Bell relates Dittmer's rhetoric (chapter 6). The Orientalism Bell discusses refers mainly to North Africa and the Middle East. South Asia enters only through the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London, where New Zealand exhibits were shown.

Hoffenberg's chapter on exhibition commissioners argues that men such as John Forbes Watson turned exhibitions into networks that circulated knowledge about the empire and its colonies. Watson, director of the Indian Department at London 1862, later sent what Hoffenberg calls 'similar' 'Raw Products and Manufactures' from his London office to the New Zealand International Exhibition, where they formed a permanent display at a local museum. The passage is brief and is about a metropolitan commissioner supplying a colonial show, not about the fair itself. The biographical appendix also lists Watson as a commissioner for the 1865 New Zealand fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Wikipedia

The New Zealand Exhibition, which took place in Dunedin, New Zealand, in 1865, was a world's fair visited by approximately 31250 people. It was the first world's fair held in New Zealand. It opened on 12 January and ran until 6 May 1865. Later world's fairs opened in Dunedin in 1889 and 1925.
