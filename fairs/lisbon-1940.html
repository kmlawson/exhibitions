Portuguese World Exhibition (Lisbon 1940)

Exposicao do Mundo Portugues
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposicao do Mundo Portugues (Portuguese World Exhibition) opened in Lisbon on 2 June 1940 and closed in December (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The idea came from Antonio Ferro, of the national secretariat of propaganda, and Captain Henrique Galvao organised it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Both reference entries say its purpose was to glorify the fascist regime of Salazar, and its summer phase was devoted to imperial history (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). The war cut foreign participation well below the plans (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). Neither entry gives an attendance figure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The idea came from Antonio Ferro, of the national secretariat of propaganda, and Galvao organised the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). Galvao had been a close friend of Antonio de Oliveira Salazar but later opposed the regime (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The official rationale was to set forth in pageant, dance, and patriotic community gatherings, the story of eight centuries of Portuguese history. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). The fair celebrated the founding of Portugal in 1140 and independence from Spain after sixty years of rule, in 1640 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Both entries say that its real purpose was to glorify the fascist regime of Salazar (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).

Site and programme

The fair stood in the Belem district near the Hieronymite Monastery, where Vasco da Gama and the poet Luis de Camoes were buried (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The 2008 entry prints the poet as Luiz Vaz de Camees (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). The programme ran in three cycles (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The first, in spring 1940, had medieval history as its theme. It opened with town meetings on 2 June, a speech by Salazar, an exhibit of primitive art in the Museum of Ancient Art and a Navy Day festival (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The Navy Day events included a reception aboard a replica of a fifteenth-century caravel, a naval procession on the Tagus with a scheduled landing of a Pan-Am Clipper, and a review of international fleets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The final phase, in the fall, commemorated the Brigantine Epoch from 1640 and offered gala performances in the reconstructed opera house and tours of the restored seventeenth-century quarter of Lisbon (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).

Empire and colony

The texts show imperial content in one phase only, the summer second cycle. It included an exhibit of arts and crafts, a garden of colonial designs from Africa, China and India, and a pageant of twenty-one imperial provinces (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Neither text says which territories the garden's designs represented, whether they were Portuguese, or which colonies took part (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). Neither describes colonised people on display or taking part (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). The only non-European art named is the exhibit of primitive art, which belonged to the medieval first cycle (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The texts state no aims for empire, trade or settlement; they give only the purpose of glorifying the regime (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). At closing there were rumours that the director of colonial fairs and exhibitions had profited from the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).

Opening and foreign participation

The opening on 2 June coincided with the British evacuation of Dunkirk, which almost crowded the fair out of the newspapers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). The 2008 entry prints "evaluation" for this word (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Because of the war, foreign participation was much smaller than planned, and the review of international fleets suffered from the war and from tensions among the countries still neutral (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). At the opening Salazar and the colonial patriarch attended services at Lisbon Cathedral (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). On 30 June the British, anxious to keep Portugal neutral, had the duke of Kent award Salazar the Grand Cross of the Most Distinguished Order of St Michael and St George, an award usually reserved for a Briton (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).

Reception and controversy

The fair, overshadowed by the war, attracted nowhere near the attention given to the New York World's Fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). The entries disagree on the closing day: the 1990 entry gives 2 December (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306), and the 2008 entry gives 3 December (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). At the close there were rumours that the director of colonial fairs and exhibitions and national broadcasting had profited from the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Salazar dismissed the charges as communist-inspired and declared his satisfaction with the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 307; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313).

How historians have interpreted it

Two reference entries interpret the fair as propaganda. John E. Findling argues that its purpose was to glorify the fascist regime (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313). Dwight W. Hoover says the same (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 307; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). Both entries cite O. H. de Oliveira Marques, who described the fair as "a typical Fascist display in its manner of interpreting the past and abusing it to herald the present." (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 307).

Its place in the series

The reference entries link the Lisbon fair with the New York World's Fair only as its rival for attention (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306). Greenhalgh shows Portugal as a lavish imperial exhibitor, with a national pavilion at the Paris Exposition of 1900 and a display at the Imperial International Exhibition of 1909 in London (Greenhalgh 1988p. 70). He records a 'Salle Outre-Mer' at the Paris Exposition of 1937, centred on Angola and Mozambique (Greenhalgh 1988p. 70). He then notes a colonial exhibition in Lisbon in 1940, in one sentence (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). A footnote says he knew little of the exhibition and did not know whether other European nations took part (Greenhalgh 1988p. 81).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Propaganda and the Salazar regime — both entries say the fair was meant to glorify the fascist regime, and Salazar dismissed the critics as communist-inspired (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 313).
  • The imperial phase — the summer cycle was devoted to imperial history, with a garden of colonial designs from Africa, China and India, but the texts give no further detail (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).
  • War and neutrality — the opening fell on the Dunkirk evacuation, foreign participation fell short of plans, and the British honoured Salazar on 30 June (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).
  • Reception and money — the fair was overshadowed by New York, and rumours of profit by colonial and broadcasting officials surfaced at its close (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 306).
  • Thin sources — the texts are short reference entries with little English-language material and no attendance figures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 314; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 307).

Questions for study

  1. What did the garden of colonial designs from Africa, China and India show, and which territories did it cover?
  2. Why did the organisers place imperial history in the middle of three cycles that ran from medieval to seventeenth-century themes?
  3. The two reference entries give different closing dates. What does that suggest about the reliability of dates and figures for fairs of this period?
  4. How far can the fair be called imperial when its imperial content is one phase, described in a few lines?
  5. What evidence would show whether colonised people took part in the Lisbon fair, given that the texts are silent on this?

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

On the web

Wikipedia

The Portuguese World Exhibition (Portuguese: Exposição do Mundo Português) was held in Lisbon in 1940 to mark 800 years since the foundation of the country and 300 years since the restoration of independence from Spain. The fair ran from 23 June to 2 December 1940, held on the Praça do Império, and was attended by 3 million people.
