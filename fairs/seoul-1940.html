Great Chosŏn Exposition

Cho-sen Dai-hakurankai
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Great Keijo Exposition was held in 1940 at Keijo (Kyŏngsŏng, present-day Seoul), as one of the events marking the twenty-six-hundredth anniversary of the Japanese nation (Henry 2014p. 1; Henry 2014p. 20; Henry 2014p. 171). Officials placed it on a suburban site near Wangsimni, far from Kyŏngbok Palace, and the Keijo nippo was its primary sponsor (Henry 2014p. 171; Henry 2014p. 173). More than 1.3 million people attended during most of September and October (Henry 2014p. 171). Henry calls it perhaps the most impressive of the 1940 celebrations, because it compressed the empire's sacred sites into one space (Henry 2014p. 171). Park mentions it only in passing (Parkp. 21). The exposition was held, and neither text reports a cancellation.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

In 1940 the state celebrated the twenty-six-hundredth anniversary of the nation with an estimated two thousand events across the peninsula (Henry 2014p. 171). Henry places the exposition in the wartime shift from assimilation to what he calls imperial subjectification, which asked all subjects, and colonised Koreans in particular, to offer themselves as loyal servants of the emperor (Henry 2014p. 169). Henry writes that events such as the torch relay and the Great Korea Exposition aimed "to create an imagined community of Pan-Asian subjects" (Henry 2014p. 20).

Organisers and finance

Officials chose the site, and the Keijo nippo, the newspaper, was the primary sponsor (Henry 2014p. 171; Henry 2014p. 173). The text does not name an organising committee or give the cost. A collection box at the entrance of the Heroic Deeds Hall raised more than 730 yen (Henry 2014p. 185). Henry calls the report that announced this self-congratulatory, and notes that the average contribution was under fifteen yen among what the author gives as nearly twenty-five thousand daily visitors (Henry 2014p. 185).

Site and buildings

The site was a developing area near Wangsimni, away from Kyŏngbok Palace, where earlier colonial expositions had been held (Henry 2014p. 171). The 1915 and 1929 expositions used the palace grounds (Henry 2014p. 18; Henry 2014p. 19). At the centre of the main corridor stood a tower "uniting the eight corners of the world under one roof", a half-size replica of a 37-metre monument recently erected in Miyazaki (Henry 2014p. 171). The Imperial History and Colonial Rule Commemoration Halls stood at the front of the corridor, and the armed forces halls at the rear (Henry 2014p. 172; Henry 2014p. 174).

Exhibits and attractions

Two dioramas showed sacred sites on the peninsula. The Puyŏ diorama presented a new shrine that officials had recently designated for the site, with four Japanese monarchs as its deities; the text places this within a project to rebuild Puyŏ as a Shinto city that failed (Henry 2014p. 173). The Korea Shrine diorama showed a Korean family bowing before a Shinto priest (Henry 2014p. 173). The Heroic Deeds Hall held swords, binoculars and about three hundred portraits of soldiers who had recently died in famous battles, including the Nanking Massacre of 1937-38, together with bloodstained uniforms (Henry 2014p. 174). The Military Rationing Hall compared Japanese rations with the milk and meat of the enemy and gave out four thousand Asian-style buns (Henry 2014p. 175). An outdoor display of aircraft, tanks and cannon came with lectures aimed at schoolchildren on class visits (Henry 2014p. 175).

Who took part and visitors

Henry says that illiterate and poorly educated Koreans made up "the vast majority of exposition visitors" (Henry 2014p. 173). He argues that the Puyŏ symbolism was therefore hard for most of them to read (Henry 2014p. 173). In the armed forces halls, an article revealed that only twenty-two of the 12,600 peninsular war dead were Korean (Henry 2014p. 174). Before 1944 officials had enlisted 16,830 Koreans from 808,000 applications (Henry 2014p. 174). Officials invited bereaved families and newly recruited Koreans to special celebrations (Henry 2014p. 174).

Reception and the colonial setting

Henry argues that the press told visitors what to feel. The Keijo nippo presented the exposition's displays as a guide to sentiment, and the highly censored media highlighted the loyalty of Japanese settlers as the model of imperial subjectification (Henry 2014p. 171; Henry 2014p. 173). Ethnic Japanese continued to dominate the symbols that officials could show as examples of loyalty (Henry 2014p. 174). Henry notes that newspaper reports on the military halls spoke only from the privileged position of the colonisers (Henry 2014p. 174).

Its place in the series

Park says that the 1915 exhibition already had features seen in later colonial expositions, naming the Chosŏn Exposition of 1929 and the Great Chosŏn Exposition of 1940 (Parkp. 21). Park gives a total of thirty large and small expositions and related exhibitions held in Korea between 1915 and 1940 (Parkp. 21). Henry describes the 1929 Korea Exposition as originally planned to commemorate fifteen years of Japanese rule in 1925, and as designed partly to lure Japanese tourists (Henry 2014p. 19). Park and Henry name the 1929 event differently, and neither text says whether they describe the same exposition (Parkp. 21; Henry 2014p. 19).

Pan-Asian framing

The texts describe no participants or visitors from other parts of Asia, and none from Taiwan, Manchuria, China or Southeast Asia. Other parts of Asia appear in the exposition only through its framing. Henry calls the donation at the Heroic Deeds Hall an example of the Pan-Asian solidarity of the wartime empire (Henry 2014p. 185). The same hall displayed portraits of soldiers who had died in famous battles, including the Nanking Massacre (Henry 2014p. 174).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial subjectification — Henry treats the exposition as one of the wartime events that asked colonised Koreans to show loyalty to the emperor (Henry 2014p. 169; Henry 2014p. 171)
  • Sacred topography — the exposition packed shrines and sacred places into one site, through the tower and the dioramas (Henry 2014p. 171; Henry 2014p. 173)
  • Militarised display — the Heroic Deeds Hall, the Rationing Hall and the weapons display linked the visit to the war (Henry 2014p. 174; Henry 2014p. 175)
  • Ethnic hierarchy — the displays and the press put Japanese settlers at the centre of loyalty (Henry 2014p. 171; Henry 2014p. 174)
  • Pan-Asian rhetoric — the aims and the donation were described in Pan-Asian terms (Henry 2014p. 20; Henry 2014p. 185)

Questions for study

  1. Henry argues that most visitors could not read the Puyŏ symbolism. What evidence would show how visitors with little schooling actually understood the displays?
  2. The torch relay reports quote no individual spectators (Henry 2014p. 176). What sources could show how visitors to this exposition responded?
  3. Officials moved the exposition from Kyŏngbok Palace to Wangsimni (Henry 2014p. 171). What did a new suburban site allow the organisers to show that the palace grounds did not?
  4. Park says the 1915 exhibition introduced Korean audiences to modern mass culture and commodified spectacle (Parkp. 21). How far did the 1940 exposition address the same audience, given that Henry says the state relied overwhelmingly on Japanese and on the Keijo nippo to instruct Koreans (Henry 2014p. 171)?

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
5Imperial Subjectification: The Collapsing Spaces of a Wartime Cityp. 168Asia

Henry examines how the late colonial state, after war with China began in 1937, tried to make Koreans in Keijo (Seoul) loyal subjects of the emperor, a process the chapter calls imperial subjectification. Its evidence comes from the Keijo nippo's coverage of the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition, the torch relay from Ise to Korea Shrine and a school tour of Japan's sacred sites, and from the Namsan shrines, the militarised autumn festival, Seoul Nation-Protecting Shrine and the campaign to install Ise amulets and miniature shrines in homes. Henry sets the official claim that rising shrine visits proved growing reverence against the complaints of Shinto priests about coercion and formality, and against photographs that show Koreans bowing in a Confucian manner or keeping their hats on. The chapter argues that loyalty was performed under observation and that the state could measure it only by counting. It ends by showing how the cult fell short in the home, where amulets were discarded and shrines misused, and by noting a Christian seminary professor who visited Namsan while keeping his own faith.

In the contents of this work

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Conclusionp. 21Asia

The brief conclusion treats 1915 as the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea, with some thirty large and small expositions held between 1915 and 1940. Park argues that the exhibition was a cultural machinery producing a conflicted Korean subject, shaped by seduction and spectacle, education and discipline, and coercive colonial control. It ends by linking this to a Korean nationalism understood as an ambivalent response to uneven development.

Henry's chapter on wartime Keijo (Seoul) treats the Great Keijo Exposition of 1940 as one of the main sites where the late colonial state tried to make Koreans loyal subjects of the emperor. It describes the suburban site near Wangsimni, the central tower, the dioramas of Puyŏ and Korea Shrine, and the armed forces halls, and argues that the exposition compressed the empire's sacred places into one space. Henry's wider argument is that the wartime state could measure loyalty only by counting performances, and the exposition is presented as one of the largest of those performances. About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.

On the web

Wikipedia

The Chōsen Grand Exposition (Japanese: 朝鮮大博覧会; Korean: 조선대박람회) was an exposition held on 1 September 1940 south of East Kyŏngsŏng Station (now Cheongnyangni Station), Keijō (Seoul), Chōsen (Korea), Empire of Japan. Its full name was Kigen Year 2600 Colonial Government 30th Anniversary Chōsen Grand Exposition (紀元二千六百年 始政三十周年記念 朝鮮大博覧会; 기원이천육백년 시정삼십주년기념 조선대박람회). It was held in honor of the 2600th year of the Japanese calendar (kigen) and 30th anniversary of Japanese colonial rule in Korea. It was organised by the Keijō Nippō newspaper.
