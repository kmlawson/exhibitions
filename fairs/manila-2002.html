Expo 2002 (Manila)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

Expo 2002, also called Manila 2002 or the World Expo 2002, was a planned international exposition for Manila that was never held. Planning began in 1993 under Commissioner-General Mina T. Gabor, and the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) gave it sanction in the mid-1990s (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335). The fair would probably have been built at Quezon Memorial Park in Quezon City (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). It would have been the first sanctioned international fair hosted by a developing country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). The project collapsed in January 1999 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). Allwood calls the lost event the first truly authentic registered exposition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Planning for a 2002 exposition in Manila began in 1993 under Commissioner-General Mina T. Gabor, who had headed the Philippines pavilion at Expo 92 in Seville (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). The World Expo 2002, as it came to be called, received BIE sanction in the mid-1990s (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). Its theme was to have been "Ecotourism: Growing with Nature" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). The fair would probably have stood at Quezon Memorial Park, a site in Quezon City, a Manila suburb (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434).

Politics, finance and collapse

The entry says the organisers were deep in debt and that there were allegations of corruption (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). It adds that rumours of political discord pitted Gabor and her main ally, former President Fidel V. Ramos, against the current president, Joseph Estrada (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). These difficulties dampened enthusiasm in the local business community and led to the abandonment of the project (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). Allwood sets the bid within a competitive culture, describing "costly and divisive competition" that undermined the Philippines' aspiration to be the first developing country to host a world exposition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197).

The texts give the end in different terms. The Queensland entry says the Manila effort collapsed in January 1999 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). The Manila entry itself gives no date. Allwood's table calls the event cancelled (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211).

Its place in the series

Allwood describes the lost Manila event as the first truly authentic registered exposition and says its loss will likely have untold repercussions for the BIE and the medium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). He also writes that the French 2004 event would occupy the period vacated by the Philippines (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). Queensland, the loser to Manila in the BIE deliberation, reconsidered its bid after the Manila collapse. The BIE refused its request to delay to 2003, and Queensland dropped its plans (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). Allwood's table places the Manila event under the recognised heading, not the registered one (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211). He also describes an earlier Manila bid: the Philippines applied in 1992 for a recognised exposition, won the right to hold it, and withdrew after a new government declined to fund it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). He does not say that the 2002 project continued this bid.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Planning and sanction — The Manila entry dates planning to 1993 and BIE sanction to the mid-1990s, and gives the theme and site as intended, not built (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434).
  • Debt and corruption — The entry names debt and allegations of corruption as the financial problems, without figures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434).
  • Political rivalry — The entry ties the project's fate to a split between Gabor and her allies and the sitting president (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434).
  • Developing-country hosts — The fair would have been the first sanctioned one hosted by a developing country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434); Allwood says the aspiration was undermined by costly competition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197).
  • Bidding rules and proliferation — Allwood links the lost event to the 1988 protocol's recognised category and to a growing number of events (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197; Allwood et al. 2001p. 197).

Questions for study

  1. Allwood calls the Manila event registered in his prose but lists it among recognised events in his table. What difference did the category make to a host, and why does it matter for his argument? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197; Allwood et al. 2001p. 211)
  2. The Manila entry gives debt, corruption and political rivalry as causes, while the Toronto entry blames a lack of private-sector support (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). What did each account assume about who should pay for a fair?
  3. The Philippines bid for both 1992 and 2002. What evidence in these texts would show that the two bids shared causes, and what does the text leave unresolved? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171)
  4. Queensland was refused a delay to 2003 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). What does the BIE's refusal suggest about how it treated schedules and hosts?

This appendix to the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions lists expositions that were planned but never held, from New York 1913 and Baltimore 1914 to Calgary 2005, each as a short history. It does not argue a thesis; it records how each proposal was made, which sites and themes were chosen, and where it stopped. The causes recur: world wars and the Great Depression, economic slumps, doubts that a city could afford the cost, governments and mayors withdrawing support, and rival bids that won the BIE vote. Several entries are detailed, notably Philadelphia 1976, Los Angeles 1981, Paris 1989, Chicago 1992, Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, which trace the bidding, financing and political fights. Some entries show a fair's afterlife, such as Tokyo 1940 tickets honored at Osaka 1970. The appendix ends with Calgary's defeat by Aichi for 2005.

This reference work's appendix on expositions that were planned but never held gives a short entry on Manila 2002. The entry records planning from 1993 under Commissioner-General Mina T. Gabor, sanction by the BIE in the mid-1990s, and the intended theme and site. It then lists the problems that ended the project: debt, allegations of corruption, political rivalry between Gabor's ally and the current president, and the loss of local business enthusiasm. The entry offers no interpretation of its own beyond these causes, so students should read it as a factual record. Its date for the collapse comes from the neighbouring Queensland entry, not from this one.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
Chapter 16, 'What of the Future?' (running head 'After 2000', printed p. 197, l. 9561); Appendix 3 table, section C under the 1988 Amendment (PDF p. 261)

Allwood's concluding chapter mentions the Manila project only in passing, as one case in a wider argument. He calls the lost Manila exposition the first truly authentic registered exposition and says its loss will likely have repercussions for the BIE and the medium. He holds the BIE accountable for the proliferation of events that followed the 1988 protocol, and the Manila loss is one part of that failure. The larger case is that the BIE's rules have allowed more events than the medium can sustain.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.
