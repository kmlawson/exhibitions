London · 1871–1874

London International Exhibitions (South Kensington series)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The London International Exhibitions of 1871-1874 were four annual shows held at South Kensington, on the Royal Commissioners' estate beside the Royal Albert Hall. The series began as a memorial to Prince Albert and was stopped in 1874 after attendance and receipts fell every year (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its reference-work accounts blame the policies taken from the Paris exhibition of 1867: smaller, cheaper and more educational shows that were also less dramatic (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its colonial displays are read as claims about empire and trade (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The series began with a proposal of 18 July 1868 from the Provisional Committee of the Royal Albert Hall to the Royal Commissioners for the 1851 Exhibition, the body that held the estate at South Kensington. The aim was to commemorate Prince Albert and his support for industrial education (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Ideas for restricted, single-subject shows, which Le Play repeated after the Paris exhibition of 1867, caught the attention of Henry Cole, who obtained the commissioners' permission to use land behind the Royal Albert Hall (Allwood et al. 2001). The commissioners adopted the Paris recommendations: limited size, no prizes, and goods grouped by class rather than by nation (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The number of shows planned is disputed. One account says the commissioners published a plan for ten annual exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 2008), and a chapter describes a projected run of ten that closed after four (Filipová 2015). Another says Cole agreed to finance up to five one-year shows (Allwood et al. 2001). Only four were held (Filipová 2015; Findling & Pelle 2008).

Organisation and finance

The buildings and fittings cost £126,383 in total (Findling & Pelle 2008). The series gave no prizes, and the lack of juried awards was later cited as a cause of its failure (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

When the series ended, the commissioners met a loss. Allwood, Allan and Reid put the overall loss at £150,000 (Allwood et al. 2001), and the Filipová chapter gives the same deficit (Filipová 2015). The reference works give a loss of £17,821 for 1874 alone (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). ## Site and buildings Two brick halls stood on the commissioners' estate, beside the Royal Albert Hall and the Horticultural Gardens. Scott and Wild built them to a design by Richard Redgrave, executed by Francis Fowke. They were red brick with terracotta and thirty feet wide (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The gardens separated the two halls, and the public disliked having exhibits on opposite sides (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Royal Horticultural Society withdrew its support after the first year and limited access to its gardens (Findling & Pelle 1990). Another account dates the withdrawal to the opening of the 1872 show (Allwood et al. 2001). Cole blamed the Society for keeping visitors out and charging for a pathway, and named the lack of a railway link and of refreshment rooms (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Exhibits, music and fine art

Each season had its own goods classes. The first show featured pottery, woollen fabrics and educational works, and the last featured lace, civil engineering and foreign wines (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Organists from eight European countries played the Royal Albert Hall organ in 1871, a fifty-piece orchestra played daily in 1873, and organ recitals took place daily in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008). A fountain lit by voltaic arcs, a form of electric light, was installed at South Kensington in 1874 (Filipová 2015).

Greenhalgh judges the 1871 and 1874 fine art shows the most impressive of the series. The 1871 show caught French painting before the Franco-Prussian War disrupted it (Greenhalgh 1988). The British definition of art was very wide and included many craft practices, and owners tired of lending works, so the organisers turned to retrospective shows, including four deceased artists (Greenhalgh 1988). Critics found few British artists to praise in 1873, and the foreign work in 1874 was of lower quality, which Greenhalgh ties to reduced foreign interest (Greenhalgh 1988).

Nations, colonies and peoples on show

Greenhalgh argues that empire was displayed continuously across the series. A similar number of colonies and dominions appeared in 1871-1874 as in 1862 (Greenhalgh 1988). The quotation "From 1851 onwards empire was proudly and exhaustively displayed." (Greenhalgh 1988) sums up this reading. Geppert writes that national pavilions were tried at the London show five years after 1867, but says Philadelphia in 1876 was the first to let each nation build its own pavilion, so the London point rests on a single sentence (Geppert 2010). Japan's participation in a London show of 1874 cost 8,932 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999).

Hoffenberg reads the colonial exhibits as trade and status claims. New South Wales funded the collection of exhibits for 1871 and gave free use of telegraph lines and railways (Hoffenberg 2001). The Queensland annexe at South Kensington had enlarged photographs of Aboriginal people across its back wall (Hoffenberg 2001). In 1874 the commissioners proposed a National Museum of the Empire and asked colonial commissioners for life-size figures of Aboriginal inhabitants (Hoffenberg 2001).

The Bombay Committee collected material for 1871, and Indian goods were sold in a bazaar beside the entrance (Hoffenberg 2001). The full-size cast of the Sanchi Tope dominated the Picture Galleries in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg describes casts as a currency exchanged for other items (Hoffenberg 2001). Across the four shows, 8,000 square feet of Indian court space was filled at a total cost of £7,351 (Greenhalgh 1988). Hoffenberg's text says six copies of the Sanchi cast were planned, while a note gives four casts made, three for South Kensington and one for French colleagues (Hoffenberg 2001).

Greenhalgh argues that at South Kensington in 1851, 1862 and 1871-1874 the English national profile was taken for granted, and that the organisers made no attempt to define the English national character (Greenhalgh 1988p. 120). In the arts, European work was casually accepted as superior to British work, even in establishment circles (Greenhalgh 1988p. 120).

Hoffenberg gives a closer picture of one colonial court. Visitors to the Queensland Annexe at South Kensington in the early 1870s saw Australian goods in glass cases and wooden boxes, arranged as both trophies and a commercial inventory (Hoffenberg 2001p. 105). After many offers to buy its brooches and gems at the 1871 International Exhibition, Richard Daintree, the colony's Agent-General, solicited more local manufactures and rough agates for later shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 106). Daintree stressed quality and novelty for the gems, while other Australians relied on size and quantity. Jury awards for white soft wheat, Burra Burra copper and merino wool helped to legitimise colonial goods in the English market (Hoffenberg 2001p. 106).

Visitors, receipts and the end of the series

Attendance fell every year. The reference works give 1,142,154 visitors in 1871 and 466,745 in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The 1872 and 1873 figures are 647,190 and 500,033 (Allwood et al. 2001). Gross receipts dropped from £76,433 in 1871 to £16,399 in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The 1871 profit of £17,671 became a loss of £17,821 by 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the 1871 profit as £30,000 (Allwood et al. 2001), which conflicts with the £17,671 given above (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The decline was visible in the ceremonies. The Prince of Wales and Princess Helena opened the 1871 show in a formal state ceremony. The 1874 show opened with none (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The Practical Magazine called the closing "most inglorious ending of any Industrial, especially any International, Exhibition" (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). In March 1874 the commissioners, on the advice of the Earl of Caernarvon, resolved to end the series unless the first two months of 1874 proved very popular. They did not, and the series ended (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Caernarvon blamed the competition of the Vienna exhibition of 1873 and the Royal Horticultural Society's lack of cooperation (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg says the London shows drew nearly a million visitors a year, against over six million for 1862 (Hoffenberg 2001). That figure does not match the falling counts in the reference works (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

Who ran the fair

The Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs lists the Prince of Wales as president of the Royal Commission for the 1871-1874 shows, the Marquis of Ripon as chair of the General Purposes Committee and Earl Granville as chair of the Finance Committee (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 383). Filipová's chapter traces the policy of smaller themed shows to the disappointing outcome of the 1862 exhibition, which had limited the ambitions of the organisers. It says that Sir Edward Cole then proposed a turn to smaller, themed shows (Filipová 2015p. 206). This chapter gives the name as Sir Edward Cole, where this essay has Henry Cole, and the two forms need checking against each other.

Entertainment and the shows' appeal

Greenhalgh says that each of the annual shows planned after 1871 would pick three or more extra areas to specialise in, such as shipping, civil engineering, the building trade or lace-making (Greenhalgh 1988p. 32). He describes the South Kensington shows of the 1870s as far less celebratory and far more studious and unpretentious than earlier ones (Greenhalgh 1988p. 32). The organisers treated popular entertainment as a side issue, and their worst fear was that the shows "might be taken for a fair" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). The next South Kensington project took a different course. Filipová describes Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protégé and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, who turned the planned Fisheries Exhibition of 1883 into a fashionable success by adding promenades under electric light, illuminated fountains and musical entertainments (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Education and the South Kensington museums

Lawn describes South Kensington as the direct descendant of the 1851 Great Exhibition in the field of education (Lawn 2009p. 19). He argues that the Paris exhibition of 1867 revealed the weakness of scientific and technical education in England, which led to a central institution at South Kensington that opened in 1885 (Lawn 2009p. 19). He also says that the organisation of the South Kensington museums became the model that colonial and dominion museums followed, and that colonial museums depended on foreign institutions for materials, designs and staff (Lawn 2009p. 20). Lawn does not discuss the 1871-1874 shows, so any link between the series and the museums is an inference from these pages.

How historians have interpreted it

Dibello, in the London entry of the Findling and Pelle reference works, argues that borrowed Paris policies made the shows cheaper and more educational but less dramatic, and that falling receipts led to the end of the series (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid argue that classifying every exhibit by subject stopped countries from showing their goods with local colour and showmanship, so that the whole show became a dull, almost scientific, display of goods (Allwood et al. 2001). A chapter in the Filipová volume argues that the dry treatment of the shows failed to excite the public (Filipová 2015).

Luckhurst, in a twentieth-century critique, went beyond Cole's guess that arrangement by class may have deterred visitors. Luckhurst argued that it obscured the international character of the displays and gave foreign countries no chance to arrange their own goods (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh argues that organisers were more worried about European and American industry catching up with Britain than about popular political consciousness, and gives public education as the stated rationale (Greenhalgh 1988). Hoffenberg reads the colonial and Indian displays as participatory exchanges through which imperial and national identity were built, rather than as displays imposed from above (Hoffenberg 2001).

The texts agree that the series ended in 1874 after falling attendance and receipts, and that the Vienna exhibition of 1873 was a rival (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). They disagree on the cause. The reference works put it down to the Paris-derived policies and to outside events (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid put it down mainly to classification (Allwood et al. 2001). Giberti mentions the London shows only as comparisons, noting that they adopted signed reports in place of medals, a policy Vienna reversed in 1873 (Giberti 2002).

Hoffenberg places the Sanchi cast within a wider argument about traditionalism. He calls the cast "immense" and lists it among the traditionalist exhibits that filled the Picture Gallery in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 273). In his epilogue such displays expressed "imperialist nostalgia", an effort to reclaim a traditional India and to establish the authority of new social groups such as Australian settlers and Anglo-Indians (Hoffenberg 2001p. 273). He sets the London cast beside the Irish antiquities that commissioners sent to Dublin's Industrial Exhibition in 1853, which filled a separate court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 274), and says that such preservationist projects played an important and visible role at the exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 274).

Giberti reads the London shows through the classification debate that led to the Centennial. In May 1874 the Committee on Classification reported on the classifications used in London in 1851, 1862 and 1871-1874 (Giberti 2002). Its verdict was that the British system "was one of the best that has been proposed", although it found no place for all materials and industries (Giberti 2002). It also said that the system did not express the evolution idea that the Centennial's brief required (Giberti 2002). This is a more favourable judgement than the account Allwood, Allan and Reid give of subject classification, which they blame for a dull display of goods (Allwood et al. 2001p. 37).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India

India was the Asian presence the texts describe in most detail. The Bombay Committee collected and forwarded material for the 1871 show, with local figures such as Sir Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy and Dr. Bhau Daji among those involved (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59). Indian commissioners sold Indian manufactures, including shawls, pottery and wood carving, in a bazaar beside the entrance in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 81). Greenhalgh gives the Indian court across the four South Kensington shows as 8,000 square feet, filled at a total cost of £7,351 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Greenhalgh says India was the most carefully displayed possession at the Great Exhibition and remained so through the period at home and abroad (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Watson, who edited the official catalogues for India, is listed as a commissioner at the South Kensington Annual Exhibitions of 1870-74 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285).

The full-size cast of the Sanchi Tope dominated the Picture Galleries in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 153). Hoffenberg says Cole and colleagues in India intended six copies, three for South Kensington and one each for the French, Prussian and Indian governments (Hoffenberg 2001p. 153). A footnote gives a different figure. Because of financial limits the government of India reduced its contributions, so only four casts were made, three for South Kensington, which contributed 75 per cent of the funds, and one for French colleagues [31, n. 125, l. 17000-17005]. The text and the note therefore give different numbers of casts.

Japan

Japan's presence is recorded only in outline. Hotta-Lister's appendix of Japanese exhibition participation lists a London Annual International Exhibition of 1874, at an expenditure of 8,932 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The texts give no further detail on Japanese goods, visitors or reception at this fair.

Interpretation

Hoffenberg reads the casts as a form of currency. The South Kensington official who asked for them told Lord Mayo that they would make the world acquainted with Indian architecture, and that copies could be exchanged for other exhibition items wanted by museum officers in England and India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 157). Greenhalgh reads the colonial displays of these years as continuous imperial display, noting that a similar number of colonies and dominions appeared in 1871-74 as in 1862 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Classification by subject — goods were grouped by class, not by nation. Cole thought this deterred visitors, and Luckhurst later argued that it hid the international character of the displays (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Attendance and receipts — both fell every year, from about 1.14 million visitors in 1871 to 466,745 in 1874, and the series ended at a loss (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Empire on display — Greenhalgh reads the colonial displays as continuous imperial display, and Hoffenberg reads them as trade and status claims (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Casts and exchange — Indian casts, including the Sanchi Tope, were exchanged for other exhibits and museum objects (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Fine art and music — fine art was a strength in 1871 and 1874 but was weakened by reluctant lenders, and music was a daily attraction (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Awards and juries — no prizes were given, and signed reports replaced medals in the London shows (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Giberti 2002).
  • Blame — Vienna 1873, the Royal Horticultural Society, the Franco-Prussian War and a dispute over the sale of goods were all named as causes (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

Questions for study

  1. The Paris policies were meant to make the shows cheaper and more educational. Did they make the fair less attractive, or were outside events such as the Vienna exhibition of 1873 more to blame?
  2. Greenhalgh reads the series as education and continuous empire, while Hoffenberg reads its colonial displays as trade. Can both readings hold for the same exhibits?
  3. Are the figures for the planned length and the 1871 profit reliable enough to base an argument on? How should a historian handle figures that the sources do not agree on?
  4. Allwood, Allan and Reid say classification by subject made the show dull. Would a display arranged by nation have been more successful, and what evidence in the texts would decide this?
  5. Why did the Indian and colonial governments send material at all, if the series was losing visitors? What did casts and specimens give them in return?
  6. Filipová says the 1871-1874 shows failed to excite the public (Filipová 2015p. 206), while Greenhalgh says the organisers refused to follow French and American fairs in offering popular entertainment (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Did the series fail because it refused such attractions, or for other reasons?
  7. Giberti finds the British classification one of the best proposed (Giberti 2002), while Allwood, Allan and Reid blame subject classification for a dull display of goods (Allwood et al. 2001p. 37). What evidence in these texts would decide whether the classification or something else was the real problem?
  8. Lawn links the South Kensington museums to the weakness in technical education that Paris 1867 revealed (Lawn 2009p. 19), while Greenhalgh describes the 1870s shows as studious and unpretentious (Greenhalgh 1988p. 32). Did the shows try to serve the same educational purpose as the museums, or did they move away from it?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
Epilogue: Recessional: Imperial Culture and Colonial Nationalismp. 273Asia

The epilogue argues that exhibitions from 1851 to 1914 presented a traditionalist vision of empire, built on relics, royal ceremony and the preservation of supposedly disappearing cultures, and that this vision later served colonial nationalism as well. Hoffenberg cites the Sanchi Tope cast shown in London in 1871, the Gwalior Gateway at South Kensington in 1886, and Irish antiquities shown at Dublin in 1853 as examples of preservation that gave commissioners authority and their sponsoring states legitimacy. Antiquarian protection laws in England and the colonies supplied the legal frame. Displays of Indian art and labour were later claimed by the Indian National Congress for its swadeshi movement, while the Melbourne Trades Hall Council used the exhibition form to promote Australian goods and labour. The contrast between the neoclassical Australian pavilion and the durbar-style Indian pavilion at Wembley in 1924-25 shows how architecture still encoded hierarchy. The conclusion is that a neoimperial network of Commonwealth exhibitions continues to shape identities into the postcolonial era.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 5

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
4The Big Step Forward and the American Centennial Expositionp. 31

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the shift from the London exhibitions to the wider fairs of the 1860s and 1870s. The main case is Paris in 1867, which introduced national pavilions, Frédéric Le Play's concentric galleries including the History of Work, and a building by Gustave Eiffel, and which was overshadowed by the Tsar's near-assassination and news of Maximilian's execution. The London International Exhibitions of 1871 to 1874 failed as a thematic series, while the Vienna exhibition of 1873 was undone by a stock-market collapse and high hotel prices, its income reaching only a sixth of its cost. Philadelphia's Centennial Exhibition of 1876, whose grounds were planned by Herman Schwarzmann, is presented as the first properly landscaped international exposition, with the Corliss engine and Bell's telephone among its displays. The chapter ends with the Centennial's funding from public money and concessions, and with the later demolition of its main buildings.

In the contents of this work

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
2The Growth and Character of the Institutionp. 7

Badger examines the world's fair as an institution, which between 1851 and 1925 became a standard part of Western culture. Each fair claimed to display the latest stage of progress, from British machinery and Bessemer steel in London to German motors at Paris in 1867 and the telephone and typewriter at Philadelphia in 1876. Architecture and the arts had to show refinement, which gave them a largely functional role, and the congresses held alongside the fairs seldom did more than reflect current thought. National purposes shaped many fairs, from Austria's recovery in Vienna in 1873 to Napoleon III's rivalry with London in 1855 and 1867. Badger then turns to finance, showing that governments subsidised fairs and that few made a profit, and to the city, which he treats as the greatest exhibit of any fair. The chapter closes by arguing that the claimed economic gains to host cities, hard to measure, were used to win public money from city governments.

In the contents of this work

Giberti, Bruno. Designing the Centennial: A History of the 1876 International Exhibition in Philadelphia. Lexington: University Press of Kentucky, 2002.
1The Order of the Exhibition

Giberti argues that the organisers of the 1876 Centennial were preoccupied with classification, and that this preoccupation shaped the whole exhibition. The chapter traces the classificatory logic from the French national expositions of 1798-1849, through London in 1851, where Playfair's thirty classes gave way to a geographic installation, to Paris in 1855 and 1867, where Napoleon's dual system of country and type produced a wheel-like plan. Vienna in 1873 is the counter-case, a developmental order realised as national courts along one nave. The Philadelphia scheme, drafted by a committee that included William Blake, combined these models in ten departments and a decimal notation modelled on Philadelphia's street numbers. The classification was then cut to seven departments to fit the buildings, and the official catalogue was late and disordered, which Giberti presents as evidence that the exhibition was never fully ordered. The chapter ends by arguing that the organisers had to keep revising their project in the face of conditions they could not foresee.

In the contents of this work

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
OneChina and the World Exhibitions before 1903p. 11Asia

Chapter One traces China's presence at world exhibitions from London in 1851 to the Paris exposition of 1900, and argues that the foreign-staffed Imperial Maritime Customs shaped how China was shown. The Customs took charge of Chinese exhibits from Vienna in 1873, and the foreign commissioners' selections of local curiosities, including bound-feet shoes and opium tools, later became a target for Chinese critics. Early travelogues by Bin Chun, Li Gui, Guo Songtao and Zeng Jize describe Philadelphia in 1876 and Paris in 1878, and reformers such as Zheng Guanying argued from the 1890s that China should hold its own exhibitions. The chapter also compares China with Japan, whose exhibits from Philadelphia onwards outshone China's, including at Chicago in 1893, and it ends with the Qing court's return to Beijing in 1902, when the reformers' hold on the debate began to change.

In the contents of this work

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
The Bull and the Book: images of Spain and Spanish education in the World Fairs of the nineteenth century, 1851-1900Maria del Mar del Pozo Andrésp. 153

Del Pozo Andrés asks how Spain presented itself through education at the nineteenth-century world fairs, from London in 1851 to Paris in 1900. The argument is that Spanish participation was driven by a wish to restore national pride after imperial decline, and that the fairs staged a contradictory nationalism, boastful about prizes yet anxious about backwardness. The evidence comes from official catalogues, commissioners' reports and the press. Cases include the absence of Spanish teaching materials in London in 1862, the Paris pavilion of 1867 with its stuffed bull, the Philadelphia display of some 715 deluxe books that won ninety-three prizes in 1876, the dispute over an illiteracy chart at Paris in 1878, the pedagogic congress held in Barcelona in 1888 and the Paris pavilion of 1900. The chapter also follows Martin de Martin y Ruiz, a deaf, dumb and blind student shown in Vienna in 1873. It concludes that the fairs supplied new ideas, but that Spanish schools adopted them slowly, and graded schools took hold only after the loss of the colonies in 1898.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
entry 'London 1871-1874' by Catherine Dibello and the Editors, running heads pp. 44-46; bibliographic essay closes the entry

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions treats the four South Kensington seasons of 1871-1874 as one series, beginning with an 1868 proposal to commemorate Prince Albert. Its argument is that the commissioners' policies, taken from the Paris exhibition of 1867 (limited size, no prizes, goods arranged by class), made the shows affordable and educational but also less dramatic, so that attendance and receipts fell every year. The entry sets out the causes of the decline (the Franco-Prussian War, the Royal Horticultural Society's lack of cooperation, the Vienna exhibition of 1873) and the explanations the commissioners gave for the losses. It closes by judging that the London shows 'never even came close' to the Great Exhibition of 1851.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. Dibello's dictionary entry on the London International Exhibitions of 1871-1874 narrates the four seasons year by year: the halls, the classes of goods, the music and lectures, and the falling receipts. Its argument is that the commissioners' policies, borrowed from the Paris exhibition of 1867, made the shows cheaper but less dramatic, and that falling receipts (from £76,433 in 1871 to £16,399 in 1874) led the commissioners to end the series in 1874. It assigns the failure to several causes: the separation of the two halls by the Horticultural Gardens, the lack of a railway link, the Vienna exhibition of 1873, and the arrangement of goods by class, which Cole thought discouraged visitors and which Kenneth Luckhurst later criticised as obscuring the international character of the displays. It measures the series against the Great Exhibition of 1851 and concludes that the London shows 'never even came close' to it.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. Wikipedia

The Annual International Exhibition was a yearly event held in South Kensington, London, United Kingdom between 1871 and 1874. It was preceded by the 1851 Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations and the 1862 International Exhibition in London, as well as many international exhibitions that been held in various countries since 1851. It was intended to be a series of 10 annual events. The first received over a million visitors and made a profit, but the subsequent three had fewer visitors and all made a loss. Details of the 1873 Exhibition, officially described as the London Annual International Exhibition of all fine arts, industries, and inventions, are quoted in this source.
