London · 1871–1874

London International Exhibitions (South Kensington series)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The London International Exhibitions of 1871-1874 were four annual shows held at South Kensington, on the Royal Commissioners' estate beside the Royal Albert Hall. The series began as a memorial to Prince Albert and was stopped in 1874 after attendance and receipts fell every year (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its reference-work accounts blame the policies taken from the Paris exhibition of 1867: smaller, cheaper and more educational shows that were also less dramatic (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its colonial displays are read as claims about empire and trade (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The series began with a proposal of 18 July 1868 from the Provisional Committee of the Royal Albert Hall to the Royal Commissioners for the 1851 Exhibition, the body that held the estate at South Kensington. The aim was to commemorate Prince Albert and his support for industrial education (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Ideas for restricted, single-subject shows, which Le Play repeated after the Paris exhibition of 1867, caught the attention of Henry Cole, who obtained the commissioners' permission to use land behind the Royal Albert Hall (Allwood et al. 2001). The commissioners adopted the Paris recommendations: limited size, no prizes, and goods grouped by class rather than by nation (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The number of shows planned is disputed. One account says the commissioners published a plan for ten annual exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 2008), and a chapter describes a projected run of ten that closed after four (Filipová 2015). Another says Cole agreed to finance up to five one-year shows (Allwood et al. 2001). Only four were held (Filipová 2015; Findling & Pelle 2008).

Organisation and finance

The buildings and fittings cost £126,383 in total (Findling & Pelle 2008). The series gave no prizes, and the lack of juried awards was later cited as a cause of its failure (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

When the series ended, the commissioners met a loss. Allwood, Allan and Reid put the overall loss at £150,000 (Allwood et al. 2001), and the Filipová chapter gives the same deficit (Filipová 2015). The reference works give a loss of £17,821 for 1874 alone (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). ## Site and buildings Two brick halls stood on the commissioners' estate, beside the Royal Albert Hall and the Horticultural Gardens. Scott and Wild built them to a design by Richard Redgrave, executed by Francis Fowke. They were red brick with terracotta and thirty feet wide (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The gardens separated the two halls, and the public disliked having exhibits on opposite sides (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Royal Horticultural Society withdrew its support after the first year and limited access to its gardens (Findling & Pelle 1990). Another account dates the withdrawal to the opening of the 1872 show (Allwood et al. 2001). Cole blamed the Society for keeping visitors out and charging for a pathway, and named the lack of a railway link and of refreshment rooms (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Exhibits, music and fine art

Each season had its own goods classes. The first show featured pottery, woollen fabrics and educational works, and the last featured lace, civil engineering and foreign wines (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Organists from eight European countries played the Royal Albert Hall organ in 1871, a fifty-piece orchestra played daily in 1873, and organ recitals took place daily in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008). A fountain lit by voltaic arcs, a form of electric light, was installed at South Kensington in 1874 (Filipová 2015).

Greenhalgh judges the 1871 and 1874 fine art shows the most impressive of the series. The 1871 show caught French painting before the Franco-Prussian War disrupted it (Greenhalgh 1988). The British definition of art was very wide and included many craft practices, and owners tired of lending works, so the organisers turned to retrospective shows, including four deceased artists (Greenhalgh 1988). Critics found few British artists to praise in 1873, and the foreign work in 1874 was of lower quality, which Greenhalgh ties to reduced foreign interest (Greenhalgh 1988).

Nations, colonies and peoples on show

Greenhalgh argues that empire was displayed continuously across the series. A similar number of colonies and dominions appeared in 1871-1874 as in 1862 (Greenhalgh 1988). The quotation "From 1851 onwards empire was proudly and exhaustively displayed." (Greenhalgh 1988) sums up this reading. Geppert writes that national pavilions were tried at the London show five years after 1867, but says Philadelphia in 1876 was the first to let each nation build its own pavilion, so the London point rests on a single sentence (Geppert 2010). Japan's participation in a London show of 1874 cost 8,932 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999).

Hoffenberg reads the colonial exhibits as trade and status claims. New South Wales funded the collection of exhibits for 1871 and gave free use of telegraph lines and railways (Hoffenberg 2001). The Queensland annexe at South Kensington had enlarged photographs of Aboriginal people across its back wall (Hoffenberg 2001). In 1874 the commissioners proposed a National Museum of the Empire and asked colonial commissioners for life-size figures of Aboriginal inhabitants (Hoffenberg 2001).

The Bombay Committee collected material for 1871, and Indian goods were sold in a bazaar beside the entrance (Hoffenberg 2001). The full-size cast of the Sanchi Tope dominated the Picture Galleries in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg describes casts as a currency exchanged for other items (Hoffenberg 2001). Across the four shows, 8,000 square feet of Indian court space was filled at a total cost of £7,351 (Greenhalgh 1988). Hoffenberg's text says six copies of the Sanchi cast were planned, while a note gives four casts made, three for South Kensington and one for French colleagues (Hoffenberg 2001).

Visitors, receipts and the end of the series

Attendance fell every year. The reference works give 1,142,154 visitors in 1871 and 466,745 in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The 1872 and 1873 figures are 647,190 and 500,033 (Allwood et al. 2001). Gross receipts dropped from £76,433 in 1871 to £16,399 in 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The 1871 profit of £17,671 became a loss of £17,821 by 1874 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the 1871 profit as £30,000 (Allwood et al. 2001), which conflicts with the £17,671 given above (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The decline was visible in the ceremonies. The Prince of Wales and Princess Helena opened the 1871 show in a formal state ceremony. The 1874 show opened with none (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The Practical Magazine called the closing "most inglorious ending of any Industrial, especially any International, Exhibition" (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). In March 1874 the commissioners, on the advice of the Earl of Caernarvon, resolved to end the series unless the first two months of 1874 proved very popular. They did not, and the series ended (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Caernarvon blamed the competition of the Vienna exhibition of 1873 and the Royal Horticultural Society's lack of cooperation (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg says the London shows drew nearly a million visitors a year, against over six million for 1862 (Hoffenberg 2001). That figure does not match the falling counts in the reference works (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

How historians have interpreted it

Dibello, in the London entry of the Findling and Pelle reference works, argues that borrowed Paris policies made the shows cheaper and more educational but less dramatic, and that falling receipts led to the end of the series (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid argue that classifying every exhibit by subject stopped countries from showing their goods with local colour and showmanship, so that the whole show became a dull, almost scientific, display of goods (Allwood et al. 2001). A chapter in the Filipová volume argues that the dry treatment of the shows failed to excite the public (Filipová 2015).

Luckhurst, in a twentieth-century critique, went beyond Cole's guess that arrangement by class may have deterred visitors. Luckhurst argued that it obscured the international character of the displays and gave foreign countries no chance to arrange their own goods (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh argues that organisers were more worried about European and American industry catching up with Britain than about popular political consciousness, and gives public education as the stated rationale (Greenhalgh 1988). Hoffenberg reads the colonial and Indian displays as participatory exchanges through which imperial and national identity were built, rather than as displays imposed from above (Hoffenberg 2001).

The texts agree that the series ended in 1874 after falling attendance and receipts, and that the Vienna exhibition of 1873 was a rival (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). They disagree on the cause. The reference works put it down to the Paris-derived policies and to outside events (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid put it down mainly to classification (Allwood et al. 2001). Giberti mentions the London shows only as comparisons, noting that they adopted signed reports in place of medals, a policy Vienna reversed in 1873 (Giberti 2002).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India

India was the Asian presence the texts describe in most detail. The Bombay Committee collected and forwarded material for the 1871 show, with local figures such as Sir Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy and Dr. Bhau Daji among those involved (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59). Indian commissioners sold Indian manufactures, including shawls, pottery and wood carving, in a bazaar beside the entrance in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 81). Greenhalgh gives the Indian court across the four South Kensington shows as 8,000 square feet, filled at a total cost of £7,351 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Greenhalgh says India was the most carefully displayed possession at the Great Exhibition and remained so through the period at home and abroad (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Watson, who edited the official catalogues for India, is listed as a commissioner at the South Kensington Annual Exhibitions of 1870-74 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285).

The full-size cast of the Sanchi Tope dominated the Picture Galleries in 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 153). Hoffenberg says Cole and colleagues in India intended six copies, three for South Kensington and one each for the French, Prussian and Indian governments (Hoffenberg 2001p. 153). A footnote gives a different figure. Because of financial limits the government of India reduced its contributions, so only four casts were made, three for South Kensington, which contributed 75 per cent of the funds, and one for French colleagues [31, n. 125, l. 17000-17005]. The text and the note therefore give different numbers of casts.

Japan

Japan's presence is recorded only in outline. Hotta-Lister's appendix of Japanese exhibition participation lists a London Annual International Exhibition of 1874, at an expenditure of 8,932 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The texts give no further detail on Japanese goods, visitors or reception at this fair.

China and Southeast Asia

The texts do not mention Chinese or Southeast Asian exhibitors, visitors or observers at the South Kensington series.

Interpretation

Hoffenberg reads the casts as a form of currency. The South Kensington official who asked for them told Lord Mayo that they would make the world acquainted with Indian architecture, and that copies could be exchanged for other exhibition items wanted by museum officers in England and India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 157). Greenhalgh reads the colonial displays of these years as continuous imperial display, noting that a similar number of colonies and dominions appeared in 1871-74 as in 1862 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Classification by subject — goods were grouped by class, not by nation. Cole thought this deterred visitors, and Luckhurst later argued that it hid the international character of the displays (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Attendance and receipts — both fell every year, from about 1.14 million visitors in 1871 to 466,745 in 1874, and the series ended at a loss (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Empire on display — Greenhalgh reads the colonial displays as continuous imperial display, and Hoffenberg reads them as trade and status claims (Greenhalgh 1988; Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Casts and exchange — Indian casts, including the Sanchi Tope, were exchanged for other exhibits and museum objects (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Fine art and music — fine art was a strength in 1871 and 1874 but was weakened by reluctant lenders, and music was a daily attraction (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Awards and juries — no prizes were given, and signed reports replaced medals in the London shows (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Giberti 2002).
  • Blame — Vienna 1873, the Royal Horticultural Society, the Franco-Prussian War and a dispute over the sale of goods were all named as causes (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

Questions for study

  1. The Paris policies were meant to make the shows cheaper and more educational. Did they make the fair less attractive, or were outside events such as the Vienna exhibition of 1873 more to blame?
  2. Greenhalgh reads the series as education and continuous empire, while Hoffenberg reads its colonial displays as trade. Can both readings hold for the same exhibits?
  3. Are the figures for the planned length and the 1871 profit reliable enough to base an argument on? How should a historian handle figures that the sources do not agree on?
  4. Allwood, Allan and Reid say classification by subject made the show dull. Would a display arranged by nation have been more successful, and what evidence in the texts would decide this?
  5. Why did the Indian and colonial governments send material at all, if the series was losing visitors? What did casts and specimens give them in return?

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions treats the four South Kensington seasons of 1871-1874 as one series, beginning with an 1868 proposal to commemorate Prince Albert. Its argument is that the commissioners' policies, taken from the Paris exhibition of 1867 (limited size, no prizes, goods arranged by class), made the shows affordable and educational but also less dramatic, so that attendance and receipts fell every year. Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Historical Dictionary of World’s Fairs and Expositions, 1851–1988. New York: Greenwood Press, 1990.
entry 'London 1871-1874' by Catherine Dibello, running heads pp. 44-47; bibliographic essay at pp. 46-47

Dibello's dictionary entry on the London International Exhibitions of 1871-1874 narrates the four seasons year by year: the halls, the classes of goods, the music and lectures, and the falling receipts. Its argument is that the commissioners' policies, borrowed from the Paris exhibition of 1867, made the shows cheaper but less dramatic, and that falling receipts (from £76,433 in 1871 to £16,399 in 1874) led the commissioners to end the series in 1874. It assigns the failure to several causes: the separation of the two halls by the Horticultural Gardens, the lack of a railway link, the Vienna exhibition of 1873, and the arrangement of goods by class, which Cole thought discouraged visitors and which Kenneth Luckhurst later criticised as obscuring the international character of the displays. It measures the series against the Great Exhibition of 1851 and concludes that the London shows 'never even came close' to it.

Moderate discussion 2

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
p. 36 (running head 'The Big Step Forward & the American Centennial Exhibition'), ll. 1950-1980; appendix table on PDF p. 256 (printed page not visible)

Allwood, Allan and Reid give the London International Exhibitions of 1871-1874 about a page within their account of 1867-76, in a passage on the origins and failure of the series. They argue that the series 'never really got off the ground' because classifying all exhibits by subject made it impossible for countries to show their goods with local colour and showmanship, so that the whole show became a dull, almost scientific, display of goods. They add outside causes: the Franco-Prussian War still being fought in 1871, the Royal Horticultural Society's withdrawal of support in 1872, and the Vienna exhibition of 1873. They close with the cancellation of the 1875 show and an overall loss of £150,000 that the Royal Commissioners had to meet.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
several chapters: ch. 1, 'Origins and conceptual development' (l. 1226); ch. 2, 'Funding, politics and society', pp. 31-32 (ll. 1816-1884); ch. 3, running head 'Imperial display', p. 58 (ll. 3158-3167) and the India court passage (ll. 3216-3237); 'The National Profile', p. 120 (ll. 6396-6401); fine arts, pp. 208-210 (ll. 10802-10891)

Greenhalgh's Ephemeral Vistas covers the 1871-74 South Kensington shows across several chapters, mostly as evidence for wider arguments. The author presents them as a shift from decennial exhibitions to annual, semi-permanent shows with narrower specialist themes, and argues that organisers became more worried about European and American industry catching up with Britain than about popular political consciousness. Education is the stated rationale: the 1874 Official Guide made the instruction of the public the main object, and the colonial displays (a similar number of colonies and dominions in each of 1871-74 as in 1862) are read as proof that empire was displayed continuously, not abandoned in an 'anti-imperial' phase. On fine art the author argues that the wide British definition of art, scarce loans and reliance on retrospectives reduced the shows' impact, and that the 1871 and 1874 shows were the most impressive fine art displays of the series.

Filipová's volume touches the 1871-1874 South Kensington series only briefly. Wikipedia

The Annual International Exhibition was a yearly event held in South Kensington, London, United Kingdom between 1871 and 1874. It was preceded by the 1851 Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations and the 1862 International Exhibition in London, as well as many international exhibitions that been held in various countries since 1851. It was intended to be a series of 10 annual events. The first received over a million visitors and made a profit, but the subsequent three had fewer visitors and all made a loss. Details of the 1873 Exhibition, officially described as the London Annual International Exhibition of all fine arts, industries, and inventions, are quoted in this source.
