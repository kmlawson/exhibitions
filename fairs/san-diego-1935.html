San Diego · 1935–1936

California-Pacific International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The California-Pacific International Exposition ran in Balboa Park, San Diego, from 29 May 1935 to 9 September 1936, in two seasons separated by a winter break (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). San Diego's leaders planned it to revive a city hit by the Depression, and it reused the buildings of the Panama-California Exposition of 1915–16 on the same site (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). Historians use it to study how federal and corporate exhibitors promoted consumer abundance during the New Deal, the federal programmes of the 1930s (Rydell 1993p. 9; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of a failed local plan. In 1931 civic progressives proposed a municipal centennial for 1934, to mark the founding of San Diego's civil government and to celebrate its Mexican heritage. A conservative city council judged it too expensive, and local enthusiasm was thin (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282). The next push came from outside. Frank Drugan, a Scripps-Howard newspaperman who had moved to San Diego after the Long Beach earthquake of 1933, believed a world's fair might promote economic prosperity and lift the city's morale (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282). Drugan went to Washington, where Jesse H. Jones, head of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC), offered a guaranteed note to begin construction if the fair was run by competent staff (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282). In July 1934 San Diego decided to stage a fair to overcome the depression. On 24 July businessmen named it the California-Pacific International Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282). Its backers also planned to use Spanish and Mexican heritage as a theme, with participation from Mexico and Latin American nations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282).

Organisation and finance

The president released $350,000 for the exposition and $125,000 for a special federal building (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). The exposition received $800,000 from the federal government and $1.2 million from private subscribers (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). A second account lists advance ticket sales, exhibitor fees, government appropriations and a local subscription, in which 3,275 people pledged $700,647 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). Frank G. Belcher, a banker, ran the show (Rydell 1993p. 121). Rydell presents Belcher's remark on western dam and water projects as evidence of the link between the fairs and the New Deal (Rydell 1993p. 148).

Site and architecture

The fair reused the Balboa Park site and many of the 1915 buildings, which were lit with the newest technology from the Hollywood film industry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). A mall and an amphitheatre were added to the south-west of the old area (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). Richard Requa, the architect, designed buildings in Pueblo, Aztec and Mayan styles, ending with contemporary industrial architecture. The entry judges that these lacked the dramatic impact of the 1915 structures (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). Rydell describes the new halls as pseudo-Mayan, Aztec and Native American in style, alongside corporate halls built in the Chicago mould (Rydell 1993p. 127). Another account says the Palisades group, which Requa and Juan Larrinaga designed, blended Art Deco with Mesoamerican styles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283).

Benedict argues that only in the 1930s could American fairs draw on New World motifs for official buildings. He cites the Mayan-style Standard Oil and Federal Buildings and the Pueblo Hall of Fame at San Diego (Benedict 1983p. 173). Allwood, Allan and Reid note that many of the buildings left from 1915, including the California State Building, now San Diego's Museum of Man, were still standing for the second fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 94). The encyclopedia lists a Standard Oil Tower and a Federal Building among the Palisades buildings, but the two sources do not say whether they describe the same structures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). The housing exhibits used the same vocabulary at small scale. The most popular of the 56 miniature home models was a Mission Style house with red tile roofs, and a Santa Fe Style house was described as an Americanized version of Pueblo Indian architecture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283).

Exhibits and attractions

The fair had over 400 exhibits from twenty-three countries, several governments and many corporations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). Starr writes that the exhibits "were not particularly distinctive", and that only Ford's exhibit and a miniature village were unique (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). The Palace of Better Housing drew record crowds. Its Modeltown and Modernization Magic exhibits showed federally approved housing designs and the twenty-year mortgages created by the National Housing Act of 1934 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). Ford built the largest and most expensive corporate pavilion (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 187). Its Roads of the Pacific ride re-created the roadside landscape of the world's imperial highways (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 284), and over 500,000 fairgoers test-drove new Ford V-8s over the attraction's roadways (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). The midway included the Zécalo entertainment area, with burlesque shows and Ripley's Believe-It-Or-Not (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283), the Zoro's Garden nudist colony (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 284), and Gold Gulch (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279). A replica of Shakespeare's Old Globe Theatre staged plays in 1935 and 1936 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279). The House of Pacific Relations had fifteen cottages, each with exhibits from a foreign country (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Starr writes that the exhibits were "generally true to their descriptive titles" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278). The encyclopedia says the most popular entertainments, especially with young sailors, were the burlesque shows, Zoro's Garden and the antics of Gold Gulch Gertie, who performed a Lady Godiva ride daily (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). The Zécalo also showed Crime Never Pays, the End-of-the-Trail and a Hollywood Hall of Fame (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). Fair America describes a Midget Village fresh from Chicago, and robots dancing with nude women at Zoro's Garden (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Visitors, money and reception

The texts disagree on attendance. The reference work gives 4,748,811 visitors in the first season and 2,004,000 in the second (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279). A general survey gives 6.75 million for the whole fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and the encyclopedia gives roughly 3.2 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285). The first season left a cash surplus of $400,000 and the second only $44,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286). Starr says the fair did not meet its promoters' expectations, but brought dignitaries, money and thousands of jobs, and helped San Diego climb out of the Depression sooner than most of the nation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279).

Politics and controversy

Local controversy surrounded the entertainments. The Zoro's Garden nudists caused enough local controversy that, according to the encyclopedia, they likely kept a struggling rival newspaper going with front-page copy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285). Queen Zorine, the colony's first leader, resigned, tired of the hypocrisy of local moral leaders and police censors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285). Strip shows on the Zécalo and Gold Gulch led to the resignation of the entire Los Angeles management team (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285). Sally Rand's shows in the Lily Pond in April 1936 chagrined local authorities (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). At the national level, Rydell says Roosevelt endorsed and took part in the succeeding expositions in San Diego (Rydell 1993p. 106), and made personal appearances there (Rydell 1993p. 153).

After returning from Washington, Congressman George Burnham told city leaders that the fair would begin a "cycle of progress" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). By March 1935 Roosevelt himself had shown interest in the fair. He knew San Diego from 1915, when he went with Josephus Daniels to the Panama-California Exposition as assistant secretary of the Navy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). In the 1936 season, after a management shake-up, the Zécalo ran three shows by Fanchon and Marco, which the entry calls more wholesome and family oriented (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283).

Aftermath and legacy

The Ford Motor Company gave its pavilion to the city as an exhibition centre after the fair closed (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). After the 1935 season the Ford lettering on the entrance tower was replaced with the word Transportation, and the exhibits moved to the Dallas fair of 1936 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 189). The fair left permanent buildings in Balboa Park and helped found local institutions, including the Old Globe Theatre and the Starlite Theatre (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279).

Ford's pavilion from the inside

Walter Dorwin Teague designed the Ford Building as a windowless structure, with a circular entrance eighty-eight feet high and a larger, lower circular main building around a central courtyard (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Schrenk says the whole interior was planned as a modern production line. Guides moved visitors along the circular corridor, and recorded voices explained each exhibit so that nobody had to stop and read the captions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188). Juan Larrinaga painted a 450-foot mural along the upper half of the curved inner wall (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188). In the Court of Nations, a revolving hemisphere held a dozen dioramas of automobiles in use in different Pacific nations, the United States and Mexico among them (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 189). Against the statement above that the exhibits moved to Dallas, Schrenk says Ford shifted many of its exhibits there, not all, and built a new pavilion for them (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188p. 189). The Ford Bowl beside the pavilion held outdoor concerts by major orchestras, which the Columbia Broadcasting System often carried (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279).

Money, contracts and records

The fair committee hired Zack Farmer, head of the Los Angeles 1932 Olympic Organizing Committee, to plan the exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 282). The encyclopedia says federal money from the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and the State Emergency Relief Administration helped restore the original Balboa Park buildings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286). The San Diego Public Library holds 33 boxes of official records, including minutes of the managers and the finance committee, and contracts and exhibitor correspondence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286). Starr lists insurance papers, loan subscription papers and invoices among these boxes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 280). The Historical Society's manuscripts, by contrast, are fragments, and virtually none of its files is complete (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279).

How historians have interpreted it

The readings differ mainly in emphasis. Bokovoy, in the encyclopedia, presents the fair as a regional promotion of the California Dream, through which America's vision of itself as a consumer democracy took shape (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286). The entry ties this to the culture of abundance, in which the productive capacity of industry would supply ever more Americans with goods (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). Bokovoy argues that the fair "previewed the American Dream for millions of locals and tourists during a time of economic hardship" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286).

Rydell argues that the depression-era fairs, including San Diego's, were a drive to modernise America by presenting an imperial dream world of abundance, consumption and social hierarchy based on race and gender (Rydell 1993p. 9). Rydell quotes G. Aubrey Davidson, president of the 1915 fair, on the 1935 exposition: "The Exposition of 1935 represents the full flowering of the ideas which motivated our earlier effort" (Rydell 1993p. 9). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say that progress now also meant increased consumer spending, as fair sponsors urged Americans to set aside older values such as thrift (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Samuel places San Diego with Dallas, Cleveland and San Francisco among fairs with similar ideological themes, but argues that the 1939–40 New York World's Fair most enthusiastically celebrated the American way of life steeped in consumerism (Samuel 2007). Hollengreen and colleagues treat Ford's San Diego pavilion as a continuation of its Chicago display, with an entrance tower that recalled Albert Kahn's Chicago rotunda (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 187).

Starr reads the fair mainly through its local results and its shortfall in attendance (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279). Starr's 1990 entry says no thorough history had been written (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 280). The encyclopedia's bibliography lists Bokovoy's 2005 book as a history of both San Diego fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 287). The texts agree that the fair reused the 1915 buildings (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278; Rydell 1993p. 127), that federal money and New Deal support were central (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283; Rydell 1993p. 106), and that Ford's pavilion was a main draw (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 187; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 284). They differ over whether the fair expressed a consumer ideology or a local recovery, and over the size of the crowds. Geppert and Lawn mention the fair only in lists (Geppert 2010p. 316; Lawn 2009p. 14).

Schrenk reads the San Diego fair as part of a Chicago pattern. San Diego, Dallas, Cleveland, San Francisco and New York all held major expositions in the late 1930s, hoping to copy Chicago's success and revive their own local economies (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 186). For Schrenk, rides and other entertaining features replaced exhibits that taught visitors how products were made, and this shaped corporate pavilions at the later fairs (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 181). This local-recovery reading sits closer to Starr's account of San Diego's results (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279) than to Bokovoy's California Dream (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 286).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Our texts say very little about an Asian presence at the San Diego fair. The one Asian place they name is China, and only as one section of Ford's Roads of the Pacific attraction. This was a Pacific version of the Chicago Roads of the World, made up of fourteen sections of Pacific Rim roads, which included the Summer Palace Road in China (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Ford sponsored the attraction, which stood behind its pavilion (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 189). One text describes the Roads of the Pacific as recounting the imperial history of the Americas (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285). The House of Pacific Relations had fifteen cottages, each with exhibits from a foreign country (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). No text names an Asian country among them, and another says the house was manned by representatives of foreign countries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 279). The texts do not say who built the Chinese road section, what visitors thought of it, or whether any Asian commissioners or visitors attended the fair.

Schrenk's Court of Nations names only the United States and Mexico among the Pacific nations whose automobiles appear in its dioramas (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 189). The Roads of the Pacific had fourteen sections, among them the Summer Palace Road in China, so the Chinese section was one road among fourteen (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). The encyclopedia says the House of Pacific Relations was created by Frank Drugan and meant to promote world peace (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283). None of the entries that describe the House names the countries whose cottages it held.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Federal money and the New Deal — Federal funds and Roosevelt's backing tie the fair to the federal programmes of the 1930s (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283; Rydell 1993p. 106).
  • Reused Balboa Park buildings — The fair's low costs and its 1915 buildings are stressed by the reference works and by a general survey (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • New World architecture — Requa's Pueblo, Aztec and Mayan designs and the Palisades group are described as a new style for official buildings (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 278; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283; Benedict 1983p. 173).
  • Corporate display — Ford's pavilion and its roads and concerts show corporate display at the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 187).
  • Housing and federal programmes — The Palace of Better Housing and its Modeltown exhibits showed federally backed housing designs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283).
  • Consumer abundance — The texts link the fair to a culture of abundance and consumer spending (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 283; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Samuel 2007).
  • Popular entertainment and controversy — Nudist, burlesque and fan-dance shows drew crowds and local disputes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 285; Rydell 1993p. 137).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the fair's organisers reuse the 1915 buildings, and how did that choice shape the way San Diego presented itself?
  2. How much did federal New Deal money change the meaning of a locally promoted fair?
  3. Are the Zoro's Garden and Zécalo entertainments evidence of consumer culture, or simply a local commercial matter?
  4. The attendance figures in our sources disagree. How should a student weigh a reference work against a general survey?
  5. Ford's pavilions are described as corporate promotion. Does the account of the Roads of the Pacific support or complicate that reading?
  6. How well does the consumer-abundance reading of Bokovoy and Rydell fit the more local reading of Starr?
  7. Schrenk treats San Diego as a local copy of Chicago, while the encyclopedia treats the fair as a regional form of the California Dream. Which reading better explains the Zécalo and Zoro's Garden entertainments?
  8. The encyclopedia says the House of Pacific Relations was meant to promote world peace, yet no text names an Asian country among its cottages. What kinds of record would show which nations exhibited there?
  9. Starr calls the exhibits generally true to their descriptive titles, but Ford's pavilion was a main draw. Did Ford's exhibits differ in kind from the other corporate and state displays, or only in their showmanship?

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
3.3Fordism, Corporate Display, and the American Expositions of the 1930sLisa D. Schrenkp. 181

Schrenk examines how American automotive companies changed their displays at world's fairs in the 1930s, when exhibits that taught visitors about production gave way to pavilions built to reinforce brand recognition. Ford and General Motors competed for visitors through increasingly theatrical buildings, often designed by architects such as Albert Kahn and by industrial designers such as Walter Dorwin Teague. The chapter follows the rivalry through the 1933-34 Century of Progress in Chicago, where GM's Chevrolet assembly line and Ford's 1934 rotunda, with its photomurals, dioramas and rotating globes, were the main attractions, and through Ford's pavilions at San Diego in 1935 and Dallas in 1936, which reused Chicago's displays. It then turns to the 1939 New York fair, where GM's Futurama, designed by Norman Bel Geddes, outdid Ford's Cyclorama of Production. The chapter concludes that the factory had become entertainment by the 1930s, and that the rivalry between the two companies carried on at later fairs.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
Introductionp. 1

Rydell opens with the depression-era world's fairs, which drew nearly one hundred million Americans to Chicago, San Diego, Dallas, Cleveland, San Francisco and New York, and asks why people paid to visit something so ephemeral while unemployment and political instability were so severe. Expositions, he argues, had long deflected criticism of the social order, and the interwar fairs, in modernistic dress, were built to restore faith in government, business, science and intellectual leaders. Their promise was of abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy, which he presents as continuous with the Victorian fairs rather than a break from them. The chapters take up colonial modernism, eugenics, the scientists' campaign to popularise science, a consumption-oriented welfare state, African American resistance and the American pavilion at Brussels in 1958. Rydell concludes that the century-of-progress expositions were theatres of power that gave a depression a sense of meaning.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
3Fairs between the World Warsp. 72

Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that fairs between the world wars turned to science, corporate pavilions and the Depression economy, and that they kept African Americans at the margins. The chapter opens with the 1920s boom and Philadelphia's Sesqui-Centennial of 1926, a historical fair that opened unfinished in the rain, and with Long Beach's 1928 fair, built around a North African theme. Its central case is Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, where modernist buildings, Sally Rand's fan dance and a Hall of Science drew crowds, while demeaning attractions and a poorly attended Negro Day marked its treatment of black visitors. The authors then cover San Diego in 1935-36, the regional fairs in Dallas and Cleveland in 1936, where the Dallas Hall of Negro Life was hidden behind shrubbery, and the New York and Golden Gate fairs of 1939-40, with General Motors' Futurama and the Trylon and Perisphere. The evidence comes from fair publicity, press reports and official histories. The chapter concludes that these fairs offered hope in hard times, and suggests that they may help explain why the United States avoided political revolution in the 1930s.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Wikipedia

The California Pacific International Exposition was an exposition held in San Diego, California, during May 29, 1935–November 11, 1935 and February 12, 1936–September 9, 1936. The exposition was held in Balboa Park, San Diego's large central urban park, which had also been the site of the earlier Panama–California Exposition in 1915. The Exposition was held to promote San Diego and support its economy, which had slowed with the country's Great Depression. The first year was such a financial and attendance success that it was held over for a second year. The exposition had hundreds of exhibits on history, the arts, horticulture, ethnic cultures, science, and industry. Some concessions and exhibits were unusual, such as the Gold Gulch, Lost Continent of Mu, Zoro Garden Nudist Colony, and the "One Ton Mechanical Man."
