Background and organisers

In the summer and fall of 1922 government authorities ran two campaigns to prepare Koreans for the semiannual cleanups (Henry 2014p. 162). The first was a traveling display by the Osaka pharmaceutical firm Jintan, held in July (Henry 2014p. 162). The October exhibition was the second (Henry 2014p. 162). Government-General officials, with their counterparts in the Kyŏnggi Prefectural Office and the Korean Red Cross, allocated 2,500 yen to host it (Henry 2014p. 162). It showed specimens, charts and pictures on individual hygiene and on contagious and venereal diseases. Organisers added films in the evening, and admission to the exhibition and the films was free (Henry 2014p. 162). The text gives no attendance figure for it (Henry 2014p. 162).

Reception and politics

The government sponsored the event, but the Tonga ilbo, in whose pages cultural nationalists criticised public health under Japanese rule, promoted it as a chance for Koreans to learn about hygienic modernity (Henry 2014p. 162). A reporter wrote: "There are explanations attached [to the display items] so that even an average person [pot’ong saram] can understand clearly. This is a very much needed exhibition to promote hygienic attitudes." (Henry 2014p. 162). Cooperative leaders hoped such events would promote "a more egalitarian form of sanitary welfare" (Henry 2014p. 161), but the events let officials target Koreans as objects of medical surveillance through inspections and free vaccinations (Henry 2014p. 161).

Its place in the series

The text places the October event within the Tonga ilbo's support for government-led exhibitions, which it says continued more than a decade later (Henry 2014p. 162). It describes a CASC hygiene exhibition held from 2 to 7 May 1932 (Henry 2014p. 164), and a Disease Prevention Exhibition held from 9 to 15 August 1933 that drew nearly eighty thousand people, most of them Korean (Henry 2014p. 163). The text does not say that the 1933 event followed from the 1922 one. A government hygiene exhibition at Pagoda Park in early July 1921 is a separate event, given in a note (Henry 2014p. 253). The footnotes date the allocation to KI 19 (May 1923): 5 (Henry 2014p. 162p. 253), and the notice of free admission and films to the Tonga ilbo of 28 September 1922, a date before the October exhibition (Henry 2014p. 162p. 253).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Hygienic modernity — the government sponsored the exhibition, but the Tonga ilbo presented it as a way for Koreans to learn hygienic modernity (Henry 2014p. 162).
  • Civic assimilation and surveillance — officials used such events to monitor residents' health, while cooperative leaders hoped they would promote a more egalitarian sanitary welfare (Henry 2014p. 161).
  • Press and cultural nationalism — the Tonga ilbo, whose pages carried cultural nationalist critiques of public health, backed government-led exhibitions and framed them as part of its own campaigns (Henry 2014p. 162).
  • Epidemic control — the second-half 1922 events were introduced against a smallpox outbreak and police inspections of Korean homes (Henry 2014p. 161).

Henry treats the October 1922 hygiene exhibition in one paragraph, as the second of two government-led hygiene campaigns of summer and fall 1922 that prepared Koreans for the semiannual neighbourhood cleanups. The text gives the organisers, the funding and the content, but no venue and no attendance figure. Henry uses the event to show how the Tonga ilbo, a Korean newspaper in whose pages cultural nationalists criticised colonial public health, promoted a government exhibition as a way for Koreans to learn hygienic modernity. The passage supports the chapter's larger argument that hygiene events both drew Koreans into self-regulation and gave the colonial state a means to monitor their bodies.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
