Seoul · 1933

Contagious Disease Prevention Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The Contagious Disease Prevention Exhibition was a government hygiene show held in Keijō (Seoul) from 9 to 15 August 1933 (Henry 2014p. 163). The Government-General, the city government and Kyŏnggi Province jointly hosted it (Henry 2014p. 162). It featured sixteen buildings displaying over twelve thousand items and drew nearly eighty thousand people, most of them Korean (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163). Henry reads it as one episode in the colonial effort to bring Korean residents under a regime of self-regulated sanitary practice (Henry 2014p. 163).

Background and aims

Henry's chapter on civic assimilation asks how colonial officials and Korean elites used hygiene to shape the bodily habits of Keijō's Korean neighbourhoods (Henry 2014p. 131). Semiannual cleanups, hygiene exhibitions and other media events were the means, and sanitation cooperatives the organisational base (Henry 2014p. 131). The 1933 exhibition belongs to this late phase of the campaign, which the text describes as local campaigns of cultural rule (Henry 2014p. 164).

Organisers and site

The Government-General, the city government and the government of Kyŏnggi Province jointly hosted the event, which Henry calls a major event (Henry 2014p. 162; Henry 2014p. 163). The text names it the Disease Prevention Exhibition in one place, and the Contagious Disease Exhibition of 1933 and the Contagious Disease Prevention Exhibition in others (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 164). Its grounds were the meeting grounds of Susong Common School, a public institution attended mostly by Koreans from nearby Chongno (Henry 2014p. 163). The book's index gives the school as Susong Normal School (Henry 2014p. 297). The Central Association of Sanitation Cooperatives (CASC), founded in June 1925, took part in the programme (Henry 2014p. 159; Henry 2014p. 159).

Exhibits and activities

The displays were instructional, and the event offered free physical examinations and inspections of food and drink on the site (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163). The surrounding disease prevention week extended the programme into homes and streets. Inspectors made home visits and gave free medicine to indigent Koreans, the CASC hired fifty labourers to disinfect toilets, and organisers held a special fly-catching day (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163). Police officers, working with local agents, cleaned streets, disinfected wells and disposed of trash (Henry 2014p. 163).

Visitors and reception

Henry says the evidence on how colonised spectators received the exhibition is hard to assess (Henry 2014p. 163). He cites congratulatory reports in the Korean-language press and in official documents, which said viewers gained "renewed appreciation for the gravity of deadly infections" while more than seven hundred people had died during the Contagious Disease Prevention Week (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163). He also notes that some Koreans took sick neighbours to police stations and emergency consultation rooms, which he calls unusual, since many poorer residents avoided impersonal quarantine (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163). Henry concludes that the exhibition and the week drew wary Koreans into "the regime of sanitary rule", chiefly through a few self-regulating individuals (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163).

Earlier and later hygiene exhibitions

The 1933 event belonged to a series. In July 1922 a travelling hygiene exhibition sponsored by the Osaka pharmaceutical company Jintan opened on 23 July with over three thousand spectators that morning (Henry 2014p. 162; Henry 2014p. 162). In October 1922 Government-General officials, with the Kyŏnggi Prefectural Office and the Korean Red Cross, spent 2,500 yen on another hygiene exhibition with films in the evenings (Henry 2014p. 162; Henry 2014p. 162). In 1932 the CASC held a hygiene exhibition from 2 to 7 May, at which it gave awards to Koreans who had best cleaned toilets, sewers, kitchens and dustbins. A CASC film on communicable illness had first been shown at Susong Common School, the site of the 1933 event (Henry 2014p. 164; Henry 2014p. 164; Henry 2014p. 164; Henry 2014p. 164).

The colonial setting

Korean elites shared the hygiene agenda but sought different ends. The Tonga ilbo, which carried cultural nationalist critiques of public health, was still endorsing government-led exhibitions and films in 1933 (Henry 2014p. 162). Henry argues that the cultural-rule strategies which allowed some Korean redeployment gave way over time (Henry 2014p. 167). In 1935 officials faced another epidemic, the deadliest since 1928 (Henry 2014p. 167). They then announced a campaign to make enlarged Keijō a "hygienic city" and introduced a controversial income-based waste fee on all households (Henry 2014p. 167; Henry 2014p. 167). The Waste Disposal Ordinance of 1936 sent cleanup teams to the homes of noncompliant residents, who had to pay for the work (Henry 2014p. 167; Henry 2014p. 167).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Sanitary rule — the exhibition and week are read as ways of binding residents to colonial hygiene regulation through self-regulating individuals (Henry 2014p. 163).
  • Exhibitions as hygiene propaganda — displays, free examinations and home visits combined to take hygiene into homes and streets (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163).
  • Korean elites and the press — the Tonga ilbo endorsed government events while nationalist writers pressed for more equal services (Henry 2014p. 162; Henry 2014p. 147).
  • Costs and enforcement — by 1935 the colonial state moved from exhortation toward fees and forced cleanups (Henry 2014p. 167; Henry 2014p. 167).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the 1933 organisers pair displays with free examinations and home visits, and how does this differ from the 1922 exhibitions in the same city (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 162)?
  2. Henry's claim that the events drew wary Koreans into sanitary rule rests on congratulatory reports. What other evidence would test it (Henry 2014p. 163; Henry 2014p. 163)?
  3. The index calls the school Susong Normal School, while the body text calls it Susong Common School. Which does the evidence support (Henry 2014p. 297; Henry 2014p. 163)?
  4. How far did the 1935 fee and the 1936 ordinance change the relation between Korean elites and the colonial state (Henry 2014p. 167; Henry 2014p. 167)?

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
4Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijōp. 130Asia

Henry examines how the Government-General and Korean elites used public hygiene in the Korean neighbourhoods of Keijo to build what the author calls 'civic assimilation', a habit of self-regulation that would bind residents to colonial norms of cleanliness. The chapter traces the dual surveillance of sanitation cooperatives and police from the protectorate years through the 1910s, when semiannual cleanups were enforced through fines, home inspections and roadside lectures, and shows how residents evaded these measures or tidied up only before inspectors came. Henry argues that the system failed poorer Koreans, who could not pay fees, relied on well water and lacked toilets, so that colonial and nationalist critics alike used disease statistics to make claims about the city. The chapter also follows Korean cooperative leaders, medical students and the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition, which drew nearly eighty thousand visitors and seems to have drawn wary residents into sanitary rule. It concludes that after 1935 the colonial state turned to harsher, centralised measures, narrowing the room for Korean elites to reshape assimilation.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
