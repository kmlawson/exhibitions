International Colonial, Maritime and Flemish Art Exposition

Exposition Internationale Coloniale, Maritime et d'Art Flamand

The International Colonial, Maritime and Flemish Art Exposition was held at Antwerp in 1930, the year Belgium marked the centenary of its independence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). It was one exposition on two sites: Antwerp, with its colonial, maritime and Flemish art displays, and Liège, with science, industry, social economy, agriculture and music (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). The texts dwell mainly on its colonial displays, which Greenhalgh argues deserve more attention than they have received (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and the centenary

Rivalry between cities shaped the fair. Brussels had staged the most impressive earlier expositions, and Antwerp and Liège decided it was their turn to host the celebrations. Brussels built the Heysel Stadium for the anniversary, and the two cities divided the exposition between them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258).

The texts give different accounts of the centenary itself. The reference works place it as the centenary of independence from Spain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258). Allwood and co-authors describe it as independence from Dutch rule (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107). The texts do not explain the difference, and this essay cannot settle it.

The texts also differ on how many exhibitions there were. Rydell describes two expositions that the Belgian government opened to mark the centenary (Rydell 1993p. 68). Greenhalgh and the two reference works treat them as one event held on two sites (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258).

Belgian imperial display was already established before 1930. The government had taken care to exhibit its empire at every exposition from 1900 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). Greenhalgh lists the Belgian fairs with extensive imperial sections as Brussels in 1897 and 1910, Liège in 1905 and 1930, and Antwerp in 1894 and 1930 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71).

Site, organisation and finance

Antwerp was by far the bigger and more lavish of the two venues (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). The reference works also call it the more successful site, because its Flemish art display was a tremendous attraction and its colonial sections dominated the official literature (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258).

Emile Dignefer was president of the exposition, and Baron Delvaux de Feuffe its special commissioner (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 391). The British commissioner general for the Antwerp exhibition of 1930 was Sir Henry Cole (1870-1932), according to the biographical appendix in Geppert's book (Geppert 2010p. 265).

Allwood and co-authors give the site as 170.5 acres, the loss as 7 million francs, and the period of opening as six months (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Imperial display: the Avenue de la Colonie and the Palais du Congo

Britain, France, the Netherlands, Italy and Portugal built substantial pavilions along the Avenue de la Colonie. Pride of place went to the Belgian Palais du Congo, and colonial villages with peoples from conquered lands were spread across the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). Greenhalgh calls the Antwerp colonial display the most ambitious Belgian imperial effort and the largest held outside Britain or France (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). The Encyclopedia entry, also by Greenhalgh, calls it "the most lavish display of empire ever held on Belgian soil" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257).

Greenhalgh describes the Palais du Congo as having a curiously hybrid appearance (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). The exhibition's General Report, which Greenhalgh quotes, explained: "Externally, the pavilion has a more oriental than Congolese character" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Greenhalgh argues that the architect judged the Congo could not supply a suitable form for the building. He used Congolese motifs only as decorative trimmings, and African building forms in exhibition architecture served as stage sets that carried visitors away from northern Europe (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72).

The British pavilion was enormous and built in the style of Wembley and Chicago (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107). Italy took part in both the Antwerp and Liège shows, together with 22 other states (Filipová 2015p. 316). Its pavilion, designed by the architect Giovanni Chevalley, held objects from the Colonial Museum of Rome and stood near the main entrance (Filipová 2015p. 316). Arena compares it with the Dutch, French, Persian and Belgian Congo pavilions and says that "the Italian attempt to impose a reminder of the Roman era was evident" (Filipová 2015p. 316).

Greenhalgh says the French took part at Antwerp mainly as a courtesy to the Belgians, since the Paris colonial exhibition was less than a year away (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). The Portuguese decided late and were allowed only a very small building (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). Taken together, Greenhalgh says, the colonial sections were large and showed the Belgian public the riches that imperial exploits had brought (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). The Dutch pavilion was the most unusual of the large national buildings, designed in what Greenhalgh describes as a late and eccentric version of art nouveau (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72).

Flemish art and modern architecture

The Flemish art display of the previous five centuries was, according to the Encyclopedia entry, a tremendous attraction throughout Europe (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). It was complemented by Vieille Belgique, a reconstructed medieval city that had become a standard feature of Belgian fairs by 1930 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). The Finnish, Norwegian, Dutch and Italian pavilions were modern, and so was the Pavillon des Villes Hanséatiques, sponsored mainly by Bremen and Hamburg (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). Greenhalgh writes that the Hanseatic pavilion has, in hindsight, an air of tragedy, because its international style was proscribed in the National Socialist pogroms (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). The Italian pavilion was one of the last pieces of modernism to come out of Italy with government approval. By 1937 Mussolini had firmly harnessed classicism to Italian fascism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257).

Marcel Schmitz, an architect and writer chosen to report on the site's architecture, saw a clear benefit for modern architecture in Belgium (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). The Belgian-built Decorative Arts Palace, Pavilion of the City of Antwerp and Electricity Pavilion were full-blooded examples of international-style modernism, with steel frames, concrete cladding and white walls (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). The entry compares this architecture with the Stuttgart Weissenhof housing exhibition of 1927 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259).

The Historical Dictionary entry says the Flemish art exhibition was the most complete and impressive since the Brussels exposition of 1897, and that it included examples from many European collections (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258). Allwood and co-authors describe the architecture as ranging from mud wall to Art Deco (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107). That sits uneasily with the modernist emphasis of the Dictionary entry's account of the pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259).

Visitors and reception

The official reports did not record attendance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259). Greenhalgh judges that both sites had good attendance and were officially considered successful (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). The Encyclopedia entry argues that the large sums the British, French, Italians and Portuguese spent on their contributions show that they expected large audiences (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258).

Liège had more of the air of a trade fair, stripped of the colourful features normally expected at such events, but it remains significant for international participation and trade (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). The Times of London published several articles on the joint exposition in 1930 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258).

Politics, rivalry and the fair circuit

Under an agreement made in Paris in 1928, two universal expositions could not be held in the same country within a decade. The Brussels 1935 entry in the Dictionary says the Antwerp-Liège exposition of 1930 should have kept Belgium from holding another until 1942. Rivalry among Belgian cities led Brussels to apply for permission for 1935, and the Bureau of International Expositions allowed it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 275).

Geppert argues that Britain, which had accepted the French invitation to the Paris colonial exhibition of 1931, withdrew to a large extent and preferred to contribute to the Antwerp exhibition of 1930 instead (Geppert 2010p. 194). Britain feared appearing diminished at a French fair, where only a building the size of the Wembley Government Pavilion would do justice to Great Britain (Geppert 2010p. 194).

Geppert reports that some invited nations cited the rapid succession of comparable events, among them Antwerp, Barcelona and Liège (Geppert 2010p. 181). Geppert lists an Antwerp fair under 1926 as well as this one under 1930. Hale, discussing competition between imperial powers at fairs, notes that the Belgians also sponsored events at the 1930 Antwerp exposition (Hale 2008p. 168).

Classified exhibits and prizes

Greenhalgh says the Antwerp organisers took a more rigorously rational approach to showing empire than many earlier fairs, and that this was unusual after the First World War. He compares it with British Victorian exhibitions and the Paris show of 1867 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Visiting nations were asked to fill a fixed list of categories: the history of colonisation, geography and cartography, the indigenous population and politics, administration and the judiciary, medicine and hygiene, missions, labour, civil engineering, transport, commerce, finance, agriculture, mining and diverse products (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Greenhalgh cannot show that every nation followed the list, but prizes were awarded for the best display in each subject area, so he thinks they probably did (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72).

Britain's display and the Paris question

The British section was the one Greenhalgh singles out for display technique. He quotes the General Report on its "a parade of dioramas, models and electrically lit maps", which the report said needed no effort from the visitor to understand (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). By 1930 such methods were common, and Greenhalgh judges the British to have been their early masters (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73).

Greenhalgh writes that Britain, aware it would not take part in the Paris colonial exhibition, made a special effort at Antwerp (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Geppert gives a different sequence. He says Britain first accepted France's invitation, which led the French to set aside a large site at Vincennes, and then withdrew to a large extent in favour of Antwerp (Geppert 2010p. 194). Greenhalgh's wording suggests Britain had already decided against Paris, while Geppert says it accepted and then withdrew. The texts do not reconcile the two.

The Belgian Village and its afterlife

The Belgian Village was a reconstructed walled town of Flemish type. Samuel says it was designed in 1930 and shown in Antwerp, then built for the Chicago fair of 1933 to 1934 and again for Brussels in 1958 (Samuel 2007). He does not name the Antwerp event, so the link to this fair rests on that date and place. The Chicago guide called it "a meticulous copy of a walled Flemish village as it might have appeared in 1800" (Samuel 2007). Samuel describes at least a hundred houses, a replica of the Gothic church of Saint Nicholas in Antwerp, a town hall with an underground rathskeller, forty shops, a canal and an arched stone bridge (Samuel 2007). Work stopped for a week in May when the money ran out, and the village was finished only in October, just before the first season closed (Samuel 2007). Samuel says the investors lost heavily (Samuel 2007). Ganz lists the Belgian Village among the ethnic villages of 1933 and says it still operated in 1934, with more villages added (Ganz 2008).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh's reading is the most developed. In Ephemeral Vistas, Greenhalgh treats the Antwerp colonial display as the most ambitious Belgian imperial effort, deserving more study, and reads its architecture as the use of African forms for decoration and stage effect (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). In both reference entries, Greenhalgh describes the colonial sections as "a spectacular, exotic expression of European imperialism" that showed how far empire had come to grip the economy and imagination of Belgian government and population (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258). Arena reads the Italian pavilion differently. For Arena, Italy's participation was part of an intensified colonial propaganda project and a move against other colonial powers, by a small colonial regime that tried to make up for its size (Filipová 2015p. 316). Arena's focus is Italian propaganda and the display of colonial economies.

Rydell argues that European imperialists presented modernism and colonialism as two sides of the same coin. In this account the Palais du Congo was meant to be the centrepiece of Antwerp, but its displays were overshadowed by the British Empire dioramas and by the modernist Dutch, Italian, Norwegian and Finnish pavilions (Rydell 1993p. 69). Rydell also describes two expositions where Greenhalgh describes one, a disagreement the sources leave open (Rydell 1993p. 68; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71).

Allwood and co-authors give only a short description, placing the show within the centenary and noting its native village and British pavilion (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107). Geppert's account of Britain's choice of Antwerp over Paris shows how the fairs of the period were weighed against one another (Geppert 2010p. 194).

Arena also treats the Antwerp exhibition as a programme for raising the standing of the colonising nations. He says its national pavilions carried activities meant to enhance each nation's political, social and economic status and to stimulate commercial traffic (Filipová 2015p. 316).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Belgian centenary and city rivalry — The fair was a centenary event, and its division between Antwerp and Liège came from rivalry over which city should host the celebrations (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • The Avenue de la Colonie and the Palais du Congo — Greenhalgh treats the colonial sections as the core of the fair, with the Palais du Congo as its main building (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Empire as spectacle — The reference entries read the colonial sections as a demonstration of imperial power, and Greenhalgh reads the architecture as decoration and stage set (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Imperial rivalry and the fair circuit — Britain's preference for Antwerp over Paris, and complaints about the rapid succession of events, show powers measuring their displays against one another (Geppert 2010; Hale 2008).
  • Italian colonial propaganda — Arena reads the Italian pavilion as part of a small colonial regime's propaganda (Filipová 2015).
  • Modernist architecture — Pavilions from Finland, Norway, the Netherlands, Italy and the Hanseatic cities, and the Belgian buildings, set modernism beside colonial display (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Rydell 1993).
  • Visitors and money — The official reports did not record attendance, but the reference works judge the fair successful and well attended (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Questions for study

  1. Why did a single centenary celebration split between two cities, and how did that rivalry shape what each site could show?
  2. Greenhalgh makes the colonial sections central to the fair, while Rydell describes two expositions in which modernism was the most striking element. What evidence would decide between these readings?
  3. The Palais du Congo was meant to be the centrepiece, yet its displays were overshadowed by modernist pavilions and British dioramas. What does this suggest about how organisers combined modern architecture with imperial display?
  4. Britain turned from Paris to Antwerp. How far was Antwerp a substitute for Paris, and what does the choice say about competition among colonial powers?
  5. The official reports did not record attendance. How should historians judge a fair's success without reliable visitor figures?
  6. Arena reads the Italian pavilion as propaganda by a small colonial regime. How should historians weigh organisers' propaganda aims against what visitors actually saw?
  7. Greenhalgh and Geppert give different accounts of Britain's choice between Paris and Antwerp. Which records, such as the British official papers or the French invitations, would show which account is right?
  8. Samuel's Belgian Village went from Antwerp to Chicago and later to Brussels. What does the reuse of one reconstruction across fairs suggest about how exhibitors used ethnic and imperial displays?
  9. Greenhalgh describes a fixed list of colonial categories at Antwerp. How far could prize records show that the visiting nations followed it?

14The Last Exhibition of the Italian Colonial Empire: Naples 1938–40Giovanni Arenap. 313

Arena examines the Mostra Triennale delle Terre Italiane d'Oltremare, a colonial exhibition planned for Naples that opened on 9 May 1940, less than a month before Italy entered the Second World War and the show closed. He places it in a sequence of Italian colonial shows that begins with Tripoli's trade fair of 1927 and the Turin colonial exhibition of 1928, and continues with the Antwerp colonial exposition of 1930, where the regime stressed imperial Rome and modernised colonial economies. The Naples site in the Fuorigrotta district combined pavilions, gardens planted with colonial species and a plan meant to renew the city, while its historical, geographical and manufacturing sections used photomontages, ethnographic recreations and a 46-metre Fascist Party tower. Arena draws on the bylaws, the artists involved, especially Melkiorre Melis and his Libya pavilion café, and archival drawings. He concludes that the propaganda depended on the close integration of architecture and art, and that much of that decoration was destroyed in the bombing of the war.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Geppert's study of the 1931 Paris colonial exhibition uses Antwerp 1930 as a point of comparison. The author explains that Britain, which had accepted the French invitation, withdrew to a large extent and chose to contribute to the Antwerp exhibition of 1930 instead, having lost faith in a project that kept being postponed and fearing that its showing in Paris would look diminished. Geppert also lists Antwerp among the rapid succession of comparable events that some invited nations cited when hesitating over Paris. The Antwerp exhibition itself is not analysed.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. Wikipedia

The International Exposition of Colonial, Maritime and Flemish Art (French: Exposition internationale coloniale, maritime et d'art flamand) was a world's fair held in Antwerp in 1930, in conjunction with another Belgian exhibition at the same time, the Exposition of 1930 in Liège, to mark the centenary of Belgian independence. It was opened on 26 April 1930 by King Albert and Queen Elisabeth, and closed on 5 November. There were 468,323 visitors.
