In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Hotta-Lister mentions the plan in a single sentence, in an appendix on Japan's participation in exhibitions (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 203; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The author says the plan was inspired by the celebration of the Japanese nation's 2,550 years anniversary in 1890, and was proposed in 1889 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The text gives no title for the plan, so the name used in this record is the timeline's, not the text's.

The passage sits under a heading on Japan as host of an international exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). The author links it to Japan's ambitions: as Japan aspired to become a world power, it was willing to host international exhibitions (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). The plan was dropped because "the time was not yet ripe for Japan to host such a venture" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Its place in the series

The passage turns next to the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903. The author notes that it had eighteen participating countries and some 9,570 foreign visitors by hotel registers, but was "too small a scale" to count as a front rank international exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Later, the government proposed a larger international show for Tokyo. A proposal of April 1907 for 1912, named Nippon Dai Hakuran Kai, was postponed to 1917 in early 1908 because of financial difficulties after the 1904-5 war with Russia, then postponed indefinitely in 1912 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The text says that after the Great War began in 1914 the scheme was finally abandoned, with its committee office dissolved (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The text does not connect the 1889 plan with these later schemes (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Japan as host — the author ties the 1889 plan to Japan's wish to become a world power and to host international exhibitions (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216).
  • Abandoned plans — the 1889 plan and the 1907 Tokyo scheme both fell away: the first because the time was not yet ripe, the second through financial difficulty after the war with Russia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).
  • Osaka 1903 as a yardstick — the text measures the 1903 domestic show by its foreign participation and visitors and finds it short of a front rank international exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Questions for study

  1. The text calls the 1889 plan a "venture" but gives no site, organiser or list of participants. What would a source need to say before the plan could count as an international exhibition?
  2. The 1889 plan was dropped for lack of readiness, and the 1907 scheme for lack of money. Which reason better explains why Japan's early plans failed?
  3. Why might a history of the 1910 Anglo-Japanese show include proposals that never came to anything?
  4. How far does the Osaka show of 1903, with its foreign visitors counted from hotel registers, meet the author's standard for a front rank exhibition?

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.
