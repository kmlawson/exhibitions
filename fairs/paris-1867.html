Paris Universal Exposition

Exposition universelle de 1867
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle of 1867 was the international exhibition that Napoleon III staged on the Champ de Mars in Paris, opening on 1 May 1867 (Allwood et al. 2001). Exhibits from 49 nations filled an elliptical iron-and-glass palace (Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967), and for the first time each nation was invited to build its own pavilion (Benedict 1983; Badger 1979). Historians use the fair to test whether world's fairs were encyclopedic lessons in progress and social harmony, or had already become entertainment and spectacle (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Geppert 2010).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Napoleon III ordered the fair by an Imperial Decree of 22 June 1863 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Our sources present it as a reply to the London International Exhibition of 1862. Victor Hugo wrote the introduction to the Paris Guide, and other writers were enlisted to promote the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990). Kaiser argues that France used the Paris exhibitions of 1855 and 1867 to consolidate the Second Empire, the regime of Napoleon III, and to compete with Britain's industrial lead (Kaiser).

The aim was encyclopedic. Barth reads the fair as an attempt at a complete inventory of the world, in which classification by object and by nation expressed the Second Empire's faith in progress and social harmony (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh describes a total view of the world, presented as education (Greenhalgh 1988). Le Play was the central planner: Barth gives Le Play the title commissioner general (Findling & Pelle 2008), Allwood, Allan and Reid call Le Play Director (Allwood et al. 2001), and Chandler credits Le Play with conceiving the palace (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Organisation and finance

The French parliament chose the Champ de Mars as the site on 29 June 1865, after twenty-two sites had been proposed (Findling & Pelle 2008). The budget was put at 20 million francs. The national government and the city of Paris each gave 6 million, and a private Association de Garantie sold shares for a further 8 million (Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967).

The texts disagree on the balance. Barth reports a profit of 3,130,000 francs (Findling & Pelle 2008), and Roche's table also lists a profit (Roche 2000). Allwood, Allan and Reid report a loss of 2.88 million francs, about £115,000 (Allwood et al. 2001).

The classification ran to ten groups, I to X (Findling & Pelle 1990). Chandler links Group X, on the physical and moral improvement of the people, to Bonapartist reform (Findling & Pelle 1990). Giberti describes a dual system, with nations in radial sections and classes in concentric rings, a plan proposed by Prince Napoleon in 1855 (Giberti 2002).

Site and architecture

The main building was the palace designed by Jean-Baptiste-Sebastien Krantz. Barth gives it 40.97 acres of the 113.51-acre Champ de Mars, with seven concentric elliptical rings, each housing one group (Findling & Pelle 2008). Giberti gives the building as thirty-nine acres (Giberti 2002). The texts also differ on the whole site: 113.51 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008), 165 acres with a 50-acre annex (Allwood et al. 2001), and 215 acres in the statistical appendix of (Findling & Pelle 1990). Chandler says Krantz contracted Gustave Eiffel (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Allwood, Allan and Reid name Eiffel as the designer (Allwood et al. 2001).

Visitors who walked round one ring passed from nation to nation along radiating compartments (Greenhalgh 1988). The national pavilions stood around the palace. Barth counts roughly thirty, many designed by French architects who had no first-hand experience of the nations they represented (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid count 101 small buildings, including mosques, Russian slobodas and Tunisian kiosks (Allwood et al. 2001). Alphand designed the park as an English garden, which Barth calls the first park in the history of international exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Exhibits and attractions

The History of Labour (Histoire du Travail) was the fair's central thematic display. Allwood, Allan and Reid call it the first true thematic exhibition, a model that later fairs adopted (Allwood et al. 2001). It held more than 5,000 artifacts from private collections (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh says Section Ten, the tenth group, dealt most fully with design for poverty: France built six model dwellings there, with one each by Prussia and Austria (Greenhalgh 1988). Mandell says 67,000 workers, 40,000 of them foreigners, used free lodging on the site (Mandell 1967).

Entertainment took up much of the grounds. The park and the ring of restaurants brought a carnival atmosphere that later fairs copied (Findling & Pelle 1990). Nadar's balloon, aquariums and rented bars were added (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001). Victor Fournel, a contemporary critic whom Chandler quotes, faulted the park for "turning it from a serious lesson into a trivial game" (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Krupp showed a 50-ton steel cannon that drew the largest crowd (Findling & Pelle 1990). French commentators judged the display an "anomaly of exposing in the temple of peace such monsters of mass destruction" (Kaiser). Chandler reads Hugo's dismissal of the shells as cultural myopia, since the same cannon helped defeat Paris three years later (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Nations and colonies on show

Forty-nine nations took part (Findling & Pelle 2008). The national pavilion was the fair's most lasting innovation (Filipová 2015; Benedict 1983). Hanson compares the layout, with the Main Building ringed by smaller foreign buildings, to a planetary system with the host nation at its centre (Raizman & Robey 2018).

Egypt, invited by France, built its own section: a Pharaonic temple, an Arab-style selamlik and an okel market, meant to show the whole history of Egypt (Raizman & Robey 2018; Çelik 1992). Mitchell describes a Parisian copy of a medieval Egyptian street, with donkeys brought from Cairo (Mitchell 1989). The Ottoman pavilions, by Leon Parvillee, included a mosque that Celik calls the first exhibition mosque (Çelik 1992). Celik argues that the Ottoman and Egyptian pavilions were shaped by European academic theory, and that the fairs presented Islamic cultures as timeless and decorative (Çelik 1992).

Tunisia and Morocco joined Algeria as centrepieces of the French empire, each with its own section (Greenhalgh 1988). Celik reads the Tunisian and Moroccan pavilions as an escape from the upheavals of the two countries, with palaces and royal costumes in place of agriculture or industry (Filipová 2015). Colonies had to be shown in the same space as the fine arts (Greenhalgh 1988). Hale observes that the colonies took a limited part in the early fairs (Hale 2008).

Japan's debut came through the Tokugawa shogunate, which sent Tokugawa Akitake, a younger brother of the shogun, at the request of the French minister to Japan (Hur). Hur gives Akitake's age as fourteen (Hur); Hotta-Lister gives thirteen (Hotta-Lister 1999). Hotta-Lister argues the mission was political, and that the Satsuma and Hizen clans sent their own exhibits, so that Europeans saw three separate Japanese entities (Hotta-Lister 1999). Japan's traditional goods took the smallest area in the Main Building (Hur). Peterson argues that the geisha became Japan's central human image at later fairs, a figure traced back to 1867 (Peterson 2020). The sources disagree on sales: Hotta-Lister says the goods were largely sold (Hotta-Lister 1999), while Komatsu says many stayed unsold because they did not match European expectations (Lawn 2009).

China's presence was limited. Barth says China declined the invitation, and that a display of Chinese objects was arranged through the French foreign ministry (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hur describes a privately run Chinese pavilion that served food and music altered to European taste (Hur). Hungary made its first independent appearance after the 1867 compromise with Austria (Filipová 2015).

Peoples on display

Hughes argues that bringing people from overseas as living displays seems to have begun at Paris in 1867, and that these displays drew the protests against the 1929 Newcastle African Village (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Qureshi argues that displayed peoples were part of the fairs from the start of the Crystal Palace, and that they were incorporated "not after the Parisian exposition of 1867" (Qureshi 2011).

Burris argues that Turkish bazaars and Cairo Street concessions began the midway format at this fair, and that photographs of foreign peoples were shown as evidence of earlier human cultures (Burris 2001). Kaiser records that indigenous people worked at the fair, for example as waiters in oriental cafés (Kaiser). Hur describes a Chinese giant and a decapitated head in the freak show (Hur). Mitchell argues that the Egyptian display placed the visitor as an observer outside the order of the exhibit (Mitchell 1989).

Visitors, reception and politics

The attendance figures differ. Barth gives more than eleven million over 217 exhibition days (Findling & Pelle 2008), Greenhalgh also gives eleven million (Greenhalgh 1988), Chandler gives about seven million (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Allwood, Allan and Reid give 6,805,969 visitors (Allwood et al. 2001), the same figure as Badger's appendix (Badger 1979). Munoz Torreblanca gives over ten million in 210 days (Filipová 2015).

The opening was troubled. A wet winter and unfinished demonstrations kept many away, and 500 extra workmen finished the site in the nine days before 1 May (Allwood et al. 2001). Napoleon III opened the turnstiles to the masses, giving free travel to peasants from many provinces (Greenhalgh 1988). Thomas Cook organised excursions, and British workers and trade unionists came because of the labour theme (Roche 2000). Rimmel criticised the exterior, and Pettit said the curved galleries confused visitors (Giberti 2002).

Politics ran through the fair. Louis Napoleon organised it to impress the world with the stability of his government (Karp & Lavine 1991). Victor Emmanuel stayed away, and Thiers felt the Second Empire had no blunders left to commit (Findling & Pelle 1990). Alexander II was the target of an attempted assassination in Paris, and news of Maximilian's death in Mexico reached the prize-giving (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). More than fifteen foreign monarchs attended the final awards ceremony (Boone 2019).

How historians have interpreted it

The texts agree that the fair set the form of later fairs, and disagree about what that form meant. Barth reads the encyclopedic project as undercut in practice by the park and entertainments (Findling & Pelle 2008). Chandler presents the fair as the template for later fairs, whose social-improvement group was overtaken by carnival and commerce, and as an optimistic high point (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid stress the gap between ambition and execution, with unfinished exhibits and a slow start (Allwood et al. 2001). Greenhalgh reads it as the high point of the Second Empire's exhibitions, mixing imperial-economic ambition with Saint-Simonian paternalism (Greenhalgh 1988). Mandell sees it as the idealistic high point of the French series, whose principles had lost their appeal by 1900 (Mandell 1967).

The readings diverge on decline and origins. Geppert shows that scholars date the point at which expositions became mere spectacle anywhere from 1867 to the First World War, most favouring 1889 or 1900, and calls it absurd to date the decline from 1867 (Geppert 2010). Hughes places the start of living displays at Paris in 1867 (Hollengreen et al. 2014), while Qureshi places it at the Great Exhibition of 1851 (Qureshi 2011).

The national and colonial displays invite different readings. Celik sees Islamic architecture at the fairs as presented in timeless and decorative terms (Çelik 1992). Mitchell argues that the more fully a display surrounded the visitor, the more the observer was set apart from what was shown (Mitchell 1989). Kaiser reads the fairs as tools for projecting national images in competition with Britain (Kaiser). Hur reads Japan's participation as an attempt to affirm Tokugawa legitimacy (Hur). Hoffenberg reads the colonial courts as presenting each colony as a store of national wealth and identity (Hoffenberg 2001). Benedict reads the layout as an expression of nationalism and of multinational corporations (Benedict 1983). Giberti shows the Paris classification as the model for the Centennial's classification committee, although the Centennial rejected the Paris building as its prototype (Giberti 2002). Rydell mentions the fair only as a point of comparison for Philadelphia in 1876 (Rydell 1984).

Taken together, the texts agree that Paris set the form of the modern fair. They differ on when the form became spectacle, on whether the fair was an encyclopedic lesson or a display of national power, and on whether peoples were first displayed in 1867 (Geppert 2010; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Findling & Pelle 2008; Qureshi 2011).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan's first appearance at a world exhibition was at Paris in 1867 (Hurp. 38; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 206). Hur says the shogunate sent Tokugawa Akitake, the shogun's brother, and gives his age as fourteen (Hurp. 38); Hotta-Lister gives thirteen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 207). The texts also differ on who sent what. Hotta-Lister says the exhibits did not represent Japan as a whole: the Bakufu, Satsuma and Hizen each sent their own, and some Europeans therefore took Japan to have a federal system [21, l. 10032-10036; 10053-10056]. Komatsu names the Satsuma and Saga clans (Lawn 2009p. 73). Hur reads the mission as an effort to affirm shogunate legitimacy, and says that rivalry with the Satsuma and Saga domains mattered more than any rivalry with China [16, l. 2127-2133; 2179-2183].

Hur places the traditional-artefact exhibit in the smallest area of the Main Building, beside China and Thailand, and records a Japanese Tea House that hired three geisha (Hurp. 39). Peterson calls the geisha the most important human element of Japan's displays, going back to the 1867 Paris fair, and cites a French magazine inviting viewers to see them (Peterson 2020p. 65). Hotta-Lister says the goods were popular and were said to have sold out (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 207). Komatsu says many stayed unsold because they did not suit European expectations (Lawn 2009p. 74). Shibusawa, who accompanied Akitake, kept a travelogue showing no sense of competition with China [16, l. 2155-2161; 2171-2172]. ## China

Barth says China was the only nation to decline the invitation, but that the French foreign ministry obtained permission for a Chinese display, organised by the Marquis Hervey de Saint-Denys (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 39). Yet Barth's list of participants includes China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 39). Hur describes a privately run Chinese pavilion, set up by a local promoter, that served Chinese food and music altered to European taste (Hurp. 19). Three teenaged girls served alcohol and had their photographs taken, and a Chinese giant and a decapitated head appeared in the freak show (Hurp. 19; Benedict 1983p. 56). Two Nanjing men, Li Shilian and Xie Damin, were hired for a race show, despite a Chinese official's protest (Hurp. 19). Hur reports that the Chinese exhibit drew criticism for old techniques and inferior standards (Hurp. 17). Wang Tao visited the site on his way to Scotland (Hurp. 22). ## India

Greenhalgh says the Indian court at Paris in 1867 filled 8,700 square feet at a cost of £4,562 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Birdwood is named as in charge of the Indian exhibits (Geppert 2010p. 263) and as special commissioner for the Bombay government (Hoffenberg 2001p. 281). Hoffenberg reports that the assistants to the executive commissioner expected India to be unable to show much machinery, and says that limited machinery suited a Raj that might rule in the name of local tradition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 158). ## Southeast Asia

Barth's list of participating nations includes Annam, Cambodia and Siam (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 39). Hur places Thailand beside Japan and China in the smallest area (Hurp. 39). Benedict says the 1867 oval gave Algeria one band and the other French colonies another, but that these showed products rather than people (Benedict 1983p. 48). The texts say little more about Southeast Asian displays at this fair.

Interpretation

Kaiser counts the Japanese sections of 1867 and 1873 among Japan's efforts at symbolic inclusion in the Atlantic industrial world (Kaiserp. 47). Hur finds little difference between the Chinese and Japanese displays in scale and kind (Hurp. 39). The texts disagree on whether China took part at all, on which clans sent Japanese exhibits, and on Akitake's age.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Encyclopedic classification — The classification by object and by nation was meant to inventory the whole world, and the texts read it as a statement of progress and harmony (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Giberti 2002).
  • National pavilions — Paris is the first fair in which nations were invited to build their own pavilions, and the texts treat this as the origin of the standard fair layout (Benedict 1983; Badger 1979; Filipová 2015; Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Entertainment and spectacle — The park, restaurants, balloon and carnival atmosphere are read both as undercutting the fair's educational aims and as a model for later fairs (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Social welfare and labour — Group X, Section Ten and the History of Labour carried the fair's social message, with different views of how far it succeeded (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 1990; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Empire and Orientalism — The Egyptian, Ottoman, Tunisian and Algerian displays are read either as claims to modernity or as orientalist representation (Çelik 1992; Mitchell 1989; Greenhalgh 1988; Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Peoples on display — Living displays, freak shows and photographs of foreign peoples were a feature of the fair, but the texts disagree on when they began (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Qureshi 2011; Burris 2001; Hur).

Questions for study

  1. Hughes and Qureshi date the origin of living human displays differently, to 1867 and to 1851. What evidence would decide between them, and what changes in the history of displayed peoples depending on the answer? (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Qureshi 2011)
  2. Barth and Chandler both see the park and entertainments as undercutting the fair's educational aims (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Can a world's fair be a serious lesson and a commercial amusement at once, and how would a student weigh the evidence for each?
  3. Celik reads the Ottoman and Egyptian pavilions as shaped by European academic theory, yet the same pavilions were built to claim modernity for those states (Çelik 1992). Who was the intended audience for these displays, and who designed them?
  4. Hur and Hotta-Lister read Japan's participation in terms of political legitimacy and rival clans (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999). How far can a national display be read as a government's message when several bodies sent their own exhibits?
  5. The attendance figures range from about seven million to more than eleven million (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001; Filipová 2015). What does this range suggest about how reference works are compiled, and how should a student cite a figure from them?

Giberti's Designing the Centennial (2002) gives Paris 1867 a full section as the model for the Philadelphia Centennial: Prince Napoleon's 1855 proposal for a dual system, with nations in radial sections and classes in concentric rings, was realised in the elliptical main building of 1867, which the U.S. commissioners praised for its classification. On the web

Wikipedia

The Exposition Universelle of 1867 (French pronunciation: [ɛkspozisjɔ̃ ynivɛʁsɛl]), better known in English as the 1867 Paris Exposition, was a world's fair held in Paris, France, from 1 April to 3 November 1867. It was the second of ten major expositions held in the city between 1855 and 1937. A number of nations were represented at the fair. Following a decree of Emperor Napoleon III, the exposition was prepared as early as 1864, in the midst of the renovation of Paris, marking the culmination of the Second French Empire. Visitors included Tsar Alexander II of Russia, a brother of the King William and Otto von Bismarck of Prussia, Prince Metternich and Franz Josef of Austria, Ottoman Sultan Abdülaziz, and the Khedive of Egypt Isma'il.
