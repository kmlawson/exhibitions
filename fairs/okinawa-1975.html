Okinawa · 1975–1976

International Ocean Exposition (Ocean Expo '75, Okinawa)

OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Ocean Exposition (Ocean Expo '75) was held on the Motobu Peninsula of Okinawa from 20 July 1975 to 18 January 1976 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). It grew out of the Japanese government's desire to correct Okinawa's disparities with the other prefectures after the island's reversion to Japan in 1972 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). Its first dates, in March 1975, were postponed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). Allwood, Allan and Reid call it the first project in Japan's programme of expositions after Osaka 1970 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161). It matters as one of the specialised Cold War fairs where the environment became a theme (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 204).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The exposition grew out of the Japanese government's desire to rectify disparities between Okinawa and the other prefectures after 1972, and to improve the social climate by using the island's natural and cultural resources (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). In November 1971 the government applied to the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) for permission to hold a Special Category exposition on an oceanographic theme. The master plan was approved in October 1972 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). Allwood, Allan and Reid place the fair within a programme in which, after Osaka 1970, Japan became a determined bidder for slots in the BIE calendar and devised new variants when the rules blocked it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161).

Opening and postponement

The fair was first scheduled to open on 2 March 1975 and to run until 31 August (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). The encyclopaedia entry links the postponement to the international petroleum crisis, which it says exacerbated an already critical logistics problem for the exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). The Japanese government successfully appealed to the BIE for the postponement (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). Allwood, Allan and Reid write that the opening slipped back and that the postponement into the winter of 1975-76 allowed a new airport, highways, hotels and a water supply to be built (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). They also note that Spokane in 1974 had no more than nine foreign participants, despite the postponement of a potentially competitive exposition in Okinawa to late 1975 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 148).

Organisers and finance

Jiro Takase was commissioner general for the Japanese government, Nobumoto Ohama was president of the Japan Association, and Takashi Ohki was supervisor (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 421; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 393). The texts give different accounts of the money. The encyclopaedia gives direct expenditure of about 90 billion yen, including 46 billion yen in operating costs, and a further 250 million yen on related public works (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). Allwood, Allan and Reid instead put the infrastructure cost at over 250 billion yen, around three times the cost of the exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The two reference entries say the fair closed without a deficit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). Allwood, Allan and Reid say it was "the only Japanese expo to lose money" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81), and their BIE table records a loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210).

Site and buildings

The site covered 247 acres on the Motobu Peninsula (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353p. 355). It lies about 80 kilometres north of Naha, the prefectural capital, and was chosen partly for the highway linking Naha and Motobu (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe a place of bloody battles in 1945, wild and uninhabited, with venomous snakes and unexploded ordnance (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The pavilions were grouped in clusters by theme (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350), and the authors judge them architecturally unremarkable (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The most spectacular structure was the Aquapolis, the world's largest floating structure, which weighed 16,500 tons and could hold 2,400 people (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). The sources differ on its height: 32 metres in the encyclopaedia and the 1990 dictionary (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350), but 35 metres in Allwood, Allan and Reid (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). After the fair part of the site became the Ocean Exposition Memorial Park, and the Aquapolis was sold to an American scrap company in 2000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355).

Exhibits and attractions

The theme was The Sea We Would Like to See, with four sub-themes: Fish, Ethnic and History, Ships, and Science and Technology (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). Two international symposia were held at the site, one on 1-3 October 1975 and one on 17-22 November 1975 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). Other attractions were ethnic dance exhibitions, beauty contests and two transpacific yacht races (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). The Okinawa Pavilion showed the island's wartime past through a rusting rifle, a bayonet and a steel helmet (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The encyclopaedia also describes an Oceanic Cultures Hall on the peoples of the South Pacific and a Ryukyuan village of old Okinawan houses (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355). The aquarium by Maki and Associates overshadowed the Aquapolis (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162).

Who took part

The encyclopaedia says 35 countries and three international organisations took part, making it the largest Special Category exposition to that date (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). The 1990 dictionary and Allwood, Allan and Reid both give thirty countries and four international organisations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). Massidda's table gives 35 countries (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192). Denmark and Sweden had no pavilions of their own (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). As at most Cold War expositions, the United States and the Soviet Union had the two largest pavilion sites (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). The American pavilion covered 55,000 square feet and received 1,750,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354).

Visitors and reception

Visitors totalled 3,480,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Allwood et al. 2001p. 162), or 3,485,750 in the encyclopaedia and in Massidda's table (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192). Attendance fell short of expectation. Allwood, Allan and Reid give an anticipated figure of 4.5 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162), and the 1990 dictionary says it fell short of what was anticipated (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350). In Okinawa the teachers' union discouraged students from attending, and environmentalists objected to new roads and other works; the objections faded in spring 1975 as the fair took shape (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). Allwood, Allan and Reid note that distance from the mainland made visitors hard to attract (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). The encyclopaedia says the fair boosted tourism and brought highways, waterworks and hotels to Okinawa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355).

The colonial setting

Okinawa had been under US control until 1972, and Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the transfer of power after 27 years of US military control as not easy (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The encyclopaedia records that the official US participation had five objectives, one of which was to enhance the psychological acceptance of the American military presence on the island (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). It says that the low profile kept by American military personnel and their help to the organisers helped defuse the "issue of U.S. military presence on Okinawa" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354).

Politics and controversy

The encyclopaedia's account of opposition is brief: "Okinawa resisted the exposition." (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). Radical groups and the teachers' union were among the opponents (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the fair "came nearer to disaster than any other event in Japan's" expo programme (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161), but they judge it a success in political terms despite the shortfall in visitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162).

Aftermath and legacy

The authors note that the tourist facilities left by the fair were under-used, and that the 2000 G8 summit in Okinawa was seen as an extension of the expo policy (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The official records are held by the Okinawa Prefectural Archive (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355). The encyclopaedia says that a history of the fair in English remains to be written (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 356).

Its place in the series

The fair was the first project in Japan's programme after Osaka 1970 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161). Japan began planning its next official exposition as soon as the BIE timetable allowed, for 1985 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). Peterson places Okinawa with Expo 2005 in Aichi and Tsukuba among Japan's smaller specialised fairs (Peterson 2020p. 290). Roche's note links it with Knoxville in 1982, Tsukuba in 1985 and Brisbane in 1988 (Roche 2000p. 244). The BIE table lists Okinawa between Spokane in 1974 and a Quebec horticulture fair of 1976 that was cancelled (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210). Molella and Knowles set the fair beside Osaka 1970 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 7).

How historians have interpreted it

Allwood, Allan and Reid read the fair as a state-led project. They place it at the start of Japan's programme, call it the one that came nearest to disaster, and argue that the postponement was put to good use in building infrastructure (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161). Knowles, in the volume edited by Molella and Knowles, reads Okinawa as one of the specialised Cold War fairs where the environmental theme emerged (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 204). The two reference entries, by Martin Manning and Mitsugu Sakihara, describe the fair as a success for tourism and infrastructure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

The texts name participants from other parts of Asia. The encyclopaedia's list of pavilions includes Indonesia, Iran, the Republic of Korea, the Philippines, Thailand and the United Arab Emirates (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354). Allwood, Allan and Reid say an Iranian display of carpets and rugs was a criticised example of exhibitors who ignored the theme (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). The fair also showed Okinawan culture, through the Ryukyuan village and the Oceanic Cultures Hall (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355). The texts say nothing about visitors from Korea, Taiwan or mainland China.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Reversion and regional development — the fair was framed as a way to correct Okinawa's gap with the other prefectures after 1972 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353).
  • American military presence — the encyclopaedia records the low US profile and an official aim to accept the American presence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354); Allwood, Allan and Reid note the 27 years of US control (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162).
  • Opposition and protest — the teachers' union, environmentalists and radical groups opposed the fair before objections faded in spring 1975 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353).
  • A state-led exposition programme — Japan's bids for BIE slots after 1970 and its use of expositions for development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 353).
  • Finance and attendance — the texts give conflicting costs and deficit accounts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Allwood et al. 2001p. 81).
  • Environment — Okinawa as an early specialised fair where environmental themes appeared (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 7).

Questions for study

  1. Why do the texts disagree about whether the fair made a loss, and about whether its infrastructure cost 250 million or 250 billion yen? Which figures would a historian trust, and why? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 355; Allwood et al. 2001p. 81)
  2. How far did the American military presence shape what the fair displayed, and how did the organisers handle the opposition from teachers and environmentalists? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354)
  3. Allwood, Allan and Reid treat the postponement as having been put to good use. What did the postponement mean for the organisers and for the Motobu site? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162)
  4. The texts disagree on the Aquapolis's height and on the number of participating countries. What do such disagreements suggest about sources on expositions? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 354; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 350; Allwood et al. 2001p. 162)

Allwood's chapter covers the exhibitions held in Japan, Australia and Korea between 1975 and 1999, and argues that Japan turned the medium into a state-led tool of regional and economic development, which its neighbours then copied. It opens with Okinawa 1975, where an ocean theme and the Aquapolis floating platform were set on a former battlefield, and moves through Tsukuba 1985, which used computers, robots and audio-visual display to promote a science city. Its main case is Osaka 1990, a garden exposition that the organisers turned into a hybrid expo within the BIE's rules, with 82 nations and a record attendance. The chapter then surveys Brisbane 1988, whose shaded riverside site and state pavilions are described, Taejon 1993, where Korean corporate pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors, and the Kunming 1999 garden expo. It ends with the Philippines' withdrawal from a recognised exposition for Manila in 1992, and concludes that developing nations were still denied a realistic route to hosting.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Molella, Arthur P., and Scott Gabriel Knowles, eds. World’s Fairs in the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of Progress. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2019.
Introduction (Molella and Knowles), Part IV summary; ch. 15 (Luca Massidda), Table 15.1; ch. 16 (Scott Gabriel Knowles)

The volume's introduction places the 1975 Okinawa Ocean Expo beside Osaka 1970 as a fair that introduced environmental critiques into the Cold War fairs of Asia. Massidda lists the fair in his table of 1970-1992 expositions, with 35 participating countries and 3,485,750 visitors, in an argument that specialised fairs lost a credible global dimension in these years. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. About the work as a whole Asia: central

Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. Each case is a chapter, organised by fair, using archival and ethnographic methods. The conclusion argues that state-directed national representation has declined as the form became costly for host cities, and that Asia has become the leading host of expositions.

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics.
