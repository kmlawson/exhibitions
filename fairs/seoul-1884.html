Planned Industrial Exposition (Seoul)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

In 1884 Korean reformist officials planned an international industrial exposition for Seoul. The plan followed the Pobingsa mission to the United States, whose members went to the exhibition in Boston (Parkp. 4). It was never held. The text says it "failed to materialize" (Parkp. 5), and that Minister Min Yŏng-ik suspended it after the failed Kapsin coup of 4 December 1884 (Parkp. 5). The author reads the plan as one of the efforts to present Korea as an autonomous member of the international community (Parkp. 5).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and the plan

The international exposition entered Korea as part of its modernisation project in the early 1880s (Parkp. 3). The Pobingsa, a mission of ten reformist officials, went to the United States a year after the 1882 United States–Korea treaty (Parkp. 4). The text says the mission saw the West free from Japanese or Chinese overlay (Parkp. 4). Its members were particularly intent on attending the Boston exhibition (Parkp. 4). Impressed by it, the reformist officials planned an international industrial exposition in Seoul "the following year" (Parkp. 5).

The text gives no venue and no exact date. It places the plan in the year after the mission, which it calls 1884 (Parkp. 5). Minister Min Yŏng-ik had admired the agricultural implements he saw at Boston (Parkp. 5).

Why it was not held

The text gives two causes: "a lack of capital", and the political turmoil after the failed coup of 4 December 1884, the Kapsin chŏngbyŏn (Parkp. 5). The Kaehwadang (Enlightenment Party), whose members included Pobingsa mission members, launched the coup with Japanese support. Chinese forces suppressed it (Parkp. 5). Its reformists were "either killed or exiled to Japan" (Parkp. 5). On returning from the mission, Min was compelled to sever ties with the reformists and to suspend the exhibition plan, which the text says was closely associated with the Japanese model and with radical reform (Parkp. 5).

Its place in the series

The text calls the efforts after the mission unsuccessful, but notable for presenting Korea as an autonomous member of the international community (Parkp. 5). Korea later took part in Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900 (Parkp. 5). King Kojong set up a Provisional Exposition Department in 1902 (Parkp. 6). The Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 was the first major exposition in the capital (Parkp. 6). The local industrial exhibitions were important precursors of the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Parkp. 6).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Reformist modernisation — the text places the post-mission efforts in a drive to make Korea an autonomous member of the international community and to introduce Western institutions (Parkp. 5)
  • Direct contact with the West — the mission is said to have observed the West free from Japanese or Chinese overlay (Parkp. 4)
  • Japanese and Chinese rivalry — the coup had Japanese support and was suppressed by Chinese forces, and the plan was tied to the Japanese model (Parkp. 5)
  • Finance — the plan failed for lack of capital, among other causes (Parkp. 5)
  • Print and terminology — the first serious press discussion of expositions appeared in March 1884 in Hansŏng sunbo (Parkp. 5)

Questions for study

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

Brief discussion 1

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Section 'The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernity' (starts l. 125; the 1884 plan is on journal pp. 3-4, l. 209-295)

Park's article on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition opens with a section on how the international exposition entered Korea. Within it, one paragraph covers the industrial exposition that reformist officials planned for Seoul after the Pobingsa mission had visited the Boston exhibition in the United States. The author says the plan failed to materialise, links that failure to the Kapsin coup of 4 December 1884, and records that Minister Min Yŏng-ik suspended it. For Park, the early efforts matter because they tried to present Korea as an autonomous member of the international community and to speed up Western institutions, including expositions. The argument is that expositions were a symbol of modernisation from the start, but the 1884 plan is only a brief episode in that story.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
