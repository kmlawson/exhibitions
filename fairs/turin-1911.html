International Exhibition of Industry and Labour

Esposizione internazionale d'industria e de laboro

The International Exhibition of Industry and Labour (Esposizione internazionale d'industria e de laboro) opened in Turin on 29 April 1911 (Della Coletta 2006p. 88). It marked the fiftieth anniversary of Italian unity and ran at the same time as exhibitions in Florence and Rome (Filipová 2015p. 38; Della Coletta 2006p. 10). It occupied the gardens of Valentino Castle on the left bank of the Po, with further exhibits on the right bank (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). The texts name no single organiser; the official magazine was published by the exhibition's executive committee (Della Coletta 2006p. 107). Its colonial displays for Eritrea and Somalia were built to show Italy's colonial project advancing (Della Coletta 2006p. 92).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Turin had sponsored more national and international exhibitions than any other Italian city (Della Coletta 2006p. 9). The 1911 fair was the first universal exhibition held in Italy (Della Coletta 2006p. 10). The author reads it as a display of Turin's industrial and political progress, presented as a story of national unity and labour (Della Coletta 2006p. 80). Its organisers aimed to build national consensus around Italy's colonial agenda (Della Coletta 2006p. 10).

The fair opened on 29 April 1911 in the presence of the king and queen, Prime Minister Giolitti and the ambassadors of forty foreign countries (Della Coletta 2006p. 88). Turin's claim to lead was contested. A joint proclamation by the mayors of Turin and Rome gave Turin the industrial exhibition and gave Rome the patriotic, historical and artistic exhibits (Della Coletta 2006p. 86). The city's rivalry with Rome also shaped the fair's aims (Della Coletta 2006p. 10).

Site and buildings

The fair stood in the park of Valentino Castle, whose stage-like setting the author links to the fair's architecture (Della Coletta 2006p. 82). Exhibits covered both banks of the Po, and a specially built two-level bridge with a moving pavement joined them (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). Allwood's table gives the site as 247 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

The organisers made the Turin baroque, and the designs of the architect Juvarra, the fair's overall style (Della Coletta 2006p. 82). The author argues that the revival stayed semiotically opaque and did not become a shared national symbol (Della Coletta 2006p. 85). Rome's exhibition of the same year used a classical revival instead (Della Coletta 2006p. 85).

The fair also gave prominence to automobiles, and it coincided with the first international automobile races in Italy (Della Coletta 2006p. 110). Its stadium is described as the largest in Europe, able to hold up to forty thousand people (Della Coletta 2006p. 284). The Padiglione della Moda, or Couture Pavilion, was one of its attractions (Della Coletta 2006p. 119).

Exhibits and attractions

The fair showed the collections of Luigi Amedeo, Duke of the Abruzzi, from his polar expedition, including scientific materials, equipment and popular publications about the journey (Della Coletta 2006p. 117). His narrative of the expedition, the Stella Polare book, was the most widely sold book in Italy for several years after its publication in 1903 (Della Coletta 2006p. 117). The author presents the expedition as part of the House of Savoy's publicity, used to restore the dynasty's credibility after the colonial defeat at Adowa (Della Coletta 2006p. 117).

Prince Scipione Borghese, who had raced a car from Beijing to Paris, was one of the fair's guests of honour (Della Coletta 2006p. 110). The author links such races with the fairs' appeal of vicarious travel (Della Coletta 2006p. 119).

Empire and colony

Turin 1911 was imperial in that Italy used it to advance its colonial programme. It showed three colonial exhibits: those of the colonial directorate of the Foreign Ministry, of the colony of Eritrea and of Italian Somalia (Della Coletta 2006p. 92). The displays aimed to persuade Italians that colonisation was progressing, despite the earlier defeats at Dogali and Massaua (Della Coletta 2006p. 92). The Biblioteca coloniale, a multi-volume history of Italian colonisation, was only partly complete when the fair opened (Della Coletta 2006p. 94).

The Somali and Eritrean displays set huts in rows and showed structured activity, which presented the colonies as productive (Della Coletta 2006p. 97). The Somali village showed people brought from Somalia and Benadir in ritualised representations (Della Coletta 2006p. 97). Beside it stood a War Exhibit that included live indigenous policemen dressed in European style (Della Coletta 2006p. 100). The texts do not record what these people themselves thought of their display.

The Frenchman Ernest Pourtauborde, who had built Oriental villages at the Paris exhibition of 1900, laid out the Kermesse orientale (Della Coletta 2006p. 100). Its rides and cafes lay on the left bank of the Po, and on the right bank a catalogue of peoples from around the world (Della Coletta 2006p. 100). The author argues that the fair aimed to win Italy equal standing with the imperial powers, while its colonial narrative depended on France and Britain (Della Coletta 2006p. 11). France had the most prominent foreign presence, with an official pavilion and a Paris city pavilion (Della Coletta 2006p. 102).

The fair was also tied to war. Italy declared war on Turkey on 30 September 1911 (Della Coletta 2006p. 89). The author argues that several exhibits shaped public opinion in favour of a colonial presence on the North African coast (Della Coletta 2006p. 91). The author places the fair near the end of the World's Fairs' story of progress: "Turin 1911 staged the last act of the grand drama of progress" (Della Coletta 2006p. 122).

Politics and controversy

Turin's newspapers reported that police and cavalry were used to stop a strike of five thousand construction workers during the fair (Della Coletta 2006p. 89). Dissent was in print as well. The reporter Toga-Rasa compared the Kermesse orientale to Dante's Hell and called it a taste of Italy's colonial poverty in Africa (Della Coletta 2006p. 100).

Who took part

The Hungarian pavilion was designed by Dénes Györgyi, Emil Tőry and Móric Pogány. It was mostly wooden and combined the work of Károly Kós with that of a group of young architects (Filipová 2015p. 35). Its interior became an exhibited object, and the Hungarian goods on show formed one display with the building (Filipová 2015p. 35). Works by Géza Maróti, Ede Telcs, Miklós Ligeti and Miksa Róth were shown (Filipová 2015p. 36). Maróti's pavilion had a tower with a pitched roof meant to evoke Attila's tent (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).

France took a large part in the fair, with its official pavilion and its city pavilion (Della Coletta 2006p. 102). Ulick Wintour served as British commissioner-general for the Turin exhibition of 1911 (Geppert 2010p. 279).

Visitors and reception

Della Coletta gives the number of visitors as seven million (Della Coletta 2006p. 80). Allwood's table also has a Turin row, but its attendance figure is illegible in the copy used for this study (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Pasquale De Luca's account of the fair quoted a London newspaper's praise of modern Italy and reported that the French press backed the 'Latin alliance' (Della Coletta 2006p. 102p. 103).

How historians have interpreted it

Della Coletta is the main interpreter. She argues that the baroque failed as a national symbol and that the fair was a late stage in the World's Fairs' story of progress (Della Coletta 2006p. 85p. 122). Székely reads the Hungarian pavilion at Turin as an exhibit in which the Hungarian display and the building formed a single display (Filipová 2015p. 35). Houze treats Maróti's Turin pavilion as eclectic, drawing on exotic and historical forms (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say little about Asian presence at Turin 1911. Pourtauborde's programme for the Kermesse orientale named China, Siam, Japan and Indo-China among the peoples to be shown (Della Coletta 2006p. 101). The text does not say whether those displays were built. Japan sent exhibits from the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition: the mineralogical section sent samples of minerals and accompanying journals to Turin (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 107). Hotta-Lister says Japan managed to send some exhibits only to Turin, Dresden and Budapest (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 184). None of the texts describes Chinese, Indian, Korean or Southeast Asian exhibitors or visitors at Turin.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — the colonial exhibits, the villages and the French-run Kermesse show Italy seeking standing among the imperial powers (Della Coletta 2006p. 92p. 100p. 11).
  • National style — the Turin baroque was meant to become a national architecture and remained a regional revival (Della Coletta 2006p. 82p. 85).
  • Colonial war — the fair's colonial displays and the declaration of war on Turkey fall in the same months (Della Coletta 2006p. 89p. 91).
  • Hungarian national pavilion — the Hungarian display and its building formed one exhibit, and the pavilion's style drew on folk architecture (Filipová 2015p. 35; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).
  • Circulation of exhibits — objects from one exhibition were sent on to the next, as Japan did from London to Turin (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 107).
  • Spectacle and travel — automobile racing, a stadium and a guest of honour from the Peking-to-Paris race give the fair its travel theme (Della Coletta 2006p. 110p. 284).

Questions for study

  1. How far did the staging of the Somali and Eritrean villages at Turin differ from the native villages of earlier fairs, and what do the texts say about the people shown (Della Coletta 2006p. 96p. 97)?
  2. Why did the Turin baroque fail to become a national style while Rome's classical revival did not (Della Coletta 2006p. 85)?
  3. The texts give seven million visitors in one place and an illegible figure in another. What sources would settle the attendance (Della Coletta 2006p. 80; Allwood et al. 2001p. 207)?
  4. What did a Hungarian national pavilion mean at an Italian fair held in a year of imperial politics (Filipová 2015p. 36; Filipová 2015p. 38)?

Della Coletta, Cristina. World’s Fairs Italian-Style: The Great Expositions in Turin and Their Narratives, 1860–1915. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2006.
Introduction: The Spectacle of Inventing a Nation: World’s Fairs and Their Narratives in Italy, 1860–1915p. 3Asia

Della Coletta argues that Italy's exhibitions between 1860 and 1915 were active tools of nation building rather than imitations of French and British fairs, and that they carried their own contradictions. The introduction reads expositions as a 'signifying practice' combining realist and spectacular codes, drawing on Said's account of culture and on critiques of binary imperial thinking. Its main case is Turin, the city that sponsored more national and international fairs than any other in Italy. Turin 1911 tried to give the Turin baroque a national meaning and to present Italy's colonial achievements, but the revival stayed regional and the colonial displays revealed dependence on France and Britain. The introduction then previews the later chapters: Emilio Salgari's jungle novels, read against Dumas's memoirs of Garibaldi, and Guido Gozzano's letters from India and exposition pieces. Della Coletta concludes that these hybrid narratives undercut the fairs' binary logic, and that Gozzano's writing reflects a shift toward unstable forms of knowledge.

2Turin 1911: The ‘Fabulous Exposition’p. 79

Della Coletta argues that the 1911 Turin exposition, staged to mark fifty years of Italian unity, combined industrial display with propaganda for the nation, a national architectural style and colonial expansion. The chapter starts with the architecture, where the organizers adopted the Turin baroque of Juvarra as the fair's overall style and the Rome exhibition of the same year used a classical revival instead. It then turns to the colonial displays for Eritrea and Somalia, the Somali village, and the 'Oriental' fairground that the Frenchman Pourtauborde laid out across the Po. Official magazines, Touring Club guides, press coverage of Scipione Borghese's Peking-to-Paris motor race and of Luigi Amedeo's polar expedition supply further evidence. The argument is that the fair prepared public consent for the war on Turkey, declared on 30 September 1911 while it was open, and that the Turin baroque never became a shared national symbol. The chapter ends by calling Turin 1911 the last act of the fairs' drama of progress.

4Guido Gozzano’s Imperial Ambiguitiesp. 176Asia

Della Coletta reads Guido Gozzano's Indian letters against the single volume that Borgese assembled from them as Verso la cuna del mondo (1917), and against the newspapers and magazines where they first appeared. She argues that Gozzano's travel writing borrows its method from the World's Fair displays and illustrated periodicals of the time: it piles up catalogues of visual contrasts and leaves Western assumptions intact. The evidence is close reading of particular sketches: the harbour of Bombay as a 'compendium of the world', the temples of Elephanta and Madura, a Devadasi's dance, the Parsee Towers of Silence, the 1857 Cawnpore massacre, and Goa, where parody of Camoens and a borrowed Heredia sonnet turn the epic into elegy. Gozzano's essays on the 1911 Turin exposition, including his two readings of Dürer's Melancholia, show the same ambivalence towards spectacle. The chapter ends with Agra and Hyderabad, where Gozzano's ideal of an art cut off from profit turns out to depend on British grain shipments, so that aesthetics and the market cannot be kept apart.

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
1A Capital in the Margins: Concepts for a Budapest Universal Exhibition between 1867 and 1917Miklós Székelyp. 23

Székely argues that the proposals for a universal exhibition in Budapest between 1867 and 1917 show how a semi-sovereign state presented itself at home and abroad. Within the Dual Monarchy Hungary had its own government but no independent foreign or military policy, so its exhibitions asserted a 'sovereign' and 'Hungarian' identity through pavilion architecture, the revival of historic styles and vernacular art, and commemoration. The chapter follows three planning efforts: Szilárd Blána's utopian permanent exhibition of 1874, Count Jenő Zichy's proposal to mark the 1896 Millennium with a universal exhibition, and Leo Lánczy's 1911 scheme for 1917, the fiftieth anniversary of Franz Joseph's coronation. Its evidence comes from Hungarian displays at Paris in 1867 and 1900, the forestry pavilion and ethnographic village at Vienna in 1873, and the Turin pavilion of 1911. Székely concludes that none of the Budapest universal exhibitions was realised, and that the plans show a modernising nation looking to its glorious past while displaying its present achievements abroad.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This book argues that Italy's exhibitions between 1860 and 1915 were tools of nation building rather than imitations of French and British fairs. Della Coletta reads the fairs as consensus-making machines that repeated ideas about national identity, imperial competition and progress, while their counter-narratives survived as silences and contradictions. Turin, which sponsored more national and international fairs than any other Italian city, is the main case: chapters cover the Turin fairs from 1850 to 1911, including 1911, which marked fifty years of unity. Two chapters then read texts that carry exposition habits into literature, Emilio Salgari's adventure novels and Guido Gozzano's writing on India and the 1911 fair. An introduction and a conclusion frame the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present but not central. Their fullest treatment is chapter 3, Emilio Salgari: Writing Exposition Style, where the Malay archipelago, Borneo and Siam supply the settings and ethnic inventories of the jungle novels, and Sandokan's Borneo kingdom, destroyed by British and Dutch allies of the sultan, is read against Garibaldi's campaigns. Here the concern is fiction and the European ranking of peoples, not exhibitions. Chapter 1 mentions a Chinese-pagoda kiosk at the 1884 Turin fair. Chapter 2, Turin 1911, has the most exhibition material: Pourtauborde's Oriental fairground, which evoked China, Siam, Japan and Indochina as 'Oriental peoples'; the Filipino villages at the 1904 Saint Louis fair, cited as a model for 'native' displays; and Barzini's Beijing-to-Paris race reports, read as a fair-like image of a motionless East. Chapter 4 touches Japan only through war photographs in illustrated magazines. Asia appears as a theatre of display and fiction, never as exhibitor: no Asian state, commissioner or merchant is discussed, no fair held in Asia is considered, and Asian observers barely appear. South Asia gets much more space, through Gozzano's India letters in chapter 4, which lie outside this question. The colonial sections of Turin 1911 concentrate on Eritrea and Somalia, so Southeast Asia is reduced to a list.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

The Turin International was a world's fair held in Turin in 1911 titled Esposizione internazionale dell'industria e del lavoro. It was opened by King Victor Emmanuel III on April 29, 1911, and lasted until November 19, 1911. It received 7,409,145 visits and covered 247 acres.
