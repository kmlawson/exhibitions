Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations opened in Hyde Park, London, on 1 May 1851 and closed on 15 October that year, although Allwood and co-authors give 11 October as the close (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Its glass-and-iron hall, Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace, drew more than six million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Historians treat it as the first international world's fair and the model later exhibitions copied, and as a display of British industry, empire and social order, staged for a mass public (Peterson 2020; Greenhalgh 1988; Geppert 2010; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Findling & Pelle 2008). Its surplus bought the land for the South Kensington museums in London (Findling & Pelle 1990; Roche 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of older trade fairs and a run of smaller shows. Davis says the Society of Arts held trial exhibitions at the Adelphi from 1845 to 1849, which showed that such undertakings could pay (Findling & Pelle 2008). Smith places the idea in local exhibitions and the Society's shows of 1847 to 1849 (Findling & Pelle 1990). The international form came from France: Greenhalgh traces it to a proposal by Boucher de Perthes in 1834 and a French government circular of 1849, and argues that Henry Cole carried the idea to London, where Britain took it up (Greenhalgh 1988). Albert, president of the Society of Arts, was the strongest advocate of an international show rather than a national one, and Greenhalgh reports that Albert insisted it must include foreign products (Findling & Pelle 2008; Greenhalgh 1988).

The motives were commercial and political at once. Greenhalgh reads British commercial ambition, and the wish for more markets, as a main reason for the invitation to foreign nations (Greenhalgh 1988). Smith says the organisers meant the fair to show British industrial supremacy and to comment on free trade, peace and the British constitution (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hollengreen and Rouse say Albert and middle-class allies such as Cole aimed to answer the revolutions of 1848 with a forecast of industrial progress (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Della Coletta sets the fair's free-enterprise message against the state-backed Italian fairs that followed (Della Coletta 2006).

Organisation, money and the building

A Royal Commission chaired by Albert ran the fair. Davis says it displaced the Society of Arts and included Peel, Gladstone and Russell (Findling & Pelle 2008). Smith adds Lord John Russell and says the Commission became permanent by supplemental charter (Findling & Pelle 1990). Roche counts four former prime ministers on it and notes that Parliament debated the site (Roche 2000). The government gave no money, though royal backing counted for much (Findling & Pelle 2008). Smith lists a promise of £50,000 from Samuel Peto, £1,500 from the queen and Albert, and about 5,000 smaller contributors (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hotta-Lister notes that the British state had never financed an exhibition in Britain (Hotta-Lister 1999), and Della Coletta observes that most later fairs were paid for by host governments (Della Coletta 2006).

The building was chosen late. The Building Committee rejected all 254 designs, and Paxton's plan, published in the Illustrated London News on 6 July 1850, was accepted (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood and co-authors say the committee could not decide, and the work went to Fox, Henderson and Company at £79,800, approved on 26 July 1850 (Allwood et al. 2001). The building covered about nineteen acres, measured 1,848 by 408 feet, and rose 66 feet, or 108 feet in the transepts (Findling & Pelle 1990). The sources disagree on the ironwork: Benedict counts 330 standardised iron columns (Benedict 1983), Allwood and co-authors 3,300 hollow columns (Allwood et al. 2001), and Roche more than 3,000 (Roche 2000). Allwood gives about 300,000 panes of glass (Allwood et al. 2001), and Benedict 293,655 (Benedict 1983).

Allwood and co-authors read the building as pure engineering, a living example of mass production, and Owen Jones's interior as built in primary colours (Allwood et al. 2001). Greenhalgh argues that everything at the exhibition was explained in terms of technology, so that it fetishised the machine (Greenhalgh 1988). Celik reports that Jones's interior principles were drawn from Islamic architecture, especially the Alhambra (Çelik 1992). Pevsner dismissed the British products as "ludicrously over-ornate" (Raizman & Robey 2018). The surplus was £186,437 by Smith's count (Findling & Pelle 1990) and by Allwood's (Allwood et al. 2001); Davis gives 186,000 pounds (Findling & Pelle 2008). It bought 87 acres at South Kensington for museums and teaching institutions (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

Exhibits and classification

Albert proposed four sections (raw materials, machinery, manufactures, and the art applied to manufactures), and Lyon Playfair devised thirty classes, nineteen of them manufactures (Giberti 2002). Painting was excluded and sculpture included (Giberti 2002). Benedict says the fair had no fine-art section, though it had much monumental sculpture (Benedict 1983), and Roche says paintings were left out as irrelevant to industry (Roche 2000). Hornstein records that only works "connected with mechanical processes" were admitted (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Greenhalgh calls the fine-art sections the weakest part of the show (Greenhalgh 1988).

Foreign exhibits and the British ones were divided between the west and east halves of the building (Findling & Pelle 2008). Greenhalgh describes the space as split half to Britain and empire and half to foreign exhibitors (Greenhalgh 1988). Smith's counts give 6,556 foreign exhibits and 7,381 from Britain and its colonies (Findling & Pelle 1990). Giberti argues that the goods were installed by country because foreign exhibits could not be predicted, which showed a conflict between an abstract order of knowledge and the real space of the building (Giberti 2002). Juries were half British and half foreign, and cash prizes were dropped as too inflammatory (Burris 2001).

Empire and colonies on show

Colonial display sits at the centre of the scholarship. Hoffenberg focuses on the colonial and Indian courts within the 1851 story, noting that the Indian commissioners had 24,000 square feet in 1851 and filled more than 65,000 at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001). The British East India Company organised an Indian Court of produce, textiles, tea and spices, and Roche dates from it a long British tradition of showing India (Roche 2000). Mathur counts about fifteen thousand Indian objects, which became a drama of British imperial power (Mathur 2007). Mathur quotes the catalogue on fashion and argues that the Indian vogue was made by mass consumption in European cities, not by any quality of the East (Mathur 2007).

Benedict describes the colonial section as tribute: trophies of arms from conquered countries and symbols of conquered royalty, such as state umbrellas and ivory thrones (Benedict 1983). Harvey argues that European nations displayed goods as marks of civilisation, against dominated territories that supplied the raw materials (Harvey 1996). Hinsley calls the London fair classically imperialist in conception and construction (Karp & Lavine 1991). Bell shows the New Zealand Court, run by Moore, displaying a large lithograph of a Maori pa among minerals and flax (Bell 1992). Bell argues that showing Maori as peaceable would have supported the promotion of New Zealand (Bell 1992).

Asia appeared mostly through objects, chiefly from British-colonised India (Peterson 2020). Hur says Consul Bowring at Canton secured many artifacts and over $8,000 from merchants, but the governor of Fujian declined to take part, and Alcock's appeal in Shanghai drew only a few compradors (Hur). Hotta-Lister notes that Japanese objects shown in 1851 and 1862 were already shaped for Western taste (Hotta-Lister 1999).

Peoples on display

The texts treat living people as part of the 1851 display. Robles, citing Qureshi, says a native custodian of the Tunis Court was the first living foreign person used in a world's fair, and that the Palace also held more than sixty wax groups of Hindu castes and North and South American peasants (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Qureshi says living foreign peoples were among the official exhibits from the start (Qureshi 2011). Hanson describes the Tunisian court, sent under Ahmed Bey, as about 500 objects arranged as a bazaar (Raizman & Robey 2018). Charlotte Brontë called the fair "such a bazaar or fair as Eastern genii might have created" (Raizman & Robey 2018). Hanson argues that the fair built a model of the East that cancelled physical distance while confirming cultural difference (Raizman & Robey 2018). Qureshi also shows the crowds treating foreign visitors as spectacle, through Mayhew's satire and Tenniel's Punch cartoon (Qureshi 2011).

Visitors and reception

Davis gives the total as over six million, but notes that repeat visits probably made up part of it (Findling & Pelle 2008). Smith and Allwood give 6,039,195 (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Smith records a record 93,224 visitors on 7 October (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Allwood a daily average of almost 43,000 (Allwood et al. 2001). Thomas Cook's excursions from the Midland Railway brought some 165,000 people (Allwood et al. 2001; Mitchell 1989). Dussel says the crowds became a spectacle in themselves (Lawn 2009).

The reception was mixed. Queen Victoria's journal described the opening as a day to live for ever (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Massidda relies on the queen's diary for large opening-day crowds with no accidents or police reports, and calls 1851 the first mass public (Molella & Knowles 2019). Smith sets the hostile reaction against it: fears of Chartist disturbance, refugees, and predictions of riot and collapse (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Times praised the fair as an act of peace, and Punch mocked it as the great derby race for 1851 (Kaiser). Lawn cites Mayhew's view of the fair as a giant schoolhouse, with 35,000 children visiting (Lawn 2009).

Legacy and aftermath

The surplus shaped the London museums. Roche describes an exhibitionary complex of museums and colleges on the South Kensington land, called Albertopolis (Roche 2000). Mandell says the surplus still funded the Victoria and Albert Museum and art scholarships (Mandell 1967). Cole's arguments for state support of art were used in Melbourne in the 1850s (Bennett 2004). The Palace was rebuilt at Sydenham and reopened in 1854 (Allwood et al. 2001; Hollengreen et al. 2014). Roche notes a Festival of Empire there in 1911 (Roche 2000), and the building burned on 30 November 1936 (Allwood et al. 2001; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The fair also set a pattern. Mandell argues that no later London, Vienna or Philadelphia exhibition matched 1851's cultural impact (Mandell 1967). New York's 1853 fair copied many of its elements (Filipová 2015; Greenhalgh 1988). Maloney counts at least four world's fairs in each decade since 1851 (Maloney 2012), and Hotta-Lister about forty international exhibitions between 1851 and 1970 (Hotta-Lister 1999).

How historians have interpreted it

The main dispute is whether 1851 is the centre of the story. Geppert argues that it became a founding myth and a narrative template, and that in Britain it set the rules of the medium until about 1900 (Geppert 2010). Richards calls it a prototype rather than a blueprint (Geppert 2010). Hoffenberg attacks this fixation, arguing that the London shows of 1862 to 1914 and the colonial shows in Sydney and Calcutta were at least as influential in building imperial and national identities (Hoffenberg 2001). Filipová's volume notes that English-language histories long treated the Crystal Palace as the yardstick of exhibitions (Filipová 2015).

Authors also differ on purpose. Davis calls the fair a "superbly effective propaganda weapon in favor of modernity" and reviews the historiography: early accounts were eulogistic, later critics read imperialist messages, and economic historians since the 1960s stressed technology transfer (Findling & Pelle 2008). Davis judges claims that the fair caused the 1850s boom hard to verify and probably groundless (Findling & Pelle 2008). Massidda argues that the fairs healed the strains of industrialisation (Molella & Knowles 2019), and Kargon and colleagues that they taught audiences to accept change as progress (Kargon et al. 2015). Hinsley places the displays within a progressivist ideology that treated peoples as raw materials (Karp & Lavine 1991), and Qureshi reads the human displays as commercial spectacle (Qureshi 2011). Burris, by contrast, insists that the Royal Commission did not plan the fair to advance British colonialism. Burris reads it through tensions among economic, political and aesthetic forces, and traces the ranking of societies as savage or civilized in the juries and displays (Burris 2001).

The texts agree that the fair mattered as a precedent, and they disagree about what it meant. Geppert and Richards treat it as a model (Geppert 2010); Hoffenberg treats the later shows as the more consequential (Hoffenberg 2001). Davis and Hinsley read it through economics and empire (Findling & Pelle 2008; Karp & Lavine 1991), while Burris reads it through religion and the ranking of societies (Burris 2001) and Lawn through the classification of knowledge (Lawn 2009). Mandell and Filipová's volume both agree that 1851 set a standard that later fairs were measured against (Mandell 1967; Filipová 2015).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India

The East India Company, not the Indian state, organised India's display. Organisers first turned to the company for a full display of Indian culture (Burris 2001p. 46), and Greenhalgh calls its contributions the centrepiece of the imperial sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 53). Roche dates from the company's Indian Court a long British tradition of displaying India (Roche 2000p. 57). Burris and Roche give the court as about thirty thousand square feet (Burris 2001p. 46; Roche 2000p. 57); Hoffenberg gives 24,000 feet (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9). Burris says the company took all the medals as official exhibitor (Burris 2001p. 46). Hoffenberg says Indian princes later donated artware and arms (Hoffenberg 2001p. 41), and that the Central Committee for India received exhibits from "native noblemen and gentlemen" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59).

Most of what was shown was produce. Greenhalgh lists coal, saltpetre, spices and ivory, and notes that the machines were "of extremely rough workmanship" [07, l. 2992-2995; 3002-3004]. Mathur counts some fifteen thousand objects and calls the section an "exotic Aladdin's Cave" (Mathur 2007p. 15). Qureshi says the Indian court held more than sixty groups of models of the Hindu castes (Qureshi 2011p. 236). Greenhalgh's list of exhibiting colonies includes the "Indian Archipelago", Ceylon, Labuan and Borneo (Greenhalgh 1988p. 53). Hanson places India and Ceylon nearest the central transept (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 16). The texts say nothing more about Southeast Asia at this fair.

China

Greenhalgh says thirty-four invited nations accepted, among them China, Persia and Turkey (Greenhalgh 1988p. 12); Hur gives twenty-five countries as participants (Hurp. 11). Consul John Bowring at Canton raised funds and recruited merchants, securing many artifacts and over $8,000 (Hurp. 11). The Governor of Fujian, Xu Jinyu, declined to take part (Hurp. 11). In Shanghai only a few compradors sent silk, mandarin gowns, tea and minerals (Hurp. 11). Alcock sent Chinese handcrafts of his own, such as inlaid wood, soapstone and enamels (Hurp. 12). Fernsebner, by contrast, names Jingdezhen porcelain in Alcock's exhibit, which European writers read as "imitation" and as "prototypes" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). Peterson says Chinese goods were shown in London in 1851 (Peterson 2020p. 80), and that before 1904 Chinese participation was largely in British hands (Peterson 2020p. 80). Mathur calls the China exhibit an unflattering "mixture of the graceful and grotesque" (Mathur 2007p. 15). In 1866 the Chinese mission led by Bin Chun saw the Crystal Palace, but its travelogue did not link it to an exhibition (Hurp. 22).

Japan

Hotta-Lister says Japanese articles were still novelties at earlier exhibitions, including 1851, shown "in meagre quantity and variable quality". The items Alcock presented already showed Western influence, having been made for export (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 204). Allwood says the Dublin exhibition of 1853 took up Indian and Oriental artefacts that the East India Company had gathered for 1851, and that its Japanese material gave many people their first view of Japanese arts and crafts (Allwood et al. 2001p. 20).

Reception and interpretation

Lawn says cartoons and journalism mocked the foreigners of the fair, among them Chinese plenipotentiaries (Lawn 2009p. 18). Greenhalgh argues that the imperial displays were meant "to simultaneously glorify and domesticate empire" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 54). Burris sets aside the view that the Royal Commission planned the fair to advance British colonialism abroad (Burris 2001p. 23). Hanson argues that the fair built a model of "the East" that negated physical distance while confirming cultural difference (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 15). Mathur argues that fashion for Indian goods was not an innate property of the East but the result of mass consumption in European cities (Mathur 2007p. 18).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Propaganda and progress — the texts see the fair as a public demonstration of industrial and national progress: Davis calls it a propaganda weapon for modernity (Findling & Pelle 2008), Massidda a way of healing the strains of industrialisation (Molella & Knowles 2019), and Kargon and colleagues a means of making rapid change look natural (Kargon et al. 2015).
  • Empire and colonial display — the colonial and Indian courts, the display of tribute and conquered symbols, and the New Zealand Court are among the topics the texts discuss most at this fair (Hoffenberg 2001; Benedict 1983; Mathur 2007; Bell 1992).
  • Living peoples and the bazaar — the fair admitted living foreign people, and its Tunisian and Indian displays were staged as bazaars that presented foreign cultures as exotic goods (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Qureshi 2011; Raizman & Robey 2018).
  • Classification and juries — the organisers imposed a system of classes and juries, and the texts see a tension between that order of knowledge and the real space of the building (Giberti 2002; Lawn 2009; Burris 2001).
  • Engineering and design — the Crystal Palace is read as a triumph of mass production, and its interior as a test of Owen Jones's Islamic-derived ideas (Allwood et al. 2001; Çelik 1992; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Money, surplus and museums — the profit bought the South Kensington land on which museums and colleges were built, a legacy the texts return to often (Findling & Pelle 1990; Roche 2000; Mandell 1967; Bennett 2004).

Questions for study

  1. Hoffenberg argues that historians have fixated on 1851 at the expense of the later London and colonial shows. Which of those later exhibitions would most change our view of 1851, and why? (Hoffenberg 2001; Geppert 2010)
  2. Burris insists that the Royal Commission did not plan the fair as colonial propaganda, while Hinsley and Qureshi read its displays as imperial and commercial. Can one reading hold both views, and what evidence would decide between them? (Burris 2001; Karp & Lavine 1991; Qureshi 2011)
  3. Hanson argues that the fair built a model of the East that cancelled physical distance while confirming cultural difference. How did a display of goods persuade visitors that they were seeing whole worlds? (Raizman & Robey 2018)
  4. Davis calls the fair a propaganda weapon yet judges claims that it caused the 1850s boom probably groundless. What would be needed to show that an exhibition changed an economy? (Findling & Pelle 2008)
  5. The texts give different figures for exhibitors, foreign nations and iron columns. How should students weigh counts that differ between reference sources and the scholarly accounts? (Findling & Pelle 1990; Badger 1979; Benedict 1983; Allwood et al. 2001; Roche 2000)
  6. Mathur argues that the vogue for Indian goods came from mass consumption in European cities rather than from any quality of the East. How far does that explanation fit the living peoples displayed at the same fair? (Mathur 2007; Qureshi 2011)

Giberti, Bruno. Designing the Centennial: A History of the 1876 International Exhibition in Philadelphia. Lexington: University Press of Kentucky, 2002.
ch. 1, “The Order of the Exhibition”, section “London, 1851”, with the Paris 1855 comparison that follows

Giberti’s study of the Philadelphia Centennial Exhibition of 1876 treats the 1851 London exhibition as the key precedent for bureaucratic ordering of international fairs. He describes how the London organisers adopted Albert’s four headings and Lyon Playfair’s thirty classes, with juries weighted towards manufactures, yet had to arrange the installation by country and court because foreign exhibits could not be predicted. He argues that 1851 already showed the conflict between an abstract order of knowledge and the real space of the building, which later fairs inherited. He sets the London system beside the Paris exhibition of 1855, where the French separated art from industry and built a different classification.

Hoffenberg treats the 1851 Great Exhibition in Hyde Park as the “parent” of a long series of exhibitions and argues that historians have been fixated on it at the expense of the post-1851 shows. Wikipedia

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations, also known as the Great Exhibition or the Crystal Palace Exhibition (in reference to the temporary structure in which it was held), was an international exhibition that took place in Hyde Park, London, from 1 May to 15 October 1851. It was the first in a series of world's fairs, exhibitions of culture and industry that became popular in the 19th century. The event was organised by Henry Cole and Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. Famous people of the time attended the Great Exhibition, including Charles Darwin, Karl Marx, C.H. Spurgeon, Michael Faraday (who assisted with the planning and judging of exhibits), Samuel Colt, John Mercer, members of the Orléanist royal family and the writers Charlotte Brontë, Charles Dickens, Lewis Carroll, George Eliot, Alfred Tennyson, and William Makepeace Thackeray.
