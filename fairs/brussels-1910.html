Brussels Universal and International Exhibition

Exposition Universelle et Internationale de Bruxelles 1910
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Brussels Universal and International Exhibition ran from 23 April to 7 November 1910 (Hurp. 145). It was the Belgian state's showcase, thirteen years after the 1897 Brussels fair, and it had large colonial and fine-art sections (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). A fire on 14 August destroyed several buildings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). Historians use it to study colonial display and the very large scale of the fairs of the period (Filipová 2015p. 3; Roche 2000p. 43).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Greenhalgh's encyclopedia entry presents the 1910 fair as confirming Belgium's role as a major host of international events and as showing the heavy commitment of the Belgian government to international display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). Depelchin places it in a run of Belgian showcases from Brussels in 1888 to Ghent in 1913, and counts seven international exhibitions in Belgium over 28 years (Filipová 2015p. 184; Filipová 2015p. 185). Filipová's introduction calls the large fairs of the period mega-events, whose political agendas and invested capital made them comparable to the established fairs of Western Europe and the US (Filipová 2015p. 3).

Greenhalgh's 1988 book reads the Belgian fairs as evidence of imperial ambition. After France, Britain and the Americans, Belgium had the most consistent policy of holding international exhibitions, and the 1897 and 1910 Brussels fairs both had extensive imperial sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Roche counts the 1910 fair among four Belgian expositions with prominent colonial themes before 1914, and records that Belgium had colonised the Congo from 1878 (Roche 2000p. 58).

Organisation and finance

The Ministry of Finance and Public Works carried out most of the organisation, and the Ministry of War supplied administrative support for the military and imperial elements (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). The minister, Count de Smit de Naeyer, was the controlling figure. King Albert held a symbolic role as high patron (haute protecteur) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). The 1990 Historical Dictionary spells the minister's name Smet (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 209).

The figures do not agree. Allwood's table gives 13,000,000 visitors, a loss of £10,000 and a site of 220 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Roche's table gives 13.0 million visits and a loss (Roche 2000p. 43). Badger's appendix gives 200 acres, receipts of £692,933, costs of £702,933 and an attendance of 4,196,939 (Badger 1979p. 131). This essay follows the reference table on attendance and sets Badger's much lower count against it. Depelchin puts the cost at 18,000,000 Belgian francs, against 16,500,000 for Ghent in 1913 (Filipová 2015p. 187).

Britain set up a special royal commission for its part at Brussels 1910, as it did for Turin and Rome in 1911. Ulick Wintour, secretary to the Special Committee, became the first director of the new branch in 1908 (Geppert 2010p. 219). Geppert calls the British committee the only one in Europe run by the state rather than by private enterprise, and calls this ironic (Geppert 2010p. 220).

Site and architecture

The site lay southwest of the parc de Solbosch and northwest of the bois de la Cambre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208). Its feature was a series of ornamental gardens that led to a Jardin de la Ville de Paris and a Jardin Hollandais. Popular literature called it the most beautiful site ever used for an international exposition, a claim Greenhalgh calls dubious (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208). The architecture was almost entirely historicist, copying earlier styles. The most notable official building was the Machine Hall, which had more floor space than the Galeries des Machines of the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208).

Exhibits and attractions

The exhibits were sorted into twenty-two groups, each with its own executive and judges. They included Education, Works of Art, Engineering, Agriculture, Mining, Textiles, Woman's Labor, the Army and Navy, and Colonies. A Congress Hall held commercial and academic meetings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208). The fine arts were a major draw. Flemish art was the key attraction, and a Maison de Rubens housed old art (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209). The modern section was chiefly the French contribution. It included Monet, Renoir and Rodin, and also Matisse, Signac, Bonnard, Vuillard and Denis, a choice Greenhalgh credits to the chief commissioner for fine arts, André Saglio (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211).

Education was a second strand. Fuchs says the Brussels fair brought the breakthrough for German school exhibitions, because many teachers attended (Lawn 2009p. 57). The German education exhibition there had a teachers' library and sections on school hygiene, the sciences, drawing and handicrafts (Lawn 2009p. 58). Berlin and Frankfurt offered buildings to house it, and it later became a school museum for the whole system (Lawn 2009p. 57; Lawn 2009p. 58).

Nations and colonies on show

Greenhalgh names the major participants as France, the Netherlands, Britain (including Ireland), Russia, Germany, Brazil, Spain and Italy. A pavilion for all other nations held several dozen countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). The colonies and the fine arts were the most popular areas. The French put on the most dramatic colonial display, with pavilions for Indochina, West Africa, Algeria and Tunisia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). The Algerian and Tunisian pavilions were in North African styles, and Algerians and Tunisians in traditional dress staffed them. The French interiors showed the economic prosperity France had brought (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). The Belgian Congo pavilions were impressive but outshone by the French, and the British spent little effort on the colonial area (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209). The Asian displays are discussed in the Asia section below.

Coté lists Brussels 1910 among the world exhibitions at which the Netherlands showed its exhibits, with London 1851 and Paris 1878 (Cotép. 2). Coté says the Dutch made "working villages" and other native exhibitions a regular feature of both home and international fairs (Cotép. 2). The article does not say that a Dutch native village stood at Brussels.

Visitors, the fire and reception

On Sunday 14 August, shortly before nine in the evening and after most of the day's crowd had left, a fire broke out near the central gallery (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206). It spread to the British section, the Paris city pavilion and a French restaurant. It was brought under control after several hours, and no lives were lost (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 209). Allwood places the fire in the afternoon and describes the Grand Palais as reduced to twisted girders by the following day (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). The texts disagree on the time of day. Greenhalgh says the fire greatly increased the publicity surrounding the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208).

Depelchin compares Brussels with Ghent. Ghent's six-month run drew 9.5 million visitors, and the total for Brussels was 3.5 million higher, but the chapter does not give that total (Filipová 2015p. 186).

Politics and the Berlin debate

Geppert uses the fair to trace the German debate about a world exhibition in Berlin. German success at Brussels in 1910 revived the question of a Berlin exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 35). When Wilhelm II visited the Brussels exposition, the Kaiser restated the objections: Berlin lacked a suitable site, the financial resources, and the general attraction for visitors and tourists (Geppert 2010p. 35). A newspaper quoted the Kaiser's explanation: "The average Berliner, the Kaiser explained, works overmuch and thus has no time to visit exhibitions" (Geppert 2010p. 35).

Tervuren and the Musée du Congo Belge

The colonial exhibition of 1897 left a museum behind. Its centrepiece, the Palais des Colonies at Tervuren, was reopened to the public in 1898 as the Musée du Congo (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Leopold II then decided to build a larger museum, begun in 1904 and finished by 1909, which became both the colonial section of the 1910 Brussels exposition and the Musée du Congo Belge (Findling & Pelle 2008). The 1897 colonial section had been far more populated: over 260 Congolese were present, and the colonial section drew between 1.2 and 1.8 million people (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Seven of the Africans housed above local stables died (Findling & Pelle 2008). The passages read for this paragraph describe no Congolese people on show at Brussels in 1910. The colonial pavilions there were "impressive but outdone by the French display" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209).

What became of the German education exhibition

After the fair, Berlin and Frankfurt offered public buildings to house the German education exhibition, and Ludwig Pallat, a government councillor in the Education Ministry, negotiated the allocation of exhibits with private and state exhibitors (Lawn 2009p. 57). The show opened at the Friedrichsgymnasium in Berlin and soon moved to a school for compulsory further training in Friedrichsstrasse (Lawn 2009p. 58). In 1913 the textbook information point was extended to the whole school system and merged with the exhibition (Lawn 2009p. 58). Fuchs argues that there was no further reason why the conversion of the Brussels exhibition into a museum for the entire school system should not take place (Lawn 2009p. 58). The institute that followed was narrower: the Ministry decided in 1911 to build a Prussian school museum, and the Central Institute for Education, opened in 1915, was "at first a solely Prussian facility" (Lawn 2009p. 58). The essay's claim that the exhibition later became a school museum for the whole system therefore overstates the outcome Fuchs describes.

Press and publicity

The fair's organisers published a monthly journal of fair news, the Moniteur de l'exposition universelle et internationale Bruxelles 1910, from November 1907 to December 1910. It appeared monthly before the opening and more often during the run (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). Official catalogues and the Livre d'or of 1910 were the other printed sources (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209). The New York Times and The Times of London both ran occasional articles, especially about the mid-August fire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209). Allwood quotes an unnamed visitor who had been at the Exposition the day before the fire, returned to the hotel tired, and went back to watch it burn after tea. The visitor called it "splendid sight, but a very sad one" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87).

State control of exhibitions

Geppert places the Belgian fairs within a wider move towards state control. A Belgian national exhibition committee was set up in 1903, with similar bodies in Italy, Hungary, Denmark, Switzerland and Austria (Geppert 2010p. 220). The conferences of 1907 and 1912 aimed to limit the number of great international exhibitions and to set uniform rules (Geppert 2010p. 220). The second, in Berlin in October 1912, agreed on a Fédération internationale des comités permanents d'expositions based in Brussels, under the French senator Emile Dupont as first chairman (Geppert 2010p. 220). The convention that followed was signed by fifteen European countries and Japan but was never ratified because of the First World War (Geppert 2010p. 220).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh's encyclopedia and dictionary entries are descriptive. They present the fair as a state event with a colonial core (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 206; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). Roche argues that Belgium was the European country most committed to imperial expositions, and that the colonial exposition was also copied within the colonised territories (Roche 2000p. 58).

Filipová and Depelchin read the fair through scale and comparison. Filipová places it among mega-events (Filipová 2015p. 3). Depelchin treats Brussels 1910 as the benchmark for Ghent 1913, which Depelchin says seemingly lacked the exotic pavilions of Brussels and Liège (Filipová 2015p. 191). The chapter then qualifies that reading (Filipová 2015p. 191). Geppert calls Brussels an under-researched hub and counts the 1910 fair among the large expositions of 1880 to 1910, which Geppert uses to argue that complaints of exhibition fatigue did not halt the growth of fairs (Geppert 2010p. 13; Geppert 2010p. 213). Fuchs reads the German education exhibition as a case of states using fairs for cultural politics (Lawn 2009p. 53).

The readings differ on the fair's character. The reference entries and Greenhalgh's book stress the Belgian state and its empire, while Filipová and Roche stress scale. The texts disagree on the time of the fire, on attendance, and on Japan's participation, which the Asia section sets out.

Depelchin explains the difference in exotic display between Brussels and Ghent by the organisers' attitude to local history. Brussels, a modern, cosmopolitan capital, needed less to anchor itself in its past (Filipová 2015p. 195). Allwood counts Brussels 1888 and 1897 among Belgium's international exhibitions and says Belgium held more shows than France (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). Depelchin's footnote warns that the Encyclopaedia of World's Fairs does not include the 1888 Brussels exhibition, although national historiography treats it as a genuinely international show (Filipová 2015p. 197). A count of Belgian fairs therefore depends on definition.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

The texts disagree about whether Japan took part. Hotta-Lister says Japan decided against exhibiting at Brussels so as to concentrate on the Japan-British Exhibition in London, which mattered more to Japan, while Britain chose to take part in Brussels (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 107). Hur says the Japanese government decided against official participation because of the London show, and that only some merchants sent exhibits individually (Hurp. 145). Hur adds that Japanese exhibitors, who were not allowed to show national flags on the site, complained to the Japanese government. Prince Fushimi Sadanaru then went to Belgium and made the Japanese section official, with the Japanese ambassador to Belgium as general director. A formal opening took place on 2 July (Hurp. 145). Hur says the Japanese exhibits drew less public attention than the Chinese ones (Hurp. 145). The texts do not say what the Japanese merchants showed, or who paid for the section.

China

Hur says the Chinese pavilion was highly praised and represented the Far East to Europe (Hurp. 145). The Shibao newspaper called the affair "a demonstration of development of the Chinese diplomacy" (Hurp. 145). Hur suggests this might be the first ever favourable Chinese press verdict on the Qing government's performance at international exhibitions. The same report still criticised the poor location and unsatisfying appearance of the Chinese pavilion (Hurp. 145; Hurp. 146). The Chinese newspaper Shenbao also printed an article on the Chinese exhibits at Brussels dated 14 August 1910 (Hurp. 146). The texts do not say who organised or paid for the Chinese pavilion.

Zhang Yuanji, a Chinese publisher, was abroad in 1910 and visited both the Japan-British Exhibition and Brussels (Hurp. 156). Zhang's essay on China's future participation in overseas expositions appeared in the journal Dongfang zazhi in October 1910 (Hurp. 156).

French Indochina

The French put on the most dramatic colonial display, including a pavilion for Indochina (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 208; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). The texts do not say who paid for it. The people described in traditional dress at the French pavilions were Algerians and Tunisians, not Asians (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 211). None of the passages gathered from our texts describes Asian people displayed at Brussels in 1910.

Interpretations

Hur reads the two 1910 fairs as one contest. At Brussels, China gained the satisfaction of beating Japan at the site of the exposition. The London fair presented Japan as the Island Empire of the East, on a par with Britain (Hurp. 146). Hur contrasts this with Japan's colonial displays in London, where Taiwan, Korea and Manchuria took up much of the Palace of the Orient (Hurp. 146). Hotta-Lister points out that Japanese books on international exhibitions omit the London fair but include the Brussels exhibition of 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 183).

Themes in the scholarship

  • State organisation and finance — The fair was run by the Belgian ministries of finance and war under a minister and a royal patron. The texts give conflicting attendance and loss figures. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Roche 2000; Badger 1979; Geppert 2010)
  • Colonial and imperial display — The French colonial pavilions were the most dramatic display, and the Belgian Congo sections were secondary. Authors link Belgian fairs to imperial ambition. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Greenhalgh 1988; Roche 2000)
  • Fire and publicity — The fire of 14 August was a near-disaster that drew publicity. The texts disagree on when it started. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • Fine arts — Flemish art was the main draw, and the French modern section was unusually open. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990)
  • Education and national display — The German education exhibition at Brussels led to a school museum. (Lawn 2009)
  • Japan, China and rivalry — Japan's and China's participation was a matter of prestige, and the sources disagree about Japan's role. (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999)
  • Scale and mega-events — Brussels is treated as a mega-event, but its scale depends on which attendance figure is used. (Filipová 2015; Roche 2000; Allwood et al. 2001; Badger 1979)

Questions for study

  1. How far can colonial pavilions be read as state propaganda when the texts say little about who paid for them?
  2. Why do the Japanese and Chinese accounts of Brussels differ from those of the London fair, and what does that say about prestige at exhibitions?
  3. What makes a fair a "mega-event", and does the answer depend on attendance, on cost or on political meaning?
  4. How did the German debate about a Berlin exhibition shape German attitudes to fairs held abroad?
  5. What did the German education exhibition at Brussels change about the use of school exhibits by the state?
  6. Why do attendance and loss figures for the same fair differ so widely, and what should a historian do with them?
  7. The Tervuren museum became the 1910 colonial section and then the Musée du Congo Belge. What did the Brussels colonial pavilions add to its collections, and what do the sources not record?
  8. Fuchs argues for a museum of the whole school system, yet the 1915 institute was Prussian only. Why did German states and cities resist a national school museum, and what did Brussels contribute to the argument?
  9. If the count of Belgian international exhibitions depends on definition, how should a historian decide which Belgian fairs belong in a comparison with Brussels 1910?

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
FourThe Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, the Last Show of the Qing Dynastyp. 124Asia

Hur turns to the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, held in Nanjing from June to November 1910, the first national exposition hosted by China and the last of the Qing dynasty. The chapter traces the project to Duanfang, the Manchu governor-general who proposed it in 1908 and modeled its regulations on Japan's domestic exhibitions, and to his successor Zhang Renjun after Duanfang's transfer in 1909. Hur describes the preparations, including a site of about 165 acres, pavilions for twenty-two provinces, foreign samples buildings, and the security measures taken amid fears of unrest in Nanjing. The main argument concerns the Chinese press, which, unlike its coverage of Osaka and St. Louis, was mostly descriptive or positive, a shift Hur ties to the constitutional movement and to newspapers and organizers who backed it. The chapter also covers the large Japanese visiting groups and the overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia. It concludes that the exposition was a momentary display of an integrated, constitutional China that faded with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

In the contents of this work

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
All the World into the School: World's Fairs and the emergence of the school museum in the nineteenth centuryEckhardt Fuchsp. 51

Fuchs traces how school museums emerged in Germany and abroad from the second half of the nineteenth century, arguing that they grew out of temporary education exhibits at world's fairs and were shaped by teachers' bid for professional status. The chapter distinguishes three developments: fair exhibitions converted into permanent museum displays, school museums serving the professional aims of elementary teachers, and a wider school-reform movement around 1900. The evidence is institutional. Fuchs follows the education section of the Paris 1867 fair into the Musee Pedagogique founded in 1879, and the German exhibitions at Chicago 1893 and St Louis 1904, which did not become a national museum. A national-scale German institute followed only after the Brussels fair of 1910, opening as the Central Institute for Education in 1915. Two tables list school museums founded in Germany and abroad, most set up by teachers' associations, and most were small, local and limited to teaching materials. The founding boom faded after 1920, when teacher training improved and schools were better equipped.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

In the contents of this work

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Introduction: communities real and imagined: world’s fairs and political meaningsDavid Raizman and Ethan Robeyp. 1

Raizman and Robey introduce the volume by asking how the world's fairs of 1851 to 1915 used manufactured goods to express national identity. They argue that earlier design history treated fairs as a foil to modernism, and that the fairs were networks of exchange rather than single, unified displays. Pevsner's dismissal of the Crystal Palace is challenged by later critics, from Banham and Sparke to Greenhalgh, Bennett and Mitchell, the last three reading the fairs as imperial spectacle. The editors use Anderson's 'imagined communities' to tie national identity to material culture. Their evidence is the Albert Memorial, Owen Jones's interior and Grammar of Ornament, and the contributors' cases: the 1888 Copenhagen Nordic Exhibition, the Nanjing and South American centenaries of 1910, Italy at Brussels and Antwerp, and the Finnish pavilion at Paris in 1900. They end with the outbreak of World War I, when the ideal of 'peaceful competition' broke down.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Wikipedia

The Brussels International Exposition (French: Exposition universelle et internationale de Bruxelles; Dutch: Wereldtentoonstelling van Brussel) of 1910 was a world's fair held in Brussels, Belgium, from 23 April to 7 November 1910. This was just thirteen years after Brussels' previous world's fair. It received 13 million visitors, covered 88 hectares (220 acres) and lost 100,000 Belgian francs.
