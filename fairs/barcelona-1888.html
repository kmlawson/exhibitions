Universal Exposition of Barcelona

Exposición Universal de Barcelona
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Universal Exposition of Barcelona, the Exposició Universal de Barcelona, was Spain's first international exhibition. It opened in the spring of 1888 in the Ciutadella Park and closed on 9 December after drawing more than two million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95). It began as a private scheme that the city council took over, and its foreign presence was modest (Boone 2019p. 51; Filipová 2015p. 58). Historians value it chiefly for its effect on Barcelona as a modern city, and for what it revealed about Catalan and Spanish identity (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Boone 2019p. 51).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair began as a private scheme. In 1885 Eugenio Serrano de Casanova offered the city council a plan without asking for money, in return for a commission on the takings (Filipová 2015p. 48). Boone says Serrano had been with the Spanish delegation at Philadelphia (Boone 2019p. 51). The opening planned for 1887 slipped a year because of Serrano's troubles and strikes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94). The mayor, Francesc Rius i Taulet, then took over, and the city's industrialists and bourgeois elite joined the venture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94). Muñoz Torreblanca sets the fair against the Febre d'Or (Gold Fever), the boom of 1871 to 1885, when it was seen as a second chance for Catalan industrialists (Filipová 2015p. 48). Boone adds that Rius saw the fair as a way to open discussion of Barcelona's relationship with Madrid, while Catalan nationalists such as Valentí Almirall opposed any collaboration with the central government (Boone 2019p. 52). The fair fell in the Restoration, the constitutional monarchy set up in 1875, in which the Liberal and Conservative parties took turns in government (Boone 2019p. 49; Boone 2019p. 50).

Organisation and finance

The texts give different figures for the money. Muñoz Torreblanca reports an initial grant of 500,000 pesetas from the city and six million reals from the state, and a final cost of 8,534,441 pesetas (Filipová 2015p. 48; Filipová 2015p. 48). Boone describes a state loan of two million pesetas, to be repaid from receipts (Boone 2019p. 52). Both make Madrid a lender rather than an organiser. The city had to repay the state from profits, or otherwise return 75 per cent of the sum in six instalments (Filipová 2015p. 49). The executive committee formed in 1886 drew on industrialists and conservative politicians, with no place for the monarchy or the government (Filipová 2015p. 49).

Site and architecture

The fair stood in the Ciutadella Park, ground with a bitter history: after the War of the Spanish Succession the neighbourhood there was razed and a fortress built to serve as a prison (Boone 2019p. 52). The sources give the site as 115 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94) or about 450,000 square metres (Filipová 2015p. 50). The Palace of Industry, designed by Alexandre Sallé, dominated the grounds (Filipová 2015p. 51). Its size is given as twenty-four naves (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94) or as a fan-shaped building of 25 aisles on an 1878 Paris model (Filipová 2015p. 56). The main entrance was Josep Vilaseca's Arch of Triumph (Boone 2019p. 53). Boone reads its brick Mudéjar features (a style developed by Muslim craftsmen under Christian rule) and its shields for all forty-nine provinces as a design that "promoted a message of national unification at the fair" (Boone 2019p. 54; Boone 2019p. 55). Modernism had little place: the chapter counts two buildings by Modernist architects, Domènech i Muntaner's café-restaurant and Gaudí's Palau Güell, a private residence (Filipová 2015p. 50). Rejected tower proposals meant there was no Eiffel-type monument (Filipová 2015p. 51).

Exhibits and attractions

Catalan exhibitors filled seven of the Palace's aisles (Filipová 2015p. 57). The Spanish government held 4,000 square metres for its ministries, France 6,600 and Austria-Hungary 3,000 (Filipová 2015p. 57). Fine art was thin. Boone counts 646 works by 349 exhibitors, against about 2,000 paintings in the Madrid national exhibition of 1887 (Boone 2019p. 61), and says most visitors were disappointed by the lack of work from foreign artists (Boone 2019p. 61). Madrid's national museum lent eight large canvases, five of them death scenes (Boone 2019p. 61; Boone 2019p. 61). A magic fountain was lit by voltaic arcs (Filipová 2015p. 54), and a captive balloon carried visitors to about 300 metres until lightning destroyed the first one after ten days (Filipová 2015p. 54). Concerts featured Isaac Albéniz, and congresses met on archaeology, economics and medicine (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95).

Visitors and reception

Muñoz Torreblanca gives 12,223 exhibitors, a quarter of the number at Paris in 1867 (Filipová 2015p. 60). The encyclopedias give 12,203 exhibitors from 27 countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 101). Visitors numbered just over two million in 245 days, according to Muñoz Torreblanca (Filipová 2015p. 60), and more than two million in the encyclopedias (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95). A ticket cost one peseta, a season ticket 25 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Filipová 2015p. 60). The working classes were largely kept out (Filipová 2015p. 60), and free passes went to people who could have paid (Filipová 2015p. 60). Muñoz Torreblanca judges the total the lowest of the so-called universal exhibitions (Filipová 2015p. 61). Only nine European countries attended, bringing foreign participants to nineteen (Filipová 2015p. 58). The royal family and the prime minister presided at the opening, which Muñoz Torreblanca reads as giving the event a national profile (Filipová 2015p. 49).

Politics and controversy

The Catalanist response was divided. Some boycotted the opening (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95). Centre Català members boycotted the Jocs Florals (Floral Games), a literary contest, after its dates were moved so the queen regent could preside, and held a rival event (Boone 2019p. 67). The Lliga de Catalunya, a less militant nationalist group, sent the queen regent a message in Catalan, signed by more than 300 people, asking for Catalan rights (Boone 2019p. 67; Filipová 2015p. 59; Filipová 2015p. 59). The games still ended with the queen regent presiding as their queen (Filipová 2015p. 59). The satirical La Esquella de la Torratxa ridiculed the mayor and the fair, but made few cutting remarks about the queen regent. Its closing cartoon shows her leaving the Palace of Industry with the word for deficit pinned to her skirt (Boone 2019p. 67; Boone 2019p. 67).

Aftermath and legacy

The volume's introduction argues that the fair's greatest mark was on the urban transformation of Barcelona, not on the history of exhibitions (Filipová 2015p. 7). The Ciutadella works and the Eixample, the new gridded district of the city, were part of that change (Filipová 2015p. 49; Filipová 2015p. 50). Lit fountains and electric lighting became a Barcelona tradition that led to the 1929 fair (Filipová 2015p. 54). Roche says the 1888 expo helped create the city's first urban park (Roche 2000p. 146). The Barcelona Zoo was founded after the fair and took over many of its buildings (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41). The 1888 success also led planners of the 1929 exhibition to add a general Spanish exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103).

How historians have interpreted it

The scholarship disagrees about what kind of event the fair was. Muñoz Torreblanca calls it "a magnificent regional exhibition with universal aspirations" (Filipová 2015p. 62), and argues that its aims rested on promise more than delivery (Filipová 2015p. 62). Del Pozo Andrés calls it "eminently local and not national", and says its pedagogic congress, mostly of Catalan educators, staged the first public debate in Spain on teaching children in their mother tongue (Lawn 2009p. 163; Lawn 2009p. 163; Lawn 2009p. 164). Boone reads the Barcelona and Paris fairs as arenas in which the Restoration government's idea of Spain was defended against Catalan and regional claims (Boone 2019p. 50), and cites Jacobson's view that the fair temporarily united the country while ultimately producing greater division (Boone 2019p. 68). McDonogh's encyclopedia entries stress the Catalan bourgeoisie: the fair's legacy was "a statement of bourgeois Catalan expectations" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 101), and a watershed for Barcelona's growth as a modern European city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95). Geppert argues that large exhibitions could only be held in big cities, chiefly capitals, for almost forty years after 1851, and names Barcelona and Liège as the first two European fairs outside capitals (Geppert 2010p. 222; Geppert 2010p. 222). Della Coletta, drawing on Epps, reads the Citadel, a site of repression, as turned into a place of diversion, and sees Barcelona's cosmopolitan aims as tied to national ones (Della Coletta 2006p. 275; Della Coletta 2006p. 45). Roche places the fair among the mega-events behind Barcelona's later tourism (Roche 2000p. 146).

The sources agree that the fair's largest effect was on the city itself, and Muñoz Torreblanca judges it weaker than the great fairs in exhibitors, visitors and foreign participation (Filipová 2015p. 60; Filipová 2015p. 61). They differ on whether it was regional or national. Muñoz Torreblanca reads the royal presence as giving a national profile (Filipová 2015p. 49); del Pozo Andrés calls it local (Lawn 2009p. 163). The figures also conflict. The opening was 8 April in a bracketed note in Muñoz Torreblanca's chapter (Filipová 2015p. 58), 8 May in both encyclopedias (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 94; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 100), and 20 May in Boone (Boone 2019p. 55).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

China and Japan

The texts say little about the East Asian presence. The two encyclopedias list the United States, China and Japan among the countries exhibiting, and say their displays were especially popular (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 101). Muñoz Torreblanca says less. The Chinese, Japanese and Turkish empires had little more than a token presence in the Palace of Industry, with Portugal and the Latin American republics (Filipová 2015p. 57). Boone quotes Pellicer, a critic who reviewed the art, asking whether the Japanese had stopped being artists since becoming industrious. Pellicer used this to argue for supporting fine art in Barcelona (Boone 2019p. 65). No source names a Chinese or Japanese pavilion, a commissioner or an observer at the fair.

The Philippines and the colonial pavilions

The Philippines was the Asian place most often named, and it appeared as a colony. A Philippines tobacco company, a private firm, ran a pavilion in which Tagalog cigarette-makers rolled cigarettes in public (Filipová 2015p. 54). Muñoz Torreblanca calls this the most exotic thing a visitor could see at the fair (Filipová 2015p. 54). The same Tagalogs had been shown at the General Exhibition of the Philippines held in Madrid in 1887, but the other Filipinos who had gone to Madrid did not come to Barcelona (Filipová 2015p. 54; Filipová 2015p. 56). Objects from the Madrid exhibition were not brought to Barcelona either (Filipová 2015p. 56). The Pavilion of Colonies, 60 metres long, was meant to show products from Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines (Filipová 2015p. 56). Lacking objects, it became little more than a warehouse of miscellaneous items (Filipová 2015p. 56). The government's main gallery held objects sorted by ministry, including the overseas ministry (Filipová 2015p. 57). Boone notes that Catalan prosperity depended on access to the remaining colonies of Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines (Boone 2019p. 52).

Exotic villages and the colonial dimension

Barcelona had no exotic village of the kind shown at Paris in 1878 (Filipová 2015p. 54). Muñoz Torreblanca argues that the colonial display was understated because Spain's colonial position was weakening, and notes that Spain lost its last American colonies in 1898 (Filipová 2015p. 54; Filipová 2015p. 56). The understated colonial dimension, with its popular exotic spectacles, reduced the fair's universal character, the chapter concludes (Filipová 2015p. 61). The encyclopedias describe Spain's colonial showcases in the Caribbean, Africa and the Far East as standing beside exhibits from Europe and Latin America (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 101). The texts do not say which possessions lay in the Far East.

South Asia and the organisers

The texts say nothing about South Asian exhibits, peoples or colonies at the Barcelona fair. No source says who paid for the Asian displays. The Philippines pavilion belonged to a company, and the Pavilion of Colonies was attached to the Palace of Industry as a supplementary hall (Filipová 2015p. 56), but the texts do not say who paid for either.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial display — The sources treat the Philippines and the other colonies as a secondary part of the fair, with the Pavilion of Colonies short of objects (Filipová 2015p. 56; Filipová 2015p. 56).
  • Catalan regionalism — The Jocs Florals and the message to the queen regent show how the fair became a stage for Catalan demands (Boone 2019p. 67; Filipová 2015p. 59).
  • Spanish identity — The arch and the royal opening were read as national statements, and the fair was also contested (Boone 2019p. 55; Lawn 2009p. 163).
  • Urban transformation — The volume's introduction calls the city's changes the fair's greatest mark, which the encyclopedias also stress (Filipová 2015p. 7; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 95).
  • Visitors and exhibitors — The figures differ, and the chapter judges the attendance the lowest of the universal exhibitions (Filipová 2015p. 60; Filipová 2015p. 61).
  • Art and display — The fine-art section was small, and the catalogue arranged Spanish works by province (Boone 2019p. 61; Boone 2019p. 62).
  • Legacy — The fair's influence on lit fountains, the park and the later 1929 exhibition is noted in several texts (Filipová 2015p. 54; Roche 2000p. 146; Allwood et al. 2001p. 103).

Questions for study

  1. If the organisers aimed at a universal exhibition, how did the sources decide that the fair failed the test, and what measures did they use?
  2. How far can a regional fair carry a national message, as the arch and the royal opening suggest?
  3. The sources disagree on the opening day, the state's money and the size of the exhibitors' numbers. What does that tell us about how the records of such fairs survive?
  4. Why do the scholars value the fair most for its effect on the city, and what does that say about how they judge exhibitions?
  5. Muñoz Torreblanca reads the colonial displays as understated, while the encyclopedias describe them as popular. What evidence would settle the question?

Boone, M. Elizabeth. “The Spanish Element in Our Nationality”: Spain and America at the World’s Fairs and Centennial Celebrations, 1876–1915. University Park: Pennsylvania State University Press, 2019.
Introductionp. 1

Boone opens with Walt Whitman's 1883 insistence that Spanish character must be recognised in American identity, and argues that an English-only national story has erased Spain from United States history. The book examines Spain's presence at five celebrations: Philadelphia 1876, Chicago 1893, San Francisco and San Diego in 1915, the Barcelona and Paris fairs of 1888 and 1889, and the 1910 centenaries in Buenos Aires, Mexico City and Santiago. Using paintings, ephemeral architecture, posters and press responses, it asks why Spain was displayed, refused or misread at each event, and how these displays helped shape national identities across the Americas. Boone draws on theories of exhibition, memory and transnational study, treating fairs as exercises in using the past to imagine a national future. The introduction ends by considering present-day consequences, including the contested reputations of Junípero Serra and Bernardo de Gálvez and the large Spanish-speaking population of the United States.

2Defining (and Defending) Spain in Barcelona and Paris, 1888 and 1889p. 49

Boone asks how Spain was defined at two fairs held a year apart, the Exposició Universal in Barcelona in 1888 and the Exposition Universelle in Paris in 1889. Spain's Restoration monarchy was divided between Liberals and Conservatives, and Catalan nationalists opposed any collaboration with Madrid, so each fair offered a different Spain. Barcelona is the main case: the chapter follows the fair's organisation, its site in the Parc de la Ciutadella, and the inauguration attended by the queen regent and the young Alfonso XIII. It then examines the fine-arts display, arranged by province and reviewed by Pellicer and Yxart. In Paris the official presence was a food pavilion by Arturo Mélida, built in neo-Mudéjar style, and a painting gallery where the jury gave its top prize to Luis Jiménez Aranda's hospital scene rather than to the government's history canvases by Gisbert and Casado. French critics dismissed those canvases as gruesome and theatrical, while Pardo Bazán defended them. The chapter concludes that the fairs turned a contested nation into a simpler story, one that was most persuasive outside Spain.

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
Introduction: The Margins of Exhibitions and Exhibitions StudiesMarta Filipováp. 1Asia

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

2Barcelona’s Universal Exhibition of 1888: An Atypical Case of a Great ExhibitionMarina Muñoz Torreblancap. 45

Muñoz Torreblanca asks whether the Barcelona Universal Exhibition of 1888 belongs with the great exhibitions of Paris and London, or was a set of good intentions of little lasting significance. Muñoz Torreblanca first sets out the institutional background, noting that exhibitions before the 1928 Paris convention had no shared regulatory framework and that terms such as 'universal' and 'international' were often used as synonyms. The planning story runs from Spain's unrealised national exhibition projects to Eugenio Serrano de Casanova's private proposal, which the city council took over, and the evidence includes the Ciutadella site, the Palace of Industry, the magic fountain and captive balloon, the rejected tower projects, and a thin colonial display that the chapter ascribes to Spain's weakening colonial position. It also compares numbers, with about 12,000 exhibitors and just over two million visitors set against London in 1851 and Paris in 1867. The chapter concludes that the exhibition was more a regional event promoting Catalonia than a universal one, though its most lasting effect was the transformation of Barcelona itself.

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
The Bull and the Book: images of Spain and Spanish education in the World Fairs of the nineteenth century, 1851-1900Maria del Mar del Pozo Andrésp. 153

Del Pozo Andrés asks how Spain presented itself through education at the nineteenth-century world fairs, from London in 1851 to Paris in 1900. The argument is that Spanish participation was driven by a wish to restore national pride after imperial decline, and that the fairs staged a contradictory nationalism, boastful about prizes yet anxious about backwardness. The evidence comes from official catalogues, commissioners' reports and the press. Cases include the absence of Spanish teaching materials in London in 1862, the Paris pavilion of 1867 with its stuffed bull, the Philadelphia display of some 715 deluxe books that won ninety-three prizes in 1876, the dispute over an illiteracy chart at Paris in 1878, the pedagogic congress held in Barcelona in 1888 and the Paris pavilion of 1900. The chapter also follows Martin de Martin y Ruiz, a deaf, dumb and blind student shown in Vienna in 1873. It concludes that the fairs supplied new ideas, but that Spanish schools adopted them slowly, and graded schools took hold only after the loss of the colonies in 1898.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Boone follows Spain's presence at a sequence of international expositions and national centenaries between 1876 and 1915: Philadelphia 1876, Barcelona and Paris in 1888 and 1889, Chicago 1893, the 1910 centenaries in Buenos Aires, Santiago de Chile and Mexico City, and the San Francisco and San Diego fairs of 1915. Its argument is that these displays helped define national identities in the United States and Latin America, and that Spain was repeatedly refused, misread or pushed into the past, so that its modern role and its links to the Americas were forgotten. Using paintings, ephemeral architecture, posters, postcards and press responses, the chapters proceed chronologically, each a case study of one fair or pair of fairs. The introduction ends by considering present-day consequences, including the Spanish-speaking population of the United States.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are slight in this book, which is about Spain, the United States and Latin America. The Philippines are the main Southeast Asian presence, and only as a Spanish colony: hemp from the islands was shown at Philadelphia in 1876 (chapter 1), and abacá fibre from the Philippines sat in the Spanish agricultural gallery at Chicago in 1893 (chapter 3). Japan is the most concrete case. Pellicer, reviewing the Barcelona fine arts display in 1888, asked whether the Japanese had stopped being artists since becoming industrious (chapter 2). In 1910 the Oriental Steamship Company of Japan organised a Japanese decorative arts exhibition in Mexico City alongside the Spanish show, and its sales were far stronger than the Spanish display's (chapter 4). China appears only as one of the national vendors in a satirical image of the Centennial restaurants (chapter 1). None of the other regions on the list (Korea, Siam, Indonesia, Indochina) appears. Asia enters only as a category: Asian identity is named among the challenges to the Protestant national image in the Chicago chapter (chapter 3), and Asia is listed as a trading region the Panama Canal would link in the chapter on 1915 (chapter 5). The Orientalism discussed concerns North Africa and the Middle East, not East Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
Entry 'Barcelona 1888', pp. 94-95, by Gary McDonogh

The encyclopedia entry by Gary McDonogh presents the 1888 Barcelona exposition as Spain's first international exposition, one that dramatized Barcelona's resurgence as an industrial, commercial and cultural centre for Catalonia and for Spain as a whole. It covers the private start under Eugenio Serrano de Casanova, the mayor's takeover, the Ciutadella site and its pavilions, the royal opening, the Jocs Florals with their Catalanist boycott, the attendance and the closing. McDonogh argues that the fair celebrated the region's power and international connections while turning Barcelona towards a seemingly bountiful future. The entry calls the fair a watershed for Barcelona's growth as a modern European city and for the assertions of the new haute bourgeoisie, and gives only one sentence to Spain's colonial showcases.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. Wikipedia

The 1888 Barcelona Universal Exposition (in Catalan: Exposició Universal de Barcelona and Exposición Universal de Barcelona in Spanish) was Spain's first International World's Fair and ran from 8 April to 9 December 1888. The second one in Barcelona was held in 1929.
