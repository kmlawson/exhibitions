Coronation Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Coronation Exhibition, also called the Coronation Exhibition of the British Empire, was an imperial show held in 1911 at the White City, Shepherd's Bush, London, in the year George V was crowned (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39; Geppert 2010p. 117). Imre Kiralfy, who ran the White City exhibitions, was its Commissioner-General (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 40). Greenhalgh calls it Kiralfy's greatest achievement in imperial terms, and says it was to have more native villages than any other show in Britain, before or after it (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Greenhalgh 1988p. 93).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The White City series

Hotta-Lister reports that by 1907, it is said, Kiralfy had begun his own pleasure ground at Shepherd's Bush, which came to be called the White City after the Chicago exposition of 1893 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39). After the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908, Geppert describes the White City shows as expositions with broad themes of an imperial or national character, naming the Imperial International Exhibition of 1909 and the Coronation Exhibition of the British Empire of 1911 (Geppert 2010p. 117). The buildings were altered only slightly from season to season to suit each theme (Geppert 2010p. 117).

The 1909 and 1910 shows had few native villages. Greenhalgh says the 1909 Imperial International drew most attention to its Dahomeyan, Irish, Scottish and Kalmuck villages, and that the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition brought a Formosa hamlet (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92; Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). He says the scarcity was probably due to Kiralfy's plans for 1911. Of the Coronation Exhibition he writes: "This was to have more native villages and related displays than any before or after it in Britain" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). Kiralfy chose 1911 for his largest celebration of empire, with the British monarchy in focus because of the crowning of George V (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93).

Villages and attractions

Greenhalgh lists the villages. A Somali village had already appeared at the Bradford exhibition of 1904 and in Dublin in 1907, and it made its last British appearance as a holiday attraction at Douglas, Isle of Man, in 1912 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). Other villages were a tribe of Iroquois Indians from Canada, a Burmese village and theatre, a Ceylon village with entertainers, an unspecified African village and a Hindoo settlement with a Temple of Magic (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). The most popular attraction was a Maori village and theatre. Its party of fifty warriors and maidens of the Arawa tribe included Mita Taupopoki, a chief who had fought for the British against Te Kooti in 1873 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). Hotta-Lister gives a similar list: Somalis, Iroquois Indians from Canada, Burmese, Ceylonese, Africans, Hindus and Maoris from New Zealand (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117).

Empire and colony

Greenhalgh ties Kiralfy's imperial spectacle to his Jewish origins and his wish for national acceptance in Britain, and says that by 1914 Kiralfy was perhaps more responsible than anyone for the public's understanding of Britain's foreign territories (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). The frame he gives the show is the British monarchy, and the Official Daily Programme describes the Maori as among the most loyal subjects of King George (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). The passages read do not say which colonial governments arranged the villages in 1911, or how the people in them were recruited or paid.

Greenhalgh says the programme's passage on the Maori wars treats them clinically and lightly, and that a chief who had sided with the British was presented as an anthropological exhibit (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). His conclusion is that acceptance of "imperial violence as a natural order" is the most disturbing feature of such celebrations of conquest (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). Hotta-Lister defines a village as a display built by the colonial power to show native peoples, which ranked below a national pavilion (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). The organisers' aims are not stated in the passages read beyond the celebration of empire that Greenhalgh describes (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93).

Keeping the 1911 London shows apart

The Festival of Empire at the Crystal Palace, Sydenham, was a separate event. It celebrated the coronation of George V and imperial solidarity, and its opening day was 12 May 1911 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). The two reference works that mention the Coronation Exhibition do so while describing the Festival. They call the Coronation Exhibition at Shepherd's Bush a smaller competitor for summer crowds, and describe Shepherd's Bush as a park and amusement area (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 212; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). These descriptions differ from the accounts by Greenhalgh and Hotta-Lister, which describe a show with many villages (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). Both reference works also list the Coronation Exhibition among the fairs they leave out (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 401).

Visitors and reception

The passages read give no attendance figures or dates for the Coronation Exhibition. The only 1911 attendance figure in them, 144,234 on opening day, is for the Festival at Sydenham (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). None of the passages read records the reception of the Coronation Exhibition.

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh calls the 1911 show Kiralfy's greatest achievement in imperial terms, and treats the Maori village as an example of how readily these shows accepted imperial violence (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93; Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). Hotta-Lister reads the same villages as one instance of a common practice at the White City shows, that of including colonised peoples and their villages (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117).

Questions for study

  1. Why did Kiralfy plan more native villages for 1911 than for 1909 and 1910, and what did the coronation year add to his plans? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93)
  2. How far does the Official Daily Programme's account of the Maori, as Greenhalgh reproduces it, differ from Greenhalgh's own reading of it? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93)
  3. Is the 1911 show better described as a common practice of including colonised peoples, as Hotta-Lister argues, or as a celebration of the monarchy, as Greenhalgh's emphasis suggests? (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117; Greenhalgh 1988p. 93)
  4. How were the people of the 1911 villages recruited and paid? The passages read say nothing on this, and Greenhalgh says that in no instance is it known what such payments consisted of or how they were made (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92).

Chapters that treat it at length 2 in 2 works

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
4Human showcasesp. 82Asia

Greenhalgh examines the practice of displaying people at international exhibitions between 1889 and 1914, when colonised and other non-Western peoples were housed in reconstructed villages and watched by paying crowds. He sorts the displays into four types (imperial, educational, commercial and ambassadorial) but argues that they overlapped, and that the imperial type was the most numerous and lavish. The chapter works through the Paris 1889 and 1900 native villages, the Jardin d'Acclimatation's peopled exhibits that preceded them, Imre Kiralfy's London spectacles, and the Chicago, Buffalo and St Louis fairs, where Native American, Filipino and Dahomeyan residents were placed within a supposed evolutionary hierarchy. Commercial examples include the Cairo Street and the Indian tea-houses, and the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes, who toured under private entrepreneurs, receive detailed attention. Greenhalgh concludes that the displays did lasting damage by teaching mass audiences that non-European peoples were inferior, and that this harm was never repaired.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
5Reactions to the Exhibitionp. 110Asia

This chapter weighs the reception of the Exhibition in Britain and Japan. Hotta-Lister conclude that it did not change British attitudes toward Japan in diplomacy or trade, though publications about Japan rose sharply in 1910 and 1911. The British press praised the Japanese fine arts, gardens and wooden buildings, including the Chokushimon gate later re-erected at Kew, while some critics judged them by European standards as barbaric or childlike. Japanese opinion was largely hostile. Elite residents in London were embarrassed by the Japanese Village, the Ainu and Formosan displays and the sumo wrestlers, and their complaints reached the Diet, where Kurahara and Nishimura attacked Komura and the promoter Kiralfy. The authors read the colonial displays as part of a common imperial genre. The chapter ends with British manufacturers doubting that exhibitions paid, and with a 1910 proposal for a British-only exhibition in Japan.

In the contents of this work

6Aftermath of the Exhibitionp. 153Asia

Hotta-Lister asks whether the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London was, as Japanese opinion held, a failure, and tests its effects in economic, financial, diplomatic and racial terms. On trade, the author sets Japanese and British statistics beside the exhibitors' sales figures to show that Japanese exports to Britain fell overall in 1910 and 1911, while matting, raw silk, shell buttons and toys rose, and treats the Exhibition as one plausible contributor. Loans get the same caution: the government's conversion loans and the municipal and South Manchurian Railway issues were raised largely through the usual banks, so the Exhibition at most reinforced existing confidence. The chapter then follows the tariff dispute, the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in July 1911, drawing heavily on British press reactions. Its racial discussion centres on the Official Guide's claims of similarity between Japanese and British people and on Japanese complaints about Chinese and Ainu displays. It concludes that the Exhibition had only a limited effect, though it was not the failure that Japanese critics claimed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.
