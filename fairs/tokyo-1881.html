Tokyo · 1881

Second National Industrial Exposition

第二回内国勧業博覧会 (Dai Nikai Naikoku Kangyō Hakurankai)
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Second National Industrial Exposition for the Promotion of Industry (Naikoku kangyo hakurankai) was a domestic show held in Tokyo's Ueno Park in 1881 (Parkp. 4; Filipová 2015p. 334). The texts name no organiser for it. Hotta-Lister dates the second show at Ueno Park to 1882 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). It was one of five official domestic industrial exhibitions held before 1910, and a Korean mission member, Pak Chŏng-yang, saw it and first recorded the Korean term pangnamhoe in his report (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219; Parkp. 4).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Park calls it the Second National Exposition for the Promotion of Industry and Daykin the Second National Industrial Exposition for the Promotion of Industry; Hur calls the series Domestic Encouragement of Industry Exhibitions, rendering the Japanese name as Naikoku kangyō hakurankai (Parkp. 4; Filipová 2015p. 334; Hurp. 47). The series began with the first show at Ueno Park in 1877 (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hurp. 47). After that show drew 500,000 visitors in three months, the state announced that a domestic exposition would be held every four years (Filipová 2015p. 334). Daykin records that the 1881 show took place as planned, and that the government then postponed the next show by an additional four years (Filipová 2015p. 334). It was also argued that the third show should be postponed until 1890, to coincide with the 2,550th anniversary of the first Yamato Emperor's enthronement (Filipová 2015p. 334).

Dates and status

Daykin and Hur both give 1881 (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hurp. 47). Hur's footnote lists it as the second show in the series, held in Tokyo, with no dates beyond the year (Hurp. 47). Hotta-Lister places the first three shows at Ueno Park in 1877, 1882 and 1890, so the second show is dated 1882 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). Park places the 1881 show in Ueno Park (Parkp. 4). None of the passages gives opening or closing dates for the show, and no text discusses the disagreement over 1881 and 1882.

Display and exhibits

Komatsu states that design sketches prepared by the government's Exhibition Bureau were used for the first and second Domestic Exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 74). In 1881 the showroom for the second show was changed to display only domestic products, which Komatsu links to a change in educational methods (Lawn 2009p. 80). Daykin describes the rules of the early domestic expositions: foreign products could be exhibited only by exposition officials, while Japanese goods, including copies of foreign products, were shown by their makers (Filipová 2015p. 334).

Visitors and reception

None of the texts gives an attendance figure for the 1881 show. Hotta-Lister says that visitor numbers for the first three shows rose gradually, from 450,000 to 1,020,000 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

A Korean visitor

Pak Chŏng-yang (1841-1905) belonged to the Chosa sich'altan mission to Japan, whose main task was to report on how to adapt the Japanese modernisation programme to Korea (Parkp. 4). After visiting the 1881 show, Pak's reports are where the term pangnamhoe first appears in Korean official writing (Parkp. 4). Park argues that the Korean understanding of expositions came mainly through Japan but not only through it (Parkp. 4).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Domestic exhibitions as a series — The texts place the 1881 show in a run of national shows whose timing the government set and changed (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hurp. 47; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).
  • Dates and status — Daykin and Hur date the show to 1881 and Hotta-Lister to 1882; none of the texts resolves the difference (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hurp. 47; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).
  • Domestic goods on display — The second show's showroom was changed to show only domestic products, which Komatsu relates to educational method (Lawn 2009p. 80); the early rules separated foreign from domestic exhibits (Filipová 2015p. 334).
  • Expositions as a model for Korea — Pak's report introduced the term to Korean officials (Parkp. 4).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the government postpone the series by four years after 1881, and why was 1890 linked to the 2,550th anniversary of the first Yamato Emperor's enthronement? (Filipová 2015p. 334)
  2. Did the domestic-only showroom of 1881 change what visitors learned about foreign goods, as Komatsu's account of educational method suggests? (Lawn 2009p. 80)
  3. Which date should a student use for the Tokyo show, given that Daykin and Hur say 1881 and Hotta-Lister says 1882? (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219)
  4. What did Pak's report teach Korean officials about expositions, and why did the concept reach Korea mainly through Japan? (Parkp. 4)

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

Brief discussion 3

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
ch. 15, section 'Japan's Domestic Expositions', printed p. 334

Daykin gives the Second National Industrial Exposition only as background to his main subject, the 1903 Osaka exposition. He reports that the 1881 show took place as planned, and that the government then set a four-year cycle for domestic shows and postponed the following one. The passage gives no venue, dates or attendance for the 1881 show.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Komatsu's chapter; sections on the Educational Museum and its decline; printed pp. 74-80

Komatsu uses the 1881 show as one step in the argument about the Educational Museum. In that year the showroom of the second show was changed to display only domestic products, which Komatsu relates to a change in educational method. The passage also records that government design sketches were used for the first and second Domestic Exhibitions.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
article; section 'The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernity'; printed pp. 3-4

Park's article is about the 1915 Chosŏn exhibition, and it uses the 1881 Tokyo show mainly as the occasion on which a Korean official first recorded the term pangnamhoe. Park argues that Korean understanding of expositions came mainly through Japan but not only through it, and he lists three other channels: a mission to the West, participation in international expositions, and domestic exhibitions in Korea. The 1881 show is therefore a starting point in his argument rather than its subject.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
chapter on Japan's participation in exhibitions, near printed p. 219 (extraction does not make the page clear)

In a passing account of Japan's official domestic exhibitions, Hotta-Lister dates the second show at Ueno Park to 1882, not 1881 (l. 10633-10634).

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
ch. 2, footnote 99 (around printed p. 99)

Hur cites the 1881 show only in a footnote that lists the Domestic Encouragement of Industry series, which he treats as a sprout of a future world exhibition in Japan (l. 2529-2533; 2556-2558).

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
