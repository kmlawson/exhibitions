Busan · 1906

Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition

Irhan sangp'um pangnamhoe (日韓商品博覽會)
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition (Irhan sangp'um pangnamhoe 日韓商品博覽會; Jp. Nikkan shōhin hakurankai) was held in 1906 in Pusan, the largest city in South Kyŏngsang Province (Parkp. 6). Park calls it the first event officially titled pangnamhoe, or exposition, and places it among the smaller national and local industrial exhibitions of the 1900s that came before the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Parkp. 6). Henry calls it Korea's first exposition (Henry 2014p. 241). The texts do not say who organised it, what it displayed or how many people attended (Parkp. 6; Henry 2014p. 241).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Dating and status

Park gives the place as Pusan, the largest city in South Kyŏngsang Province (Parkp. 6). Henry's endnote gives the same place and year, refers to the hosting of the exposition, and cites Yi Kak-kyu's study for it (Henry 2014p. 241). Neither text says the event was planned but not held (Parkp. 6; Henry 2014p. 241).

Among the 1900s exhibitions

Park places the event after Korea's own experience of smaller national expositions and local industrial exhibitions in the 1900s (Parkp. 6). He writes: The first such event officially titled “exposition (pangnamhoe) was the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition" (Parkp. 6). By the early 1910s the idea had reached the local level and produced the kongjinhoe, a series of local industrial exhibitions (Parkp. 6). Park calls these "were important precursors of the 1915 exhibition" (Parkp. 6). The passage does not describe the Pusan event as one of the kongjinhoe (Parkp. 6).

The protectorate setting

Henry says that officials of the Great Han Empire took measures to host expositions on the peninsula, but "was not until the protectorate period that such events took place" (Henry 2014p. 93). His endnote places the Pusan event within those efforts (Henry 2014p. 241). Henry's chapter treats the 1907 Keijo Exhibition as a small-scale precursor of the 1915 and 1929 expositions (Henry 2014p. 93).

What the texts leave open

The texts say no more than this. Neither gives the organisers, the exhibits, the visitor numbers or a day in 1906 (Parkp. 6; Henry 2014p. 241).

Themes in the scholarship

  • The first titled exposition — Park calls the Pusan event the first event officially titled pangnamhoe, placed before the 1900s local exhibitions (Parkp. 6)
  • Kongjinhoe and the 1915 exhibition — Park presents the early-1910s local industrial exhibitions as precursors of the 1915 event, and does not tie the 1906 event to them directly (Parkp. 6)
  • Protectorate period — Henry dates the hosting of expositions on the peninsula to the protectorate period, and his endnote places the Pusan event within that history (Henry 2014p. 93p. 241)

Questions for study

  1. Park calls the Pusan event the first titled pangnamhoe, and Henry calls it Korea's first exposition. How far do the two claims about firsts depend on how each author defines an exposition? (Parkp. 6; Henry 2014p. 241)
  2. Henry says such events took place only from the protectorate period. How does a single 1906 event in Pusan fit that account? (Henry 2014p. 93p. 241)
  3. The texts give no organisers, exhibits or visitor figures for the Pusan event. Which other sources would a historian need to reconstruct it? (Henry 2014p. 241)
  4. Park places the event within Korea's own experience of exhibitions. Does that reading change if the Japanese-Korean name is taken as the main clue to its character? (Parkp. 6)

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
1Background of the Time (1905-10)p. 9Asia

Hotta-Lister sets out the diplomatic and economic setting for the exhibition in the years 1905 to 1910. Japan had become a great power after the Russo-Japanese War, and its legations in London, Washington and other capitals were upgraded to embassies between 1906 and 1908. The chapter then traces strains in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which British newspapers and officials increasingly questioned over Manchuria, the 'open door' pledge and Japanese immigration. It goes on to tariff revision, which Komura pursued against British objections, and to Japan's trade deficit and reliance on foreign loans. Its last sections cover the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the rivalry between Katsura and Saionji. It also surveys the Japanese and English-language newspapers on which much of the study relies, including The Times, the Japan Weekly Mail and the Chuo Shimbun. The chapter concludes that these pressures explain why the government was so committed to the exhibition.

In the contents of this work

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

Brief discussion 1

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Section 'The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernity', printed p. 5

Park's article on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition opens by tracing how the exposition idea (pangnamhoe) entered Korea, and in that account names the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition held at Pusan in 1906 as the first event officially titled pangnamhoe. Park places it among the smaller national and local industrial exhibitions of the 1900s, and treats the early-1910s local kongjinhoe as precursors of the 1915 exhibition. The passage gives only a few sentences to the Pusan event, with no organisers, exhibits or attendance. Park's wider argument is that expositions were a symbol of modernity for Korean reformers, and that Korean understanding came through Japan but also through direct contact with the West.

“were important precursors of the 1915 exhibition” p. 6
About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
Chapter 3, 'Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds', main text printed p. 93 (l. 4399-4401); endnote 4 on printed p. 241

Henry's study of colonial expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds mentions the 1906 Pusan exposition only in passing, in one sentence of the main text saying that officials of the Great Han Empire tried to host expositions on the peninsula but that such events came only in the protectorate period, and in an endnote that calls the Pusan event Korea's first exposition.

“was not until the protectorate period that such events took place” p. 93
About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
