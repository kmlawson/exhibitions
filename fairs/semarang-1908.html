The First Semarang Arts and Crafts Exhibition (Eerste Semarangsche Jaarmarkt-Tentoonstelling) was held in Semarang, one of the colonial cities of the Dutch East Indies, in 1908 (Cotép. 3p. 36). The texts give no organisers, site or visitor numbers for it. Its only mention is in Joost Coté's article on the 1914 Semarang exhibition, in a footnote and a bibliography entry (Cotép. 36p. 44). For the texts it matters mainly as Semarang's earlier arts and crafts show, set against the 1914 exhibition (Cotép. 4).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

What the texts record

The texts say little about this exhibition. Coté's footnote states that Semarang "had held its first" arts and crafts exhibition in 1908 (Cotép. 36). The footnote is attached to a sentence about 1914: that the Semarang exhibition of that year was the first in the Dutch colony with pretensions to being international (Cotép. 3). Coté does not say how the 1908 show related to that claim.

Coté's bibliography gives the report on the show as Verslag van de Eerste Semarangsche Jaarmarkt-Tentoonstelling 1908, published by Landsdrukkerij in Batavia in 1911 (Cotép. 44). The entry does not say which note relies on it, and the footnote gives no source.

Its place in the series

Coté places the 1914 exhibition against the native arts and crafts exhibitions held in Batavia, Surabaya and Semarang, which he does not date or describe (Cotép. 3). He calls the 1914 show the "first large exhibition in the Netherlands Indies", a phrase he takes from van Heel's 1916 book on that exhibition (Cotép. 3p. 43). He also says that regional displays gave the 1914 exhibition a standing that earlier local arts and crafts exhibitions had not had (Cotép. 4). The text does not say whether that remark refers to the 1908 show.

What the texts leave open

The texts give no organisers, site, exhibits, attendance or reception for the 1908 show. The visitor figures Coté gives, 306,795 native and 370,472 European visitors, refer to the 1914 exhibition (Cotép. 30). Nothing in the article describes a market-fair format or an annual cycle for the 1908 show. Apart from the footnote, the article's only reference to it is the bibliography entry for the report (Cotép. 44).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Earlier arts and crafts shows — Coté names the 1908 Semarang show only as the city's first such exhibition, and mentions earlier native arts and crafts exhibitions in several colonial cities without dates (Cotép. 3p. 36).
  • Comparison with 1914 — the article sets the 1914 show, with its regional displays, against earlier local arts and crafts exhibitions, without naming the 1908 show (Cotép. 4).
  • The published report — the only trace of a published record of the 1908 show is a bibliography entry giving its Dutch title and a 1911 publication in Batavia (Cotép. 44).

Questions for study

  1. Van Heel's description of the 1914 show as the "first large exhibition in the Netherlands Indies" implies a contrast with smaller shows (Cotép. 3). What evidence about the 1908 show would be needed to judge how it compared in scale?
  2. Coté places the 1908 show in a footnote to his claim about 1914 (Cotép. 3p. 36). Why might a study of 1914 mention an earlier show only in this way?
  3. The article gives its 1914 visitor figures in detail (Cotép. 30) but none for 1908. What sources would a historian consult to recover the 1908 figures, and what would they show?

Joost Coté's article on the 1914 Semarang International Colonial Exhibition mentions this 1908 Semarang show only in a footnote, which says Semarang had held its first arts and crafts exhibition that year, and in a bibliography entry for its published report; it does not discuss the show's meaning.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.
