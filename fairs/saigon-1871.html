The Exposition agricola et industrielle was an agricultural and industrial exhibition held at Saigon in 1871 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423). Our texts mention it only once, as an entry in an encyclopedia appendix of fairs left out of the book's main body (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). The texts say nothing about its organisers, site, exhibits, visitors or reception, and nothing about its significance.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The record in the texts

Only one text in the collection mentions this exhibition: the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions (2008), edited by Findling and Pelle. Its Appendix D, "Fairs Not Included", lists by year and city fairs and exhibitions held after 1851 that fell short of the qualifications for the main body (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). The appendix says the list shows how widespread such fairs were, especially in the nineteenth century (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). It gives the general reasons for exclusion: many entries were "very specialized in nature", and others lacked "a suitable international scope" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).

The Saigon entries

The entry for this exhibition gives the year, the city and the name, and nothing more (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423). The name is printed as Exposition agricola et industrielle, in the form the appendix uses for all its entries, which give names in the host country's language unless it is not a common Western tongue (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). The column headed City (Country) gives only the city for this entry (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 423).

The same list has a separate Saigon entry for 1866, the "Cochin China Exhibition" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423). The texts do not say whether the two events were connected.

What the texts leave open

The texts say no more than this. The appendix describes its entries as fairs and exhibitions held after 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422), which is the only statement that bears on whether this one took place, and it applies to the whole list rather than to this entry. No other text in the collection describes the fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Fairs outside the main canon — the encyclopedia places this exhibition outside its main body, and gives a general reason for such exclusions, without saying which applies here (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).
  • Proliferation of fairs — the appendix presents the list as evidence of how widespread fairs were, especially in the nineteenth century (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).
  • Naming — names are given in the host country's language, so the entry keeps the printed name rather than an English title (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 423).
  • Saigon listings — the list has two Saigon entries, for 1866 and 1871, with no link between them stated (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423).

Questions for study

  1. Why does an encyclopedia of fairs leave out an exhibition that its own appendix lists as held after 1851, and which of the stated exclusion reasons would apply to it? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422)
  2. What does an entry that gives only a year, city and name allow a student to conclude about a fair, and what other sources would be needed? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423)
  3. How might the 1866 "Cochin China Exhibition" and the 1871 exhibition at Saigon relate, and what evidence in the texts would show a connection? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423)

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.
