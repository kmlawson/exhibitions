Delhi Durbar Exhibition of Indian Art

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Delhi Durbar Exhibition of Indian Art was a show of Indian art held at Delhi in 1903 in connection with Lord Curzon's Durbar (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242; Mathur 2007p. 178). Curzon, the British viceroy of India, invited Indian princes, Anglo-Indians, soldiers, local artisans and English tourists to it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). George Watt directed it and compiled its official catalogue (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285). Hoffenberg's notes give its span as 1902-1903, while his text gives 1903 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 316p. 242). He treats it as an act of imperial ceremony and as part of a Raj-sponsored picture of Indian art (Hoffenberg 2001p. 257p. 83).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and occasion

Hoffenberg opens his chapter on ceremony at the exhibitions with this show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). He describes Curzon as an organiser who staged an extravaganza (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). A visiting British journalist described "a fortnight of splendour and pageantry" in Delhi (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). Crowds surrounded the Durbar halls and the artware exhibits and were dazzled by fireworks, polo matches and state processions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242).

Hoffenberg calls the show by several names: Durbar Art Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242), Delhi Indian Art Exhibition and Durbar (Hoffenberg 2001p. 83) and Delhi Durbar and Indian Art (1903) in his appendix (Hoffenberg 2001p. 280). The passages give no venue, attendance figure or opening date.

Empire and colony

Hoffenberg places the show in British India, under a British viceroy (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). He reads Curzon's Durbar as a recognition of the Crown's central role in imperial ritual after Victoria's death, and as an echo of the Crystal Palace opening of 1851 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 257). He argues that Curzon's combination of state ceremony, traditional arts and crafts, and a formal exhibition continued the triumph of such forms in public culture since 1851 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). One domestic critic of the viceroy's imperial gathering claimed that the English disliked theatrical display (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). The passages name no other colony or dominion taking part. Mathur's passage says nothing about the imperial purpose of the show.

Organisers and catalogue

Watt's official catalogue appears in Hoffenberg's account of Indian art publications. He places it among later Indian government volumes modelled on Birdwood's Indian Art handbooks, which he says created an alternative national vision for India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 83). He argues that this vision was "painted with the Raj’s palette" rather than in the colours of the I.N.C. (Hoffenberg 2001p. 83). The notes give the catalogue as Indian Art at Delhi, 1903, Being the Official Catalogue of the Delhi Exhibition, 1902-1903, edited by George Watt and Percy Brown and printed at Calcutta (Hoffenberg 2001p. 307).

Craftsman images

Mathur mentions the show only briefly. He says its official catalogue contained numerous images of Indian craftsmen (Mathur 2007p. 44). Percy Brown, then director of the Art School in Lahore, sketched these craftsmen in detail (Mathur 2007p. 45). Figures 9 and 10 reproduce catalogue sketches of wire inlayers from Uttar Pradesh and enamellers from Rajputana (Mathur 2007p. 44p. 45). Mathur argues that the craftsman had become a "dominant trope in the presentation of Indian art" by the turn of the century (Mathur 2007p. 44). Coomaraswamy later praised such images in his 1909 book The Indian Craftsman (Mathur 2007p. 45). Mathur's note 62 refers readers to Julie Codell's essay on the show's relation to Curzon's Delhi Durbar (Mathur 2007p. 178).

Visitors and reception

Hoffenberg's wider argument is that visitors to the exhibitions were participants, not only spectators. They helped make the picture of nation and empire by marching in ceremonies, touring as tourists and acting in pageants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 243). He says the chapter argues that visitors took part in building the Victorian public (Hoffenberg 2001p. 248). The chapter begins with the Delhi show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242).

Its place in the series

Hoffenberg traces the catalogue back to Birdwood's Indian Art handbooks, including the popular handbook for the Paris exposition of 1878 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 83). He says Curzon's Durbar echoed the official opening spectacle of the Crystal Palace in 1851 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 257). The passages do not connect the show with any later exhibition.

Indian princes, artisans and images

Curzon invited Indian princes and local artisans to the show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 242). The passages name no Indian commissioner or exhibitor. The catalogue reproduced Indian craftsmen at work (Mathur 2007p. 44). Hoffenberg's general account of Indian princes and artisans as regular participants at the shows under Victoria refers to exhibitions in general, not to Delhi (Hoffenberg 2001p. 244).

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
ch. 8 (pp. 242-244, 257-259, 267); ch. 3 (pp. 82-84); appendix A (p. 285); notes to chs. 3, 8 and the bibliography

Hoffenberg opens chapter 8 with Lord Curzon's 1903 Durbar Art Exhibition at Delhi as an example of imperial ceremony. He describes Curzon as a showman whose Durbar combined state ceremony, traditional crafts and an exhibition, and he treats the Crown as the centre of that ritual. In chapter 3 he places George Watt's official catalogue among the Indian government volumes modelled on Birdwood's handbooks, which he argues offered an alternative national vision for India painted in the Raj's colours. The larger argument of chapter 8 is that visitors to the exhibitions were participants who helped create an imperial and Victorian public, not passive spectators. The Delhi show is used as the opening case for that argument rather than studied in depth.

“a fortnight of splendour and pageantry” — visiting British journalist quoted by the author p. 242
About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Brief discussion 1

Mathur, Saloni. India by Design: Colonial History and Cultural Display. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2007.
ch. 1 (pp. 44-45); note 62 (notes to pp. 39-45); bibliography and figure list

Mathur mentions the 1903 exhibition of Indian art at Delhi only briefly, in chapter 1 on the Indian craftsman. He notes that its official catalogue contained numerous images of Indian craftsmen, sketched in detail by Percy Brown and reproduced as figures in his book. He uses these images to argue that by the turn of the century the craftsman had become a dominant image in the presentation of Indian art, linked with the art criticism of Coomaraswamy and Havell. His note refers readers to Julie Codell's essay on the exhibition's relation to Curzon's Delhi Durbar. The passages say nothing about the exhibition's organisers, dates, site or visitors.

“The official catalogue for the 1903 exhibition of Indian art in Delhi, for instance, contained numerous images” p. 44
About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Mathur's India by Design (2007) asks how an image of a timeless, craft-based 'traditional India' was made and circulated in Britain from the 1880s. It is organised in three moments: imperial, nationalist and postcolonial. Each chapter takes one site of production: the Liberty department store and its 1885 living village (chapter 1); the living display at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, with jail inmates and the petitioner Tulsi Ram (chapter 2); Rudolf Swoboda's portraits for Queen Victoria (chapter 3); colonial postcards (chapter 4); and a dispute over two Buddhist relics at the Victoria and Albert Museum (chapter 5). The argument is that the categories of caste, tradition, village and craftsman still organise how India is staged.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a slight presence in this book, which is about South Asia. The one substantial regional thread is Burma in chapter 5, 'A Parable of Postcolonial Return'. The V&A's Buddhist displays had rooms on Burma, and in the years before the 1952 reburial the Sanchi relics were shown across Burma from Mandalay to Rangoon, as well as in Calcutta and Nepal. The Burmese government and the Burmese premier, who attended the reburial, shaped that tour. Mathur reads these displays as the new state's use of the relics as national symbols. Elsewhere the region is passing. A Malay dressmaker is among Swoboda's portraits for the Queen (chapter 3), and Malays are listed among the peoples housed at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (chapter 2). Natives from Hong Kong attended the Windsor luncheon (chapter 2), and Tulsi Ram worked in Rangoon before reaching London (chapter 2). Liberty's Paris bazaar of 1889 stocked Chinese lanterns (chapter 1), and the introduction cites the Great Exhibition's China exhibit only as a foil for India's section. Japan, Korea, the Philippines, Siam, Indonesia and Indochina are not discussed.
