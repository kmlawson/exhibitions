Brussels World's Fair (Expo 58)

Exposition Universelle et Internationale de Bruxelles 1958
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Brussels World's Fair of 1958, known as Expo 58, ran from 17 April to 19 October 1958 in Heysel Park, Brussels, and drew nearly 42 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). It was the first major world's fair after the Second World War (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 311). Belgium meant it to promote its economic growth and its rule in Africa, but the Cold War gave it wider meaning: the United States and the Soviet Union used their pavilions as a propaganda contest (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 319). Historians study it as a case of how a fair staged the atomic age and superpower rivalry.

Where the dates and figures come from, and where the texts disagree

Attendance: table 35 and table 39 give 41,454,000 (used); texts give 41,454,412 (Badger 1979, 26, 43, 57), 41.5 million (Benedict 1983, 42, 50), nearly 42 million (Findling & Pelle 2008 text) and forty-two million (Samuel 2007). Theme wording differs across sources (balance of the world, a new humanism, evaluation of the world), so no theme is given. Findling & Pelle 2008 text says it was first set for 1955 and delayed by the Korean War. Cotter 2009 mentions the fair only in passing.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Belgium first proposed the fair in 1947 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). Its 1955 opening was postponed to 1958 after the Korean War disrupted finance and politics (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 311). The sources word the official theme differently. Kargon gives "The Balance of the World for a More Humane World" (Kargon et al. 2015p. 140), while Samuel's version is an evaluation of the world rather than a balance (Samuel 2007). Kargon treats the fair as the first postwar universal and international exposition, continuing the ideological struggle of the 1930s between two superpowers (Kargon et al. 2015p. 140). Rydell gives its avowed theme as a new humanism devoted to the peaceful uses of nuclear energy (Rydell 1993p. 193).

Organisation and finance

Baron Georges Moens de Fernig was commissioner-general, and an organisation supervised the building work, which employed up to 15,000 construction workers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 311). Kargon describes Moens de Fernig as a former Belgian trade minister who ran the Marshall Plan in Belgium, and says the Belgians wanted Brussels promoted as the new capital of Europe (Kargon et al. 2015p. 140). The state paid, with a grant from the colonial lottery, and corporations also contributed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). Spending outran income, but the fair brought jobs, thirty miles of new road and five miles of tunnels (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 322). The United States set up its own commissioner-general's office in October 1956 (Rydell 1993p. 194). Low gives the American budget as $13 million, against a Soviet budget of some $50 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 48). Kargon gives an original American appropriation of $15 million, cut by $4.5 million in 1957 and restored to about $12 million (Kargon et al. 2015p. 145).

Site and architecture

The grounds covered nearly 500 acres, linked by a concrete passenger viaduct and a cable car (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 311). The architect-in-chief, Marcel van Goethem, favoured modernist halls with no fixed formula (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 319). The best-known buildings were Le Corbusier's Philips Pavilion, which Allwood calls a herald of later exhibition environments (Allwood et al. 2001p. 129), Egon Eiermann's West German pavilion and Edward Stone's American pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 319). The Atomium, designed by André Waterkeyn, was a 334-foot structure of nine spheres, each 59 feet across (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321). Other texts give 355 feet (Samuel 2007) and 360 feet (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). Czechoslovakia won the Grand Prix for its pavilion, and critics praised the Yugoslav pavilion by Vjenceslav Richter (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 225). Allwood records complaints that the layout was confused: the Canadian pavilion was almost hidden by the blank wall of the Russian cinema (Allwood et al. 2001p. 127).

Atoms and science

Rydell argues that the Atomium and the nuclear exhibits reinforced claims that nuclear power was safe (Findling & Pelle 2008). Low notes that the fair was dubbed the world’s first Atomic Fair (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 46). Organisers first planned to power the fair from a nuclear generator but dropped the plan over health concerns (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321). Low says they set up a reactor at Mol instead, which the Atomium could show only as a photograph (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 11). A Hall of International Science, backed by scientific bodies in fifteen nations, presented science as a service to humanity (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321). Benedict finds that the fair gave more attention to science than to social progress (Benedict 1983p. 36). The midway attractions, including an Interplanetary Rocket to Mars, turned fear of atomic technology into leisure and consumption (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 317).

Cold War rivalry

After the Sputnik launches, the Soviet pavilion put models of the satellites at its centre (Kargon et al. 2015p. 142). Sputnik was the world's first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 46). The American pavilion chose the soft sell, an indirect appeal built on consumer lifestyle, while the Soviet pavilion was more forceful (Kargon et al. 2015). Rydell calls the American pavilion a "cultural cyclotron" that bombarded visitors with images (Rydell 1993p. 211). Southern legislators attacked the planned Unfinished Business exhibit on desegregation (Rydell 1993p. 200), and Kargon says it was removed and replaced by a public health exhibit (Kargon et al. 2015p. 146). The Czechoslovak and Yugoslav pavilions presented socialist modernity as an alternative to the West (Hollengreen et al. 2014).

Colonies and peoples on show

The Belgian colonial display covered 19 acres and seven pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321). Rydell argues that the Belgian government relied on the fair "to reaffirm domestic support for its crumbling imperial policies in the Congo" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 311). The show served imperial aims but masked colonial realities, which the Congo's independence struggle exposed two years later (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321). Belgium brought nearly 600 Congolese to staff the fair, and many were exhibited in virtual human zoos, staged scenes of people in supposedly native conditions (Peterson 2020p. 27; Peterson 2020p. 27). Peterson calls this perhaps the last spectacular export of colonised peoples to an exposition (Peterson 2020p. 27). A note in the Massidda chapter calls Brussels 1958 the last universal exhibition to host a human zoo, and attributes the point to Sánchez Gómez (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 250).

Visitors and reception

The attendance figures vary. Rydell's encyclopaedia entry gives nearly 42 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318), and Mandell gives 41,454,412 (Mandell 1967). Fewer than 6,000 Czechs and Slovaks saw the Czechoslovak pavilion in person because of travel restrictions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 231). Many ordinary Yugoslavs found their pavilion empty and too modern, and less than ten per cent of visitors went to it (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 236; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 236). A Belgian survey ranked the Czechoslovak pavilion first, the American second, the French third and the Soviet fourth (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 42).

Aftermath and legacy

Many prefabricated buildings were sold and reused in Belgium and abroad, and the Atomium remained (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 322). Roche calls the Atomium a permanent physical heritage of the event (Roche 2000p. 132). The Yugoslav pavilion was sold and reassembled as a college in Wevelgem (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 236). The Bel-Gem waffle, which many associate with Brussels, originated at the New York fair of 1964, not at Brussels (Samuel 2007). Depelchin notes that Expo 58 is the only universal exhibition that still lives on in Belgian popular culture, which the chapter ascribes to post-war optimism and living memory (Filipová 2015p. 184).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell's encyclopaedia entry argues that the nuclear, colonial and corporate displays served political and commercial interests, and that the fair's optimism stimulated cold-war competition as much as it muted conflict (Findling & Pelle 2008). In a chapter on the American pavilion, Rydell argues that the Society in Ferment theme masked government concern with national security (Rydell 1993p. 196). Swift argues that both superpowers used the fair for propaganda, the Soviets through facts and things and the Americans through the soft sell (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 45). Yet visitor surveys rated the Soviet pavilion higher (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 43). Kargon sees the soft and hard sell as opposed styles in one Cold War contest (Kargon et al. 2015), and foreign press praised the American approach while American commentators mocked it (Kargon et al. 2015p. 146). Samuel, by contrast, calls Brussels the first fair to suggest a dystopia that science could bring about (Samuel 2007). Zarecor and Kulić argue that the socialist pavilions transcended geopolitics while remaining within Cold War narratives (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Leslie and Mercelis argue that Expo 58 served Belgium's ambition to make Brussels the capital of a united Europe (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 19). Allwood, Allan and Reid treat the fair as a turning point in exhibition design (Allwood et al. 2001). The authors agree that the fair was a Cold War stage. They differ over whether the Atomium reassured or warned, and over whether the American approach failed: Kargon reports praise abroad and criticism at home (Kargon et al. 2015p. 146; Kargon et al. 2015p. 147), Swift's surveys favour the Soviets (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 43), and Samuel says the showing embarrassed the government into planning another fair (Samuel 2007).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Our texts give three Asian presences at Expo 58: a Japanese pavilion, a Philippine dance company and a Thai pavilion. The texts do not say who commissioned or paid for any of them. They name no Chinese, Indian, Indonesian or other South or Southeast Asian commissioner, display or visitor for this fair.

Japan

Japan's pavilion aimed to show a country that had put the war behind it and rebuilt its economy through its own people. Its position behind the American pavilion reminded the world of the American role in that reconstruction (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49). Its theme was The Japanese Hand and the Machine, and it told its story in three parts: history, industry and everyday life (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49). The history section used haniwa funerary sculptures and a reproduction of a Buddha head from Kōfukuji temple in Nara, with gagaku (imperial court music) and Nō (classical theatre) music in the background. It ended with a large image of Hiroshima after the atomic bomb, which visitors walked under (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49). The industry section showed precision scientific instruments (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 50). Low argues that the fair was a setting for Japanese attitudes to nuclear power, and that the pavilion acknowledged the impact of the atom on Japanese history (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 46; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 46). Japanese newspapers covered the fair closely: the Yomiuri Shimbun carried a French journalist's piece on the fair before it opened (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 48), and a month in it ran an article comparing the American and Soviet pavilions (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 48). Among the former Axis powers, Japan kept a low profile, with a pavilion modest in size and low in silhouette (Kargon et al. 2015p. 141). The texts also note that Japan, with Germany and Italy, used the fair to refurbish its national image, and that its timber and glass pavilion signalled a new openness (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 313; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 313).

The Philippines

The Philippine folk dance company Bayanihan was warmly received by a European audience at Brussels in 1958 (Peterson 2020p. 22). Peterson calls this a key moment of self-representation through performance. The company then toured the world for the next decade and became emblematic of the Philippines (Peterson 2020p. 22). Peterson does not say who sent the company.

Thailand

The Thai pavilion was a gilded building in the style of a temple, set against the plainer glass and timber structures around it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 127; Allwood et al. 2001p. 127).

How the scholars read it

Peterson reads the Bayanihan performance as an early example of Asian self-representation through performance, and Low reads the Japanese pavilion as part of the effort to present the peaceful atom to Japanese audiences (Peterson 2020p. 22; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 46). The texts say nothing about Asian visitors' reactions to the Japanese or Thai pavilions.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Atomic optimism — the Atomium and the nuclear displays are read as reassurance that nuclear power was safe, while the midway turned fear of the atom into entertainment (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 317).
  • Cold War rivalry — the American and Soviet pavilions are treated as a propaganda contest, shaped by Sputnik and by budget fights in Congress; visitor surveys cut across the official aims (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 48; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 43).
  • Colonial display — the Belgian colonial show served imperial aims and masked colonial realities, and Congolese staff were themselves put on show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321; Peterson 2020p. 27).
  • Socialist pavilions — the Czechoslovak and Yugoslav pavilions presented socialist modernity; one won the Grand Prix, the other drew few visitors (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 225; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 236).
  • Science as service — science was presented as a service to humanity, and Belgian industries showed reactor models for the future (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 322).
  • Afterlife — the Atomium and reused buildings kept the fair in Brussels long after it closed, and Belgians still remember it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 322; Filipová 2015p. 184).

Questions for study

  1. How could a fair promise nuclear power as a peaceful servant while one of its pavilions showed the Hiroshima bomb's destruction? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 49)
  2. If visitors preferred the Soviet pavilion in surveys, what does that say about whether Cold War propaganda worked? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 43)
  3. Can the colonial show be judged a success when it was built to support Belgian rule in the Congo? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321)
  4. Why did critics praise the Yugoslav pavilion while ordinary visitors found it empty and too modern? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 236)
  5. Should the Atomium be read as reassurance about nuclear power or as a warning about it? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 321; Samuel 2007)
  6. How did the fair's buildings outlast its ideas, and what does that tell us about the way Brussels remembers the fair? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 322; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 19)

Rydell's chapter on the American Pavilion is the fullest account of the 1958 Brussels fair in this set, and it treats the fair as the first world's fair of the postwar period. Substantial discussion 3

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
Brussels 1958 discussion at printed pp. 122-129 (index also lists pp. 75, 137); BIE table p. 208

Allwood, Allan and Reid's general history gives the 1958 Brussels fair several pages, as the first international exhibition after the war and one concerned mainly with science. The authors describe the Atomium, the Water Ladder, the main exhibition halls and the national pavilions, and they judge the layout and the British and US displays harshly, calling the British section confused and commercial and the US pavilion light on content. They single out the Philips Pavilion as a herald of the multi-media environments of later fairs, and they note that Canadian senator Marc Drouin, present at the Brussels Canada Day, conceived the idea of Expo 67 there. The fair is thus treated as a turning point in exhibition design and as the origin of later Canadian plans.

Fair America presents the 1958 Brussels fair as the first major fair after the Second World War, one that drew great American interest because of a cold war-style contest with the Soviet Union. The authors say the Atomium denied that nuclear energy posed a threat, while the US pavilion's popular-culture approach left the American side looking weaker, and that the Eisenhower administration resolved never to repeat that showing, with Seattle's Century 21 as the chance to improve on it. In the conclusion they cite Brussels, Seattle and New York as the high point of the utopian promise that technology and consumerism could cure modern anxieties.

Brief discussion 5

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
Focal structures (PDF p. 31); Table 1 (PDF p. 41); Table 2 (PDF p. 46); Table 3 (PDF p. 49); Belgian exhibit classes (printed p. 35, PDF p. 53)

Benedict's anthropology of world's fairs discusses the 1958 Brussels fair briefly, mainly as an example of the 'focal structure' that stood for each fair. He notes that the Atomium was Brussels' focal structure, that the Belgian exhibits were grouped into eight broad classes, and that this classification was less comprehensive than earlier fairs' attempts to present a whole history of mankind. He argues that Brussels gave more attention to the achievements of science than to social progress, and that its social-services exhibit contrasted 1958 with pre-1900 conditions rather than showing development. The tables give the fair a 500-acre site and 41.5 million visitors.

Depelchin's chapter on Ghent 1913 notes in one paragraph that the 1958 Brussels fair is the only universal exhibition that still lives on in Belgian popular culture, while the pre-war and inter-war Belgian fairs have been forgotten. He attributes Expo '58's enduring popularity to post-war optimism and to the fact that it is within living memory, and says that the 2008 fiftieth anniversary produced accessible books and documentaries. He contrasts this with the mainly academic or local interest in earlier Belgian fairs, and cites two Expo 58 publications in a note. The fair is a comparison point here rather than the chapter's subject.

Wikipedia

Expo 58, also known as the 1958 Brussels World's Fair (French: Exposition universelle et internationale de Bruxelles; Dutch: Wereldtentoonstelling van Brussel), was a world's fair held on the Heysel/Heizel Plateau in Brussels, Belgium, from 17 April to 19 October 1958. It was the first major world's fair registered under the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) after World War II and the fifth in Brussels overall. With nearly 42 million visitors, Expo 58 was an important showcase for the technological and cultural aspirations of the post-war era. The exhibition left a lasting impression on Belgium; it was also associated with major redevelopment works in Brussels, whose boulevards were transformed into urban motorways. The Atomium, constructed as the fair's centrepiece, has since become one of the city's most recognisable landmarks.
