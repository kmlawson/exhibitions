Melbourne · 1875

Melbourne Intercolonial Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Melbourne Intercolonial Exhibition opened in Melbourne, Victoria, on 2 September 1875, and the texts describe it as preparation for the Philadelphia Exhibition of 1876 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 387). It was one of a series of Inter-Colonial Exhibitions held in a new, larger building in 1866, 1872 and 1875 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 52). Commissioners and an Executive Committee ran it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2; Hoffenberg 2001p. 354). No text studies it at length. Hoffenberg uses it in several chapters to illustrate colonial wine, machinery and decoration (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109; Hoffenberg 2001p. 176).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and organisers

Allwood places the 1875 show in a Melbourne series. An 1855 show on the site of the present Royal Mint drew 40,000 visitors, and an 1861 show that followed led to Inter-Colonial Exhibitions in a new, larger building in 1866, 1872 and 1875 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 52). Hoffenberg lists Sir Redmond Barry as a commissioner of the Local Executive Committee for Melbourne exhibitions, including the 1875 Intercolonial (Hoffenberg 2001p. 281). He also lists George Collins Levey as a commissioner there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 284; Geppert 2010p. 272). The Executive Committee issued the invitation to exhibitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2), and the official catalogue was issued by the commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001p. 354).

Empire and colony

The invitation is the only statement of the show's regional scope in the passages. The Executive Committee invited exhibitors and visitors from "all the British, French, and Dutch possessions in Australasia and the neighbouring islands" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2). The passages name no French or Dutch exhibitors. They show New South Wales taking part through the Upper Hunter River Agricultural and Horticultural Association, whose objects Charles Moore selected for the show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 35; Hoffenberg 2001p. 299). The passages name no Indigenous or colonised people among the displays, and none of the texts reports the organisers stating an imperial purpose for this show. The trade aim they describe is selling: nearly three hundred bottles of wine were provided for experts to taste and for the public to buy (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109). Hoffenberg says the stoves, ploughs, pumps, a screw steamer and mining equipment were shown to illustrate colonial manufactures (Hoffenberg 2001p. 176).

Exhibits and attractions

Hoffenberg describes a wine bar and sales court at the 1875 show that contrasted with early exhibitions, where visitors had to accept the experts' judgement on quality (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109p. 324). In the machinery court, exhibitors invited visitors to observe and test working machines, including those for tin, gold and wheat (Hoffenberg 2001p. 176). The main hall was hung with flags and banners, which gave the show "a festive air of pageantry" and tied it to older traditions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 201). Hoffenberg also reads the flags and medieval banners in the main halls of the 1870 Sydney and 1875 Melbourne shows as reflecting the efforts of imperial historians to anchor the New Empire in older myths (Hoffenberg 2001p. 245).

Visitors, reception and the colonial silence

Over 74,000 ticket-holders visited in the first three weeks, nearly doubling the attendance of the 1872-73 Victorian Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2). The figure rests on the Age of 20 September 1875 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 290). None of the passages records a dispute about the 1875 show. Hoffenberg's chapter on machinery argues that the colonies' displays left out war. In a note he quotes the 1875 catalogue: "merely in its theoretical form" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 353p. 354). He places this beside the silence about settler and Aboriginal confrontations (Hoffenberg 2001p. 200). He also cites the catalogue's Department 18, for firearms and ordnance, as an example of the weapon collections he describes at Australian shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 228; Hoffenberg 2001p. 362).

Its place in the series

Melbourne held local and intercolonial exhibitions in 1854, 1861, 1866, 1872 and 1875. Those shows came before the international events of Paris 1855, London 1862, Paris 1867, Vienna 1873 and Philadelphia 1876 (Filipová 2015p. 251). The encyclopedia gives the same sequence as preparation for overseas participation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67), and the 1990 dictionary describes the shows as building acceptance of an international fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Levey, who appears as a commissioner at the 1875 show, is also named as commissioner at Vienna 1873, Philadelphia 1876, Paris 1878 and Sydney 1879 (Geppert 2010p. 272). He was secretary of Melbourne's 1880-81 International Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67). Allwood notes that in 1877 a plan was submitted to Parliament for a permanent building to open with an international exhibition in 1879 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 52). Two reference works list the 1875 show only among fairs they excluded from their main entries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 398; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424). Their appendices say those fairs fell short of the qualifications for the main body (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).

Asia

The only Asian participation in these passages is Japanese. Hotta-Lister's appendix on Japan's exhibition participation gives the 1875 Melbourne show as an entry with an expenditure of 2,229 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The text gives no detail of what Japan sent or who attended.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial invitations — the 1875 invitation named the British, French and Dutch possessions in Australasia, but the passages name only New South Wales and Victorian participants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2; Hoffenberg 2001p. 35).
  • Wine and trade — wine was provided for tasting and for purchase in a sales court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109).
  • Machinery and progress — working machines were shown as evidence of colonial industry (Hoffenberg 2001p. 176).
  • Pageantry and tradition — flags, banners and medieval ornament decorated the main halls (Hoffenberg 2001p. 201; Hoffenberg 2001p. 245).
  • Armaments and silence — the 1875 catalogue treated colonial war as theoretical, and the author sets this against the silence about frontier violence (Hoffenberg 2001p. 200; Hoffenberg 2001p. 353).
  • Series and reference works — the show is a preparatory step in a Melbourne series and is listed in two reference works only as an excluded fair (Filipová 2015p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 398).

Questions for study

  1. The invitation named French and Dutch possessions. What evidence would show whether any of those colonies sent exhibits to Melbourne in 1875?
  2. Hoffenberg relies on an official catalogue to show that colonial war was only theoretical. How far can such a catalogue be trusted as evidence of a colony's view of its own history?
  3. Why do the two encyclopedic works leave the 1875 show out of their main entries, when Allwood places it in a series of Inter-Colonial Exhibitions?
  4. Wine and machinery are both used to show colonial progress. How do the sources differ in what they say the exhibition was for?

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
ch. 5 'Paris, Australia and All the World', opening Melbourne passage (l. 2745-2761); index entry (l. 10746-10747)

Allwood, Allan and Reid place the 1875 Melbourne show in a Melbourne series: an 1855 show on the site of the present Royal Mint, an 1861 show, and then Inter-Colonial Exhibitions in a new, larger building in 1866, 1872 and 1875. The passage gives no visitor figure or other detail for 1875. Its main concern is the permanent building planned after 1877, so the 1875 show serves as background rather than as an argument in its own right.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.
