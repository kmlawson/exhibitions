St. Louis · 1904

Louisiana Purchase Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition opened in Forest Park, St. Louis, on 30 April 1904 and closed on 1 December after almost twenty million visits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 183; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). It celebrated the centenary of the 1803 Louisiana Purchase, which doubled the area of the United States (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 171). Its organisers meant it to outdo Chicago's 1893 fair and to restore St. Louis's reputation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172). Historians read it as a showcase of imperial rule, above all the Philippine Reservation, where colonised peoples were displayed in a racial hierarchy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 174; Rydell 1984).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

St. Louis planned the fair as a centenary of the 1803 purchase, which Boger calls perhaps the greatest feat of Thomas Jefferson's presidency (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 171). Civic leaders had discussed a commemoration since 1889, and in 1890 David R. Francis led a delegation that failed to win the World's Columbian Exposition, which went to Chicago (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178). Badger notes that the defeat stung Francis, who later became the chief organiser of the 1904 fair (Badger 1979p. 147). The promoters wanted to outdo Chicago and to end the city's image as a corrupt, strike-hit place. Mayor Rolla Wells backed the fair as part of the New St. Louis reform programme (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178). Boger calls the fair a thoroughly imperialist spectacle that celebrated Manifest Destiny, Western superiority and a man-over-nature theme (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 171; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 171).

The opening was delayed from 1903 so that foreign governments and states could take part. Francis and the fair's agents toured European capitals, and other agents went to Asia, Latin America and North Africa with invitations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178). Francis himself described the aim as universal peace through bringing diverse people together (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 179).

Organisation, money and site

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition Company was organised in May 1901, with Francis as president and ninety-three directors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178). Pledges of nearly $5 million came from a 1899 rally. A city bond issue and a federal loan of the same size followed, and the federal government later doubled its loan as costs rose (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178). Badger's appendix lists a federal appropriation of $11,427,500 (Badger 1979p. 132). Greenhalgh calls the company's administration perhaps the most emulated model among exhibition companies. The same author says the state committed the fair to a larger site than any earlier international exhibition, which made it a high-risk venture from the start (Greenhalgh 1988p. 39; Greenhalgh 1988p. 39).

The site was the western half of Forest Park, which the city approved in May 1901. It grew by purchase and lease to about 1,270 acres (Maloney 2012p. 101). Other texts give 1,272 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172), and Badger's appendix gives 1,240 (Badger 1979p. 131). The main palaces fanned out from Festival Hall, and the ivory-toned buildings, limited to 65 feet, gave the fair its nickname, Ivory City (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Maloney describes a classical and academic scheme (Maloney 2012p. 101). Walking the whole grounds meant about 35 miles (Allwood et al. 2001p. 84).

The texts disagree about the balance sheet. Allwood's statistics table records a loss of $19.974 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207), and Roche's table also lists a loss (Roche 2000p. 43). Greenhalgh calls the loss small because of the directors' shrewdness, yet also names the fair among the great financial disasters, probably with the largest deficit in the American tradition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 39; Greenhalgh 1988p. 48). Boger says the fair was considered a great success in financial terms too (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 176). Condon's entry says profits built the Jefferson Memorial and paid the company's debts (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). Samuel says Francis ran short of money and had to ask Congress for a further $4.5 million (Samuel 2007).

Exhibits and attractions

The Pike, the amusement street, offered about 540 amusements and concessions on opening day (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 183). Rydell describes the Pike as a world in living colour, whose concessions taught ethnological lessons in economics and sociology (Rydell 1984). Benedict cites panoramas of the Creation and the Hereafter, premature infants in incubators, and a horse said to do arithmetic (Benedict 1983p. 56; Benedict 1983p. 56; Benedict 1983p. 57).

Science and technology drew as much attention. Allwood says St. Louis surpassed Chicago in automobiles, wireless and aeronautics, and that Baldwin's airship made the first successful American dirigible flight from the grounds on 25 October 1904 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 84; Allwood et al. 2001p. 85). Badger credits the fair with the first public showing of the radio tube (Badger 1979p. 8). The third modern Olympiad was held with the fair, and track and field events ran from 29 August to 3 September (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 184). Roche says Roosevelt settled a dispute over the Games in St. Louis's favour, and that De Coubertin effectively boycotted them, so they were mainly a North American affair (Roche 2000p. 89; Roche 2000p. 89). Fair America judges the Games no better than Paris 1900 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Nations and colonies on show

The Philippines were the centre of the imperial display. The exhibit covered 47 acres at the south-western end of the grounds, set apart on an artificial lake (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 174; Greenhalgh 1988p. 77). Greenhalgh reports $125,000 from the company and a further $250,000 from sponsorship, and says a Philippine Commission act created the exposition board (Greenhalgh 1988p. 77; Greenhalgh 1988p. 77). The grounds reproduced a miniature city with streets, parks and utilities, including the Ancient Walls of Manila and the Bridge of Spain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 77). Greenhalgh argues that America could simulate imperial control over what it displayed, and says the exhibit outdid anything Britain showed on India or France on Algeria (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76; Greenhalgh 1988p. 77).

Other nations were less central. Spain stayed away, having ceded Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines in 1898 (Boone 2019p. 160). Hoffenberg records confused British official support: the chairman of the British Art Committee complained of contradictory directives, while colonial governments funded Indian and Australian displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 88; Hoffenberg 2001p. 88). Austria-Hungary exhibited in two pavilions run by two committees (Filipová 2015p. 125). Hungary had no national pavilion, and its decorative arts were scattered through the Manufactures Building (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 158). Germany used the fair to publicise its social insurance laws (Kaiserp. 50).

Peoples on display

The Anthropology Department was the most extensive of any world's fair. W J McGee ran it after leaving the Bureau of American Ethnology amid charges of financial irregularity (Rydell 1984). McGee's 1899 theory placed peoples in grades from savagery to enlightenment, and the department displayed Pygmies, Patagonian giants, Ainu, Kwakiutl and Native American groups as living types (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Two Columbia University psychologists ran measuring laboratories, and Bruner's report called the Pygmies dense and stupid (Rydell 1984). Hrdlička proposed obtaining the bodies of Filipinos expected to die at the fair (Rydell 1984). The Igorots' dog-eating drew crowds and was a lasting notion (Peterson 2020p. 182; Benedict 1983p. 44). Taft and Roosevelt pressed for clothing for the Igorot and Negrito villagers, and by mid-July the administration dropped plans for silk trousers (Rydell 1984). Santesso places Ota Benga among displays of people that included Philippine tribesmen and Inuit (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 43). Estimates of the Filipino population range from over a thousand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 175) to about 1,200 (Rydell 1993p. 19).

Visitors, reception and controversy

Official attendance was 19,694,855 over seven months (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). Boger gives roughly 20 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 176). Badger's appendix gives 12,804,000 paid admissions (Badger 1979p. 131). Rydell's oral histories suggest many fairgoers were transfixed by the Filipinos (Rydell 1993p. 19). Health stations treated some 14,000 visitors who needed medical attention (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The fair gave women no separate exhibit building. Women acted as hostesses in a social space while their exhibits competed with men's (Ganz 2008). Weimann records that the twenty-three Lady Managers met in a building that later became a physics hall at Washington University (Weimann 1981p. 598). Greenhalgh says anti-suffrage groups used the women's sections to promote a womanhood without the vote (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). Black leaders cancelled a planned day for Black visitors after a Black National Guard regiment was denied barracks (Samuel 2007). Marines and guards attacked Filipino Scouts who escorted white schoolteachers on the grounds (Rydell 1984).

Aftermath and legacy

Profits built the Jefferson Memorial, which cost nearly $500,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). The Jefferson Memorial Building stood at the main entrance, and the directors gave the city a statue of St Louis (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). The St. Louis Art Museum is another legacy, and the city bought the Bird Cage aviary for $3,500 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 176). The St Louis Zoo traces its origins to the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41). Forest Park was promised a return to its prefair state within two years, but restoration took nine years and Kessler redesigned it for recreation (Maloney 2012p. 113; Maloney 2012p. 114). The 1944 film Meet Me in St. Louis is also a legacy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 176).

Food stories are disputed. One text calls the belief that iced tea, hot dogs and the ice cream cone began at the fair a myth with no basis in fact (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). Others say the cone was introduced there (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87; Rydell 1984; Samuel 2007). Boone notes that the treatment of foreign exhibitors at St Louis was one reason many nations stayed away from San Francisco in 1915 (Boone 2019p. 162).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell gives the fullest reading: the fair crowned civilisation as the outcome of conquest, anthropology supplied the ranking of peoples, and the Philippine exhibit made Americans see colonised people as commodities that confirmed white superiority (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1993p. 20). Rydell argues that world's fairs were elite instruments for legitimising imperial and industrial power to a mass public (Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle stress in their later co-written book that the psychological tests left visitors believing in Anglo-Saxon superiority (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell also argues that the fairs' dream worlds of imperial abundance blunted class conflict without removing it (Rydell 1993p. 31).

Boger reads the same displays as a bid by the business and civic elite, led by Francis, to change the city's image, with the Philippine Reservation familiarising Americans with their new acquisitions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 174). Condon agrees the fair was imperialist but gives it a positive civic and financial verdict (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 179; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185). Greenhalgh reads the displays through simulated empire, and Roche adopts Greenhalgh's typology of human display (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76; Roche 2000p. 83). Benedict calls the anthropological display Social Darwinism triumphant (Benedict 1983p. 50). Qureshi takes St Louis as the benchmark of the native-village convention, which later fairs never equalled (Qureshi 2011p. 253). Della Coletta calls it the probable climax of the attempt to master the world's peoples by labelling and pricing them (Della Coletta 2006p. 96). Allwood treats the fair mainly through scale and spectacle, not interpretation (Allwood et al. 2001p. 83). Mandell calls it the last really large American exposition before the decline of huge fairs (Mandell 1967p. 119).

The readings agree on the imperial and racial framing. They differ on whether the displays were mainly education, racial science or commerce. Roche stresses that governments always financed such events (Roche 2000p. 43). Hollengreen and colleagues stress the centrality of race to anthropologists' displays (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Kaiser sees the fair as a showcase for German social policy (Kaiserp. 50). Peterson, Hur and Fernsebner read the Asian displays through the struggle over national representation (Peterson 2020p. 80; Hurp. 85; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 177).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts give most attention to Japan, China and the Philippines, and say little about other Asian countries (Hurp. 84; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213).

Japan

Japan's official commitment was larger than at Paris in 1900. The space was double, the exhibits trebled and the expenditure was 820,000 yen. An appendix gives 800,000 yen for the same fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222). The Japanese section was ready before the opening despite the Russo-Japanese War (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). An enormous replica of the Yomei gate towered over the Pike (Shepherdson-Scottp. 541). Private showmen also worked there. Kushibiki, a Japanese businessman who specialised in popular amusements, ran the Fair Japan concession and recreated the Gate of Nikko over the entertainment area (Peterson 2020p. 61; Peterson 2020p. 63). Japanese visitors did not complain about the Japanese displays, unlike the bitter complaints about the London village of 1885 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192). Kaneko Kentaro attended to win American support for the war with Russia, met Roosevelt, and judged Americans more interested in showy arts than simple ones (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 214). Eight Ainu were sent after the anthropology chief persuaded Japan's minister in Washington that their display would be educational (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). ## China China's official participation began with an invitation from the US minister, Edwin H. Conger, which the Qing government accepted on 13 October 1901, weeks after the Boxer Protocol (Hurp. 86). Pulun 溥倫, a nephew of the Guangxu Emperor, was president of the commission, and Huang Kaijia 黄开甲 was vice-commissioner (Hurp. 87). Pulun estimated the cost at about 1,400,000 taels, and the government raised about 750,000 (Hurp. 89). The pavilion copied Pulun's summer residence and was dedicated on 6 May 1904, though major Chinese journals did not report the ceremony. The New York Times called it the most picturesque foreign structure (Hurp. 110; Hurp. 111).

Peterson calls China's display its only truly integrated exercise in national self-representation before 1915 (Peterson 2020p. 80). The exhibits were selected through foreign Customs staff, and Chinese journals blamed the Qing for it (Hurp. 100). In the Palace of Liberal Arts a Customs display of a bound-foot woman and an opium-addicted man drew more attention than the Chinese goods (Peterson 2020p. 109; Hurp. 99). Dongfang zazhi complained that all visitors looked down on China (Hurp. 100). Chen Qi and Chen Huide, Chinese visitors, complained of chaotic presentation and minerals without explanations, and counted China's flag at four sites against more than five hundred for other nations (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 175; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 176). Peterson says Chinese exhibitors faced hard entry to the United States, and that the fair was not remembered fondly in China (Peterson 2020p. 80).

The Philippines

The Philippine Reservation was the fair's most popular exhibit (Rydell 1993p. 19). Peterson says commercial operators brought Filipinos to live in the villages (Peterson 2020p. 182), while Greenhalgh describes an exposition board created by the Philippine Commission (Greenhalgh 1988p. 77). The constabulary and Filipino scouts showed Americans a model of order and discipline (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 175; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Rydell says the exhibit made Filipinos commodities and confirmed American superiority (Rydell 1993p. 20). Boger says it familiarised Americans with the new acquisitions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 174).

India, Siam and Ceylon

Allwood records India's pierced marble screens and Siamese exhibits set in a reproduction of a temple building from the Bangkok palace (Allwood et al. 2001p. 86; Allwood et al. 2001p. 86). Hoffenberg says colonial governments funded Indian displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 88).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — The texts treat the fair as a showcase of American power over new possessions, with the Philippine exhibit at its centre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 171; Greenhalgh 1988p. 76).
  • Race and anthropology — Anthropologists ranked peoples by evolutionary grade and tested visitors, which the texts read as confirming racial hierarchy (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • The Philippine Reservation — The 47-acre exhibit drew on company and federal money and reproduced Manila's walls and the Bridge of Spain, and the texts describe Filipinos as both fascinating and objectified (Greenhalgh 1988p. 77; Rydell 1993p. 19).
  • Civic boosterism and reform — The fair was designed to repair St. Louis's image after a strike and corruption scandals (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 178).
  • Architecture and landscape — A fan-shaped plan of white buildings and an ornamental park gave the fair its look (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 172; Maloney 2012p. 101).
  • Women's space — The Lady Managers had no separate building, and women's exhibits competed with men's (Ganz 2008).
  • Commerce, food and technology — Science and technology displays and food legends such as the ice cream cone are disputed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 185; Allwood et al. 2001p. 84).

Questions for study

  1. If the anthropology displays ranked living people by evolutionary grade, can the fair be called educational, or was education a cover for racial science?
  2. Why did Chinese journals judge the Qing display a national shame when the government had entered it to show modernity?
  3. Do the balance-sheet figures in the reference works agree, and how should a historian weigh a claimed success against a recorded loss?
  4. Did the Philippine Reservation teach Americans about their empire, or did it also shape how Filipinos understood their own future?
  5. How far should Kaneko's view of American taste be trusted as evidence of how the Japanese displays were received?
  6. How should a historian compare the stated purpose of an exhibit with the way its visitors and national critics received it?

Wikipedia

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition, informally known as the St. Louis World's Fair, was an international exposition held in St. Louis, Missouri, United States, from April 30 to December 1, 1904. Local, state, and federal funds totaling $15 million (equivalent to $538 million in 2025) were used to finance the event. More than 60 countries and 43 of the then-45 American states maintained exhibition spaces at the fair, which was attended by nearly 19.7 million people. Historians generally emphasize the prominence of the themes of race and imperialism, and the fair's long-lasting impact on intellectuals in the fields of history, architecture, and anthropology. From the point of view of the memory of the average person who attended the fair, it primarily promoted entertainment, consumer goods, and popular culture.
