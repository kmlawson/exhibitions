Ghent Universal and International Exhibition

Exposition Universelle et Internationale de Gand
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Ghent Universal and International Exhibition (Exposition Universelle et Internationale de Gand) opened on 26 April 1913 in Ghent (Filipová 2015p. 185). A Ghent society formed after the Liège fair of 1905 first examined the plan, and Gerard Cooreman presided over the executive committee (Filipová 2015p. 187; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420). The texts call it the largest of Belgium's fairs before the First World War and the last European fair before it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). Its colonial pavilions were pushed to the edges of the site, and its Filipino village drew reports of hunger and unpaid wages (Filipová 2015p. 191; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Davy Depelchin argues that the Ghent organisers gave the fair a local agenda. Public opinion favoured an international exhibition from 1889 (Filipová 2015p. 187), and the city had run a successful Provincial Exhibition in 1898 (Filipová 2015p. 187). Ghent's textile industry, nicknamed the Belgian Manchester, backed the scheme (Filipová 2015p. 187). The fair was rooted in the city's past while presenting modernity, and its promotion stressed cultural and city tourism; the poster showed the cathedral of Saint Bavo and the new post office (Filipová 2015p. 187p. 188). Depelchin calls the fair the end of a line rather than a catalyst for progress, since the war cut short its plans six months later (Filipová 2015p. 187). Preparations began in 1905, so Ghent and Brussels were planned at much the same time (Filipová 2015p. 195).

Site and buildings

The grounds covered three linked terrains south of the city, near the new Saint Peter's station, and the northern gate was a fifteen-minute walk from the historic centre (Filipová 2015p. 185). The entry gives the site as 309 acres around a city park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). The Court of Honour, modelled on the White City of Chicago in 1893, was designed by Oscar Van de Voorde (Filipová 2015p. 190). Depelchin describes the Avenue of Nations as the modern part of the site, set against Old Flanders, a picturesque reconstruction by Valentin Vaerwyck and Armand Heins (Filipová 2015p. 190). The Belgian city pavilions each took the style of their heyday (Filipová 2015p. 190). The Modern Village was a prototype for modern living, but Depelchin argues that it, like Old Flanders, promoted regionalism, as Tim Dickel also argued (Filipová 2015p. 190).

Empire and colony

The texts place the fair in a Belgian colonial setting. Antwerp in 1885, the first Belgian international exhibition, was the first showcase for the King's new acquisition in Central Africa, the Congo Free State (Filipová 2015p. 184). Depelchin applies Greenhalgh's four motives for fairs (industry, trade, national cohesion and empire) to the Belgian case (Filipová 2015p. 185). At Ghent the Belgian Congo pavilion stood at the end of the Avenue of Nations, a circular building with a panorama of the Congo by Paul Matthieu and Alfred Bastien (Filipová 2015p. 191; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). Depelchin finds its style hard to classify, calling it an orientalised classicism after Johan Lagae, and a hybrid because the architects could not find an authentic Congolese style (Filipová 2015p. 191p. 192). The entry says a good deal of emphasis was placed on showing Belgian imperialists and the empire in a favourable light (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). King Albert, opening the Belgian section on 15 May, urged parliament to give the natives autonomy and better living standards to benefit the empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).

France had seven pavilions for its colonies, protectorates and trade posts (Filipová 2015p. 193). Depelchin argues that the Maghreb pavilions were modern commercial showcases, with maps and products, and that their core business was to stimulate international trade (Filipová 2015p. 194). Morocco's pavilion was its first participation in a universal exhibition as a French protectorate, a status it had obtained on 30 March 1912 (Filipová 2015p. 194). The Algerian presentation was unofficial, an initiative of Algerian entrepreneurs (Filipová 2015p. 194). Morocco, Tunisia and the Algerian pavilion stood in a wooded part of the site (Filipová 2015p. 195). Depelchin notes that imperial powers treated colonies, protectorates and concessions as instruments of prestige (Filipová 2015p. 195).

Exhibits and attractions

Depelchin argues that the exotic was muted rather than absent. As he puts it, "Exotic features did appear on the fairgrounds in Ghent, albeit less obviously." (Filipová 2015p. 184). A display called Cairo Street, of stereotyped Middle Eastern scenes, stood near the French colonial section (Filipová 2015p. 195). The entry describes the fine arts pavilion, whose works Cardinal Francois Joseph Mercier forbade priests, school principals and parents to visit, and the Russian ballet with Nijinsky as a more acceptable attraction (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). Persia alone had an orientalist building on the Avenue of Nations (Filipová 2015p. 192p. 193). A three-man committee of Georges Van Oost, Louis Coetermans and Mahmoud Khan sought to build trade with Belgium (Filipová 2015p. 192). Coetermans had persuaded a shipping line in 1906 to link Antwerp directly with Persia, and Persia's 1913 pavilion was its first freestanding one at a Belgian fair (Filipová 2015p. 192).

At least six fires struck the fair, destroying the Indochina pavilion, a German restaurant and much of Old Flanders (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 217). A gold ingot valued at $20,000 was stolen from the Belgian colonial pavilion on 28 July; it proved to be an imitation worth $200 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).

Visitors and reception

Depelchin gives 9.5 million visitors over the six months the fair was open, with about 2 million single tickets, 86,000 season tickets and 500,000 passes for personnel (Filipová 2015p. 186). He reports that total visitors were 3.5 million fewer than at Brussels 1910, but he judges Ghent successful beside Antwerp, Brussels and Liège (Filipová 2015p. 186). The entry gives a different picture: "some 11 million visitors had been" counted by the close in early December (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). It says the fires and the Filipinos' plight probably kept attendance 2 million below Brussels 1910, while the flower beds and textile machinery drew praise (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). The Livre d'or that accompanied the fair seems to have been relieved that Ghent did not focus on the exotic (Filipová 2015p. 191).

The Filipino village

The Philippine village stood on the outskirts of Old Flanders, and visitors had to walk through half of that section to reach its only entrance (Filipová 2015p. 191). The entry describes an anthropological exhibit, doubtless modelled on recent American fairs, where the natives "were reported to be starving" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). Nine of the fifty-five people had died from exposure by early November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). The Filipinos said they had received no wages for eight months, after the agency that had brought them to Ghent went bankrupt, and a US congressman asked the Belgians to send them home (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 218).

Its place in the series

Depelchin lists seven international exhibitions in Belgium between 1885 and 1913: Antwerp 1885, Brussels 1888, Antwerp 1894, Brussels 1897, Liège 1905, Brussels 1910 and Ghent 1913 (Filipová 2015p. 184p. 185). Liège's success encouraged the Ghent promoters (Filipová 2015p. 187). The introduction contrasts Ghent with Brussels 1910, where exotic display against technological advance was emphasised (Filipová 2015p. 11). After the fair, the entry says, Belgium was overrun within a year, and its international fairs did not resume until 1930 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).

Disputed details

The texts differ on several points. Depelchin gives the closing date as 3 November (Filipová 2015p. 185); the two Findling entries say the fair closed in early December (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 218). Visitor figures are 9.5 million (Filipová 2015p. 186) against some 11 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 218), and the table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives 11,000,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). On the comparison with 1910, Depelchin reports 3.5 million fewer visitors (Filipová 2015p. 186), while the entries report 2 million fewer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 218). The title is also given differently: Depelchin gives Universal and International and notes that Findling's Universal and Industrial is wrong (Filipová 2015p. 188p. 197); the 2008 entry prints Exposition Universelle et Internationale (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213); the 1990 entry prints Exposition Universelle et Industrielle (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 217). The 2008 entry has King Albert urging mere autonomy for the natives (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214), where the 1990 entry has more autonomy (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 217); the extractions cannot settle which is right. Site figures are given in square metres (Filipová 2015p. 185) and in acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 217), and are left unconverted here.

How historians have interpreted it

Depelchin, in the 2015 collection edited by Marta Filipová, argues that the emphasis on local history suppressed exoticism and that Ghent reshaped the universal exhibition to fit local ambitions (Filipová 2015p. 195). He puts it this way: "The Ghent exhibition of 1913 illustrates how the concept of the universal exhibition could be rethought to meet local demands and ambitions." (Filipová 2015p. 196). He places the Maghreb showcases within a shift to neo-mercantilist display, and cites Johan Lagae on the Congo palace as a nationalist image of the Belgian colony (Filipová 2015p. 194p. 198). The editor's introduction restates the contrast with Brussels 1910 (Filipová 2015p. 11). Geppert treats Ghent as a town-planning exposition in which the city itself was the subject, and notes Geddes's extensive report on it (Geppert 2010p. 239). Findling's entries are descriptive and offer no interpretation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213).

Philippines, India, Indochina and Japan

The Philippine village, and the Filipinos' treatment there, is discussed in the section on the Filipino village above (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). The Indian delegation erected a Moghul-style pavilion, an initiative of the Bhumgara trading company. It was not prominent enough to be taken seriously as an official pavilion, stood between a restaurant and a sanitary block, and appears on few plans (Filipová 2015p. 193). The Indochina pavilion was among the buildings destroyed by fire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 217). Japan appears only in Depelchin's list of participating countries (Filipová 2015p. 186).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Local history against exoticism — Depelchin argues that the organisers' emphasis on Ghent's past and future suppressed exoticism and pushed the exotic pavilions out of sight (Filipová 2015p. 195).
  • Colonial display as commerce — the French colonial and Maghreb pavilions were designed as trade showcases rather than picturesque displays (Filipová 2015p. 194).
  • Belgian empire on show — the Congo pavilion was given prominence, and the entry says it showed the empire favourably; Antwerp 1885 had shown the Congo Free State (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Filipová 2015p. 184).
  • Human display — the Filipino village was hidden in one text and reported as starving with unpaid wages in another (Filipová 2015p. 191; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).
  • Attendance and disaster — the texts give different attendance figures, and fires and a theft marked the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214; Filipová 2015p. 186).
  • The city as exhibition — Geppert places Ghent among exhibitions where the city itself was the theme (Geppert 2010p. 239).

Questions for study

  1. Depelchin argues that Ghent suppressed the exotic, yet the Congo pavilion kept a prominent place at the end of the Avenue of Nations. How far does the local-history explanation account for that position? (Filipová 2015p. 191p. 195)
  2. The texts give 9.5 million and about 11 million visitors. What might account for the gap, and which figure would you trust more? (Filipová 2015p. 186; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214)
  3. The entry's account of the Filipino village rests on contemporary reports of starvation and unpaid wages. What evidence would a historian need to check those reports? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214)
  4. Depelchin reads Ghent as a regional and commercial fair, while Geppert reads it as a town-planning exposition. Which reading better fits the passages each text gives? (Filipová 2015p. 187; Geppert 2010p. 239)

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
Introduction: The Margins of Exhibitions and Exhibitions StudiesMarta Filipováp. 1Asia

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

In the contents of this work

8The Ghent Universal and International Exhibition of 1913: Reconciling Historicism, Modernity and ExoticismDavy Depelchinp. 183

Depelchin asks why the Ghent exhibition of 1913, which was formally universal and international, gave so little prominence to the exotic, which usually featured so strongly in universal exhibitions. He places Ghent within Belgium's run of international exhibitions from Antwerp in 1885 to Ghent itself, and within the city's need to define itself through local history. The chapter then describes the fairground: the white Beaux-Arts Court of Honour, the 'Old Flanders' and 'Modern Village' sections that promoted architectural regionalism, and the Belgian city pavilions in historical styles. Its central case is the colonial and non-Western pavilions. Persia had the only prominent orientalist building on the Avenue of Nations, the Congo pavilion took a hybrid style, and the Moroccan, Tunisian and Algerian pavilions were pushed into a wooded area and presented as trading showcases. The French colonial displays were more commercial than picturesque. Depelchin concludes that the organizers' emphasis on local history suppressed exoticism, and that Ghent reshaped the universal exhibition to fit local ambitions.

In The Wake of Exposition Gardensp. 16Asia

Brown considers the gardens' legacy as relations between Japan and the United States soured from about 1913. American fascination with Japanese culture was separated from views of Japan as a political power, and the feminine, pastoral image became easy to dismiss once Japan was a threat. He asks whether the propaganda delayed American responses to Japanese expansion, citing the St. Louis displays of 1904 and the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939 and 1940.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.
