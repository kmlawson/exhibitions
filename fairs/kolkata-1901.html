In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Identification and status

The texts give the name as the Indian Industrial and Agricultural Exhibition, at Calcutta in 1901 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 401). Both reference works list it in Appendix D, Fairs Not Included, which covers exhibitions held after 1851 that fell short of the qualifications for the main body of each book (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). Each entry gives only the year, the city and the name (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 401).

Hoffenberg does not use this name. He refers to the Calcutta exhibitions of 1901 as one of the Indian National Congress's annual nationalist shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59; Hoffenberg 2001p. 59). The link between the Calcutta show and the Congress therefore rests on that one passage (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59).

Hoffenberg places the Calcutta show in a series of annual Congress exhibitions. The series ran from 1901 to the outbreak of the Great War in 1914 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193). Its exhibits of swadeshi and railways were shown alongside Congress sessions at Madras, Bombay, Benares, Lahore and other major centres (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193).

Empire and colony

The texts say little about the imperial side of this show. Hoffenberg places it in British India and writes that the exhibitions in Calcutta (1901) and Madras (1903) "transformed the formerly imperial spectacle into celebrations of swadeshi" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59). He asks whether South Asians who organised exhibition displays could undermine British rule in India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59). He does not show that this happened at Calcutta. The passages read name no colonies or dominions that took part, no colonial products, and no native villages in the 1901 show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Hoffenberg gives one example of South Asian leadership in these shows. The South Asian president of the committee organising the Lahore exhibition wrote that the "great value of Exhibitions is that they show the industrial resources of the countries which display their goods" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193; Hoffenberg 2001p. 193). Hoffenberg describes two rival visions of technology: one of large-scale industrial projects with a Eurocentric shape for the imagined Indian nation, and one of small-scale, traditionalist forms. He says both informed the Congress series (Hoffenberg 2001p. 192; Hoffenberg 2001p. 192). The passages read give no South Asian exhibitors, commissioners or visitors for the 1901 Calcutta show itself (Hoffenberg 2001p. 59).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Nationalist use of the exhibition form — Hoffenberg says that by the turn of the century the Indian National Congress had taken up the exhibition form for a series of annual nationalist shows (Hoffenberg 2001)
  • Swadeshi and self-sufficiency — he reads the Calcutta and Madras shows as celebrations of swadeshi, or economic self-sufficiency, and of artistic self-determination (Hoffenberg 2001)
  • Industrialism and rival visions of technology — he argues that the Raj and the Congress both promoted industrialism at exhibitions, and that the two visions fed different ideas of the Indian nation (Hoffenberg 2001)
  • Excluded fairs — both reference works list the 1901 show only in appendices of fairs outside their main entries, and give no detail beyond year, city and name (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990)

In chapter 2 he argues that South Asians became producers as well as consumers of imperial knowledge, and that Congress shows turned the imperial exhibition form into celebrations of swadeshi, or economic self-sufficiency. About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
