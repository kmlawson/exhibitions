Paris Universal Exposition

Exposition universelle internationale de 1900
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle of 1900 opened in Paris on 14 April 1900 and closed on 12 November, with 50,860,801 recorded visits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 150; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155). It was the last of the five Paris universal exhibitions begun in 1855, and the biggest world's exhibition yet held (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 162; Roche 2000). Historians value it for its colonial displays, its national pavilions, and the effect of the Dreyfus Affair on foreign opinion (Greenhalgh 1988p. 36; Mandell 1967p. 32). Whether it was a triumph or a failure, and whether it broke with the nineteenth century, remain disputed (Mandell 1967p. 120; Geppert 2010p. 64).

Origins and aims

The Third Republic announced the exposition by decree on 13 July 1892, eight years before it opened (Mandell 1967p. 32). The government moved quickly after a June 1892 Le Figaro report that Germany intended a universal exhibition in Berlin for 1900 (Mandell 1967p. 31). Jules Roche, the minister in charge, said the fair would sum up the nineteenth century while inaugurating the twentieth (Geppert 2010p. 64).

Alfred Picard was appointed head of the governing commission on 9 September 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 149). By November 1895 he projected 60 million visitors. More than forty of the 53 foreign powers invited accepted, the German Empire among them for the first time at a Paris fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 149). Mandell argues that Picard's plan to stress retrospective displays was rarely followed, because nations preferred to show products that would sell (Mandell 1967p. 56). Planners first fixed the dates as 5 May to 31 October, then 15 April to 12 November, with 47 nations taking part (Geppert 2010p. 77). Mandell gives the decree's dates as 15 April to 15 October (Mandell 1967p. 32).

The opposition to the show came from the provinces as well as from the press. Greenhalgh says that committed decentralists troubled the early planning (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119). Maurice Barrès led an attack in Le Figaro, and Léon Goulette, editor of L'Est Republique in Nancy, led the most vigorous campaign against it (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119; Greenhalgh 1988p. 119). Greenhalgh argues that the nationalist role of the expositions alienated the provincial bourgeoisie, who had a stake in promoting their own regions (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119; Greenhalgh 1988p. 119). The state-funded expositions in Marseilles, in 1906 and 1922, were almost certainly a result of agitation from the south (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119).

Organisation and finance

The Chamber debated the government's bill in March 1896, and the Senate passed it on 12 June 1896 (Mandell 1967p. 149). The law approved 105 million francs: 20 million each from the state and the city of Paris, and 65 million from admission coupons sold in advance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 150). The bankers who bought the coupons needed a visitor forecast, and they used the 39 million admissions of 1889 as their guide (Mandell 1967p. 38).

Concessions were crowded and expensive. Restaurant spaces rose from 37 in 1889 to 207 in 1900, and sixty owners threatened a strike on 14 September 1900 (Mandell 1967p. 106). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the concession rents were set on expected attendance, which fell short by nearly a third, and that the organisers later refunded part of the fees (Allwood et al. 2001p. 70).

The accounts disagree. Mandell sets expenses of 116 million francs against receipts of 74 million, an apparent loss of 42 million. Counting subsidies as receipts, the usual French method, cuts the loss to about 2 million (Mandell 1967p. 107). Picard's January 1901 estimate also gave a loss of 2 million, while accounts of 1903 show a profit of 7 million, and others suggest up to 9 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155). Allwood, Allan and Reid record a loss of 82,000 francs (Allwood et al. 2001p. 70). Brown's entry concludes that the exposition "must be reckoned a financial success" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155).

Site, architecture and attractions

The main grounds covered 279 acres in Paris and the Vincennes annex 274 acres, in a layout that formed an immense letter A across central Paris (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 150). The architects, largely affiliates of the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, set neo-baroque stone facades in front of iron and glass. Critics called the temporary palaces chaotic (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 152; Allwood et al. 2001p. 71). The Grand Palais, the Petit Palais and the Pont Alexandre III outlasted the fair (Geppert 2010p. 65).

Art Nouveau appeared in Hector Guimard's metro entrances (Allwood et al. 2001p. 71). Greenhalgh says it arrived in real force, although neobaroque and neorococo styles were still generally preferred (Greenhalgh 1988p. 160). In the Centennale, Roger Marx hung impressionists beside David and Courbet, while the Décennale had none (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 154).

Attractions ranged from the serious to the sensational. Grimoin-Sanson's Cineorama closed after three days because of fire risk, and the moving walkway caused about forty minor accidents (Allwood et al. 2001p. 76; Allwood et al. 2001p. 78). The Tour du Monde cost 2 million francs and took 691,245 francs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155). Bransford argues that Paris set out to outdo Chicago with a new generation of illusions, and the railway panorama built by the Compagnie Internationale des Wagons-Lits simulated the fourteen-day journey from Moscow to Peking (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 201; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 202). The fair hosted 127 congresses, the largest on medicine (Benedict 1983p. 43). Benedict describes the Porte Monumentale, which could admit 60,000 people an hour, and La Parisienne, a modern woman in evening dress who crowned the gateway (Benedict 1983p. 13; Benedict 1983p. 17). The Olympic Games were held at Vincennes, though Picard, who disliked sport, spread them thinly across the five months (Allwood et al. 2001p. 78; Roche 2000p. 88).

Mandell measures the grounds on foot. From Binet's gate to the far end of the Galerie des Machines was about 2¼ miles (Mandell 1967p. 63; Mandell 1967p. 63). The electric moving walkway was a private speculation and a novelty, but few visitors used it, and almost everyone walked (Mandell 1967p. 63; Mandell 1967p. 63). The new Petit Palais, with its retrospective of older French art, was among the best planned and most heavily visited parts of the site (Mandell 1967p. 63; Mandell 1967p. 63). Mandell admires the Grand Palais for its leaded-glass roof and its wrought-iron staircase, and the Petit Palais for the wrought-iron grille before its entrance (Mandell 1967p. 72; Mandell 1967p. 72; Mandell 1967p. 72).

The gate itself needs a note. Mandell says Binet's Porte de la Concorde admitted up to 75,000 ticket holders an hour (Mandell 1967p. 74; Mandell 1967p. 74). The essay gives the Porte Monumentale a capacity of 60,000 an hour, from Benedict (Benedict 1983p. 13). The two texts may describe the same gate, and the figures differ.

Nations and colonies on show

The colonial sections were the fair's centre. Greenhalgh calls the colonial area the most impressive part of the site (Greenhalgh 1988p. 67). Mandell says France took far more than half of the Trocadéro's space (Mandell 1967p. 66), and that Indo-China, Cambodia, Senegal, Tunisia and Algeria had pavilions grander than those of several sovereign states (Mandell 1967p. 66). France brought hundreds of native craftsmen to build them, and they stayed through the summer (Mandell 1967p. 66).

Hale describes a pavilion for each colony, native villages of artisans, entertainers and soldiers, and cafés selling exotic goods (Hale 2008p. 14). Colonial administrators in Dahomey, Senegal and Madagascar paid for transport and lodging for workers and their families (Hale 2008p. 32). Hale argues that the French used the colonial exhibits "to express confidence in their own race and in La Plus Grande France" (Hale 2008p. 39). Çelik argues that the Algerian and Tunisian palaces, set beside the Eiffel Tower, placed the empire's industrial progress against the timelessness of its colonies (Çelik 1992p. 91–92).

Mandell and Hale disagree about how much of the Trocadéro the French took. Mandell says that France took far more than half of the area there (Mandell 1967p. 66). Hale says the French colonial sections were confined to half of the Trocadéro plot, on the far side of the Seine from the main exhibits (Hale 2008p. 165; Hale 2008p. 165). Mandell's own account of the Grand Palais gives France far more than half of that building's space (Mandell 1967p. 64; Mandell 1967p. 64).

Hale says that officials handed most of the living displays to colonist entrepreneurs (Hale 2008p. 165; Hale 2008p. 165). The Algerian section's concession company hired a series of exotic entertainment firms to create a picture of Algerian culture (Hale 2008p. 170; Hale 2008p. 170). In the hands of businessmen, the Algerian attractions became a noisy spectacle of oddities, close to what the administration had conceded in the show contracts (Hale 2008p. 170; Hale 2008p. 170). Later colonial officials contracted performers directly to avoid this (Hale 2008p. 170; Hale 2008p. 170). Charles-Roux, the colonial minister's delegate, complained that the cramped Trocadéro site and the short time allowed made it impossible to do the colonies justice (Hale 2008p. 166; Hale 2008p. 166).

Peoples on display

Greenhalgh reports that about a thousand people worked on the Rue du Caire for six months, and its small theatres offered belly-dancing (Greenhalgh 1988p. 103). Ernest Pourtauborde was a partner in the company that ran the Algerian attractions, and he had created the Oriental villages at the fair (Hale 2008p. 57; Della Coletta 2006p. 100). Mandell notes that the Algerian and Egyptian dancers alarmed those who wanted an elevated moral tone (Mandell 1967p. 65). Hale argues that the sub-Saharan villages reinforced an image of African primitiveness set against French power (Hale 2008p. 32).

Visitors, reception and politics

The Dreyfus Affair hung over the fair. Le Figaro warned on 23 February 1898 that foreign papers were telling readers to stay away, and Paris shops and hotels reported a slump (Mandell 1967p. 92). Greenhalgh argues that Dreyfus, not republicanism, was the central political issue, and that on the eve of opening Picard faced threatened mass withdrawals of exhibitors (Greenhalgh 1988p. 36). Mandell reports that Liverpool merchants threatened to remove their exhibits, and that the Anglican Bishop of Calcutta proposed that India withdraw (Mandell 1967p. 95; Mandell 1967p. 95). Mandell argues that the reaction abroad to the second court-martial at Rennes forced action before the fair (Mandell 1967p. 93). Caricatures of the dining showed that the wealthy paid most, and photographs of ordinary visitors eating on park benches show the other end of the market (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 436; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 438).

Attendance reached 50,860,801, against a cautious plan of 60 million (Mandell 1967p. 109). It lagged until August, and 7 October was the peak day with 652,082 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155). The texts give several causes: late completion, summer heat, resentment abroad over the Affair, and the absence of visiting monarchs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 161). The totals differ. Badger's appendix gives 39 million (Badger 1979p. 131), Allwood, Allan and Reid's table gives 48,130,300 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207), and Greenhalgh gives 48 million (Greenhalgh 1988p. 37). The reference entries give 50,860,801, and the essay follows them.

Aftermath and legacy

After the fair, Picard became President of the Conseil d'Etat. The Comité français des expositions à l'étranger was declared a body of public utility by decree of 12 June 1901, and its membership grew from 400 in 1900 to 1,300 in 1913 (Mandell 1967p. 118). Roche argues that the French elites were exhausted by the monumental fair, and that France held no further exposition until Marseilles in 1922 (Roche 2000p. 42). Charles Garnier had already asked in 1900 that two Champs-Élysées palaces become a permanent colonial museum (Geppert 2010p. 190). Women's participation also drew notice: Bertha Palmer, the only woman among the United States commissioners, used the post to win places for women on the awards juries (Weimann 1981p. 595; Weimann 1981p. 595).

The Finnish pavilion

Finland won its own pavilion through three men: the senator Leo Mechelin, who negotiated with the tsar, the artist Albert Edelfelt, who was Finnish art commissioner, and the engineer Robert Runeberg, who spoke Russian (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 132; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 132; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 132; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 133). The permission came with two conditions. The site was a poor one, at the back of the Quai d'Orsay, and the building had to carry the words "Le Pavilion Finlandais, Section Russe" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 133; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 133).

Pushaw says the Finns chose stone. The winning design by Gesellius, Lindgren and Saarinen was a rural stone church, planned like a basilica with a bell tower and set in grey and white (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 133; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 134; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 134; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 134). He adds that the Russians built their own pavilion entirely of wood (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 133). Mandell, by contrast, describes Saarinen's building as constructed of native woods (Mandell 1967p. 77). He calls it possibly the architectural jewel of the 1900 fair (Mandell 1967p. 77). The two accounts cannot both be right about the building's material. Edelfelt's own verdict, after he viewed it from a distance, was "our small, very small Finnish church" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 135).

Inside, Akseli Gallen-Kallela's four Kalevala frescoes were the pavilion's lasting image. One showed the defence of the sampo against the witch Louhi, and another Ilmarinen ploughing a field of vipers, coloured red, blue and white, the colours of the Russian flag (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137). Pushaw says the international critics either did not see these allusions or did not wish to mention them (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 137). Emil Halonen's relief of a Sámi reindeer herder stood in the pavilion, and Pushaw notes that the Sámi were one of the peoples put on display at world's fairs, though they avoided that fate in 1900 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 138; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 139). The Svecoman elite, the Swedish-speaking faction, criticised the interior as too Finnish-nationalist in its emphasis (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 140; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 140). The pavilion was later destroyed, although many of its paintings went to Helsinki's state gallery (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 143; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 143).

The Musée Centennal du Mobilier et de la Décoration

Fair officials asked each French product section to add a retrospective centennial museum to its display (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 111; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 111). Five sections, for fixed decoration, furniture, carpets, mobile decoration and bronzes, merged into one museum under François Carnot, president of the Union Centrale des Arts Décoratifs (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 111; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 111). It was 80 metres long and designed by Jacques Hermant (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 112; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 112). Its eight period rooms, planned along the same axis, ran from Louis XVI to Napoléon III, and electricity was banned from them for fear of fire (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 114; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 114; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 114).

The rooms were built from original pieces where possible. One hundred and forty-six private collectors joined the effort, and the Comédie-Française, the Mobilier National and the Gobelins lent further items (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121). The upholsterers Georges and Henri Rémon finished the missing parts with imitations (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121). Jules Claretie, writing in Le Journal in November 1900, called the rooms a memory palace (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 121). Lasc argues that the museum presented the nineteenth century as one unbroken line of revivals ending in the work of Gallé, Majorelle and Chéret, whose Gallé cabinet, Majorelle chest and Chéret pastels closed the sequence (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 125; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 125; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 125).

Bing, Art Nouveau and the critics

Mandell says that conservatives controlled the decorative displays, so Siegfried Bing could show unorthodox art only in a seven-room house on the Esplanade des Invalides (Mandell 1967p. 75; Mandell 1967p. 75; Mandell 1967p. 75). Lasc names Georges de Feure, Edouard Colonna and Eugène Gaillard as the artists in that pavilion (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 119; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 119; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 119). Mandell disputes the common view that Art Nouveau peaked at the fair. In his reading it was already "lost optimism and decay" by 1900 (Mandell 1967p. 75; Mandell 1967p. 75).

Critics were divided. André Hallays, writing in the Journal des débats, praised Alexandre Charpentier's furniture for the Grands Magasins du Louvre while disliking the style in general (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 119; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120). Gabriel Mourey judged Charpentier's dining room to have an excess of truth in its natural ornament (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 120). Mandell also records that Hector Guimard showed sample interiors from his Castel Béranger at a stand on the Esplanade, as well as his Metro work (Mandell 1967p. 76; Mandell 1967p. 76; Mandell 1967p. 76).

Spain's school exhibit and its reception

Del Pozo Andrés places Spain's showing at Paris in 1900 in a wider comparison. After the defeat in Cuba, Spanish commentators wrote bitterly that Spain was placed below Portugal at the first world's fair of the twentieth century (Lawn 2009p. 170; Lawn 2009p. 170; Lawn 2009p. 170). The Swiss commission was harsh: it dismissed Spain as not worth attention, citing an illiteracy rate of 681 per 1,000 (Lawn 2009p. 171; Lawn 2009p. 171). Some Spanish visitors refused to visit the American pavilion or mention it, as a mark of patriotism, while others studied its exhibits and judged the American display the finest in education (Lawn 2009p. 171; Lawn 2009p. 171; Lawn 2009p. 171; Lawn 2009p. 171).

How historians have interpreted it

Mandell, whose book-length study is the fullest in the texts, argues that the fair was great but a failure as a statement of Western civilisation. It "showed Europe so much more than she actually wished to see": a glut of goods and the pursuit of wealth and power, which dispelled faith in progress and cosmopolitan co-operation and left France looking weak even to herself (Mandell 1967p. 120). Brown, in both reference entries, takes a more qualified view. The fair was a financial success, and most contemporaries thought it a success, though not the astonishing one anticipated. Brown draws on Mandell for the pessimistic reading (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 162). Patrick Geddes called the fair a "literal museum of the present" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 162).

Geppert argues against the standard view that 1900 was a decisive break. The apparent decline of the medium may reflect a shift in its function, and 1900 was a retrospective synthesis of the century (Geppert 2010p. 64). Greenhalgh calls Paris 1900 the most developed example of the exhibition as an unreal paradise, and reads the colonial sections and La Parisienne as images of a confident empire at its peak (Greenhalgh 1988p. 47; Greenhalgh 1988p. 189). Hale argues that France used the colonial displays to stress cultural supremacy, modernisation and national responsibility within an imperial framework (Hale 2008p. 39). Çelik reads the North African palaces as a visual symbol of French tactics of assimilation and contrast (Çelik 1992p. 91–92). Benedict treats the fairs as collective representations of nations and classes during industrial change, and reads La Parisienne as a figure of consumer society (Benedict 1983p. 17). Molella follows Henry Adams, who saw the dynamo of science clashing with the Virgin of faith and thought the dynamo would likely prevail (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 97; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 107).

The readings agree that the colonial and national displays were the fair's centre. They differ on the outcome: Mandell and Brown see a failure or a qualified success, while Geppert sees continuity rather than rupture (Mandell 1967p. 120; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155; Geppert 2010p. 64). They also differ on what the displays meant. Hale and Greenhalgh read them as propaganda for empire, while Benedict reads them as a broader anthropology of nations (Hale 2008p. 39; Benedict 1983p. 17).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan was the most visible Asian presence in the texts. The Japanese Diet allotted 1.3 million yen to the exposition, and for the first time valuable art owned by the Emperor, noblemen and temples was shown (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). The section included 142 Western-style paintings (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 121) and a twenty-metre model of the Kondo (golden hall) of Horyuji, built near the Trocadero from materials bought in France (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). Hotta-Lister says the organisers anticipated that the fair would change European opinion, which after the 1894-95 war took Japan more seriously (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). Mandell reports that Japan's requests for more space were repeatedly refused (Mandell 1967p. 56). De Vogüé judged that Japan surpassed France in art (Mandell 1967p. 113). Kaneko Kentaro had been a commissioner for the 1900 exposition, and afterwards advised on future Japanese displays (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 214).

China's exhibit was a diplomatic problem. Hur, drawing on letters between Robert Hart and Campbell, shows that the French government did not want an Englishman on China's commission (Hurp. 15). Charles Vapereau, a former French teacher at the Tongwen Guan, was finally appointed Commissioner-General (Hurp. 15). A Chinese visitor condemned a Shanghai troupe in the entertainment sections as grotesque and disgraceful (Hurp. 20). The Qingyibao, a reformist journal published in Japan, reviewed about twenty national pavilions but did not mention China (Hurp. 34). Hale notes that the French described the Chinese as troublesome, but Southeast Asians as model subjects (Hale 2008p. 68).

The Pavilion de la Corée stood on the Champs de Mars (Mandell 1967p. 91). Park reads it as shaped by Western images of a Hermit Kingdom, and as a declaration of independence for the newly proclaimed Taehan Empire (Parkp. 5). Henry says late Chosŏn leaders set up displays at Paris 1900 (Henry 2014p. 90). The texts do not say who paid for the Korean pavilion.

India was shown as a treasury. Greenhalgh says India's exhibiting reached its grandest scale in a grand palace at Paris 1900, a store of opulent produce staffed by beautifully dressed Indian attendants (Greenhalgh 1988p. 60). The Indian government normally paid half to two-thirds of such palaces' costs (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Maurice Talmeyr attacked the picture, asking why a starving India was embodied in well-dressed attendants (Greenhalgh 1988p. 60). Indian coffee planters argued for the same government grants as the tea industry (Hoffenberg 2001p. 325).

Indo-China and Cambodia had pavilions grander than those of several sovereign states (Mandell 1967p. 66). The Indochinese palaces copied temples from Vietnam and Cambodia, and Governor-General Paul Doumer hired a colonist to run a theatre for Indochinese actors, dancers and musicians (Hale 2008p. 68; Hale 2008p. 68). Hale argues that the official image of the Indochinese as diligent and gentle masked resistance to French rule (Hale 2008p. 68). The Tour du Monde showed Ceylon, Cambodia, China and Japan, with living natives in the foreground (Benedict 1983p. 56).

The pavilion of Asiatic Russia was a joint venture with Wagons-Lits Cook. Visitors sat as passengers on a Trans-Siberian journey past posed Siberian and Chinese people (Mandell 1967p. 79).

Hur cites Ito for the claim that the Taiwan Pavilion at Osaka 1903 was inspired by the colonial exhibitions of Paris 1900 (Hurp. 73). Hale and Park read the displays as statements of power: the Indochinese as model subjects, the Korean pavilion as a claim to independence (Hale 2008p. 68; Parkp. 5). Findling and Pelle report that some French observers concluded that France looked second-rate, overshadowed by Germany, the United States and even Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155).

Hale shows that the French sorted the Indochinese into groups. Groups counted as advanced received more positive treatment at the fair, while others were presented as primitive (Hale 2008p. 171; Hale 2008p. 171). Observers praised the Vietnamese actors of the Théâtre Annamite (Hale 2008p. 171). Organisers set aside a special building for Asian art and hired indigenous workers to carve and paint the pavilions (Hale 2008p. 171; Hale 2008p. 171).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial display — the colonial sections were the fair's centre, and Hale and Çelik read them as propaganda for race, assimilation and empire (Hale 2008p. 39; Çelik 1992p. 91–92).
  • Dreyfus and foreign boycotts — the Affair shaped foreign press and merchant threats and, in the texts' view, the shortfall in attendance (Mandell 1967p. 92; Greenhalgh 1988p. 36).
  • Money, concessions and accounts — the texts give a loss of 82,000 francs, a loss of 2 million, and a profit of up to 9 million, depending on what is counted (Allwood et al. 2001p. 70; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 155).
  • Architecture and Art Nouveau — Beaux-Arts stone facades over iron and glass, and Guimard's Art Nouveau metro entrances (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 152; Allwood et al. 2001p. 71).
  • Entertainment and illusion — panoramas, the Cineorama and the Tour du Monde sold simulated travel to mass audiences (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 201; Benedict 1983p. 56).
  • National pavilions and national image — the British pavilion presented a domestic national profile, and Korea's pavilion claimed independence (Greenhalgh 1988p. 123; Parkp. 5).
  • Optimism and fin-de-siècle doubt — observers such as Geddes saw both a glittering surface and a retrospective museum (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 162).

Questions for study

  1. How far did the colonial villages and palaces persuade visitors that empire was normal, and what did critics such as Talmeyr make of that picture?
  2. Geppert argues that 1900 was not a break, and Mandell calls it a turning point. What evidence from the fair would settle this disagreement?
  3. The texts give a loss of 82,000 francs, a loss of 2 million and a profit of up to 9 million. What should a student take "success" to mean for the organisers?
  4. Japan and Korea both used the fair to claim a place among the nations. Did their pavilions change how the European powers saw them, or mainly how Japan and Korea saw themselves?
  5. Panoramas and rides let visitors travel to distant places without leaving Paris. What did that teach them about the peoples they were shown?

Mandell, Richard D. Paris 1900: The Great World’s Fair. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1967.

Mandell's book is a study of the Paris Exposition of 1900 set against the history of nineteenth-century world's fairs. Its argument is that the fair, planned as a display of French confidence, was shaped by politics: the state controlled it, opponents such as the Nancy editor Goulette campaigned against it, and the Dreyfus Affair was quietly suspended while it ran. The early chapters cover the French industrial exhibitions from 1798, the London exhibition of 1851 and the Paris expositions of 1855 to 1889. The rest follows the planning and opposition, a tour of the grounds, the political background, the verdict on success and the end of the era of great French expositions. The scope is Paris and France, with attention to national pavilions, decorative arts and finance.

Mandell traces exhibitions in the nineteenth century as the background to the Paris Exposition of 1900. He begins with markets and medieval fairs, then with the Paris salons and the first French industrial exhibitions from 1798, which made prizes and medals central and tied displays to state policy under Napoleon and later regimes. The chapter follows the spread of national and international shows: the London Crystal Palace of 1851, with its jury system and surplus, and the French universal expositions of 1855, 1867, 1878 and 1889, each assessed by its organisers, finances and political purpose. Mandell argues that the French state, unlike private organisers elsewhere, controlled these events. The chapter ends with the 1889 triumph and its reputation for French prestige, followed by the political troubles of the next decade.

Wikipedia

The Exposition Universelle of 1900 (French pronunciation: [ɛkspozisjɔ̃ ynivɛʁsɛl]), better known in English as the 1900 Paris Exposition, was a world's fair held in Paris, France, from 14 April to 12 November 1900, to celebrate the achievements of the past century and to accelerate development into the next. It was the sixth of ten major expositions held in the city between 1855 and 1937. It was held at the esplanade of Les Invalides, the Champ de Mars, the Trocadéro and at the banks of the Seine between them, with an additional section in the Bois de Vincennes, and it was visited by more than fifty million people. Many international congresses and other events were held within the framework of the exposition, including the 1900 Summer Olympics.
