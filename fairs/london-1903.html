International Fire Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Fire Exhibition was held in 1903 at Earl's Court in west London (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 102). The British Fire Prevention Committee organised it (Geppert 2010p. 121). The sources say little more about it. Its one notable feature was an entire section, Assouan: The Village of the Nile, brought from Egypt (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Organisers and place

The fair was organised by the British Fire Prevention Committee, and the text names no impresario for it (Geppert 2010p. 121). Earl's Court, the venue, served as a permanent exhibition centre from 1887 through the First World War (Geppert 2010p. 102). Geppert's appendix lists the fair under the heading "Earl's Court Exhibitions" (Geppert 2010p. 254; Geppert 2010p. 254). In that list it falls between the 1902 Paris in London Exhibition and the 1904 Italian Exhibition (Venice by Night) (Geppert 2010p. 254; Geppert 2010p. 254).

Exhibits and attractions

The fair included an entire section, Assouan: The Village of the Nile (Geppert 2010p. 121). Geppert reports that it was extolled as a "graphic and real representation of life on the Sacred River" and that it had been brought from Egypt by permission of the government (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121).

Empire and colony

The passage shows little that was imperial about this fair. Geppert says that the connection between the Egyptian section and the exhibition's overall subject "remained unclear, to say the least" (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121). He places the section in a wider pattern. Since the mid-1880s, native villages and exotic sections had become a standard part of major exhibitions, and even "thematically unrelated" and smaller shows included them (Geppert 2010p. 120; Geppert 2010p. 120). The text does not say what the village was meant to do for empire or trade (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121). Geppert describes the exhibitions that Kiralfy staged at Earl's Court from 1894 as devoted to imperial themes (Geppert 2010p. 105; Geppert 2010p. 105). That description concerns Kiralfy's shows, and the text does not apply it to this fair (Geppert 2010p. 121).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Native villages as standard exhibits — Geppert argues that such villages were a regular feature of major exhibitions from the mid-1880s, and counts this fair among the smaller, thematically unrelated shows that still had one (Geppert 2010p. 120; Geppert 2010p. 120).
  • An Egyptian village — the Assouan section was promoted as a "graphic and real representation" of life on the Nile, but Geppert calls its link to the fair's subject unclear (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121).
  • Contrast with 1908 — Geppert sets the fair's ethnographic section against the more carefully integrated ensembles of the Franco-British Exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 121; Geppert 2010p. 121).
  • Organisers — the British Fire Prevention Committee, not an impresario, is named as organiser (Geppert 2010p. 121).

Questions for study

  1. Why would an exhibition with this subject include a whole village from Egypt, and what could its organisers have gained from it?
  2. How did native villages become a standard feature even of shows with no imperial theme, and what does that suggest about who chose to display colonised peoples?
  3. What would the fair's daily programme, cited by Geppert, need to show before a reader could say whether the Assouan village was meant as a colonial exhibit?

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

Brief discussion 1

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
Ch. 4, 'London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition', pp. 120-21 (l. 5588-5602); note 48 (l. 13621); appendix chronology (l. 10991) and 'Earl's Court Exhibitions' list (l. 11114); venue background pp. 102-3 (l. 4804-4807)

Geppert gives the 1903 International Fire Exhibition one paragraph, as a comparison within his chapter on the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. He notes that it was organised by the British Fire Prevention Committee and included an Egyptian village, Assouan, brought from Egypt. He argues that native villages had become a standard part of major exhibitions since the mid-1880s, and that even thematically unrelated shows used them, while the connection between this village and the fair's subject remained unclear.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.
