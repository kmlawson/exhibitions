Hanoi Exposition (French and International)

Exposition Française et Internationale

The Exposition Francais et Internationale was a French colonial fair at Hanoi, in French Indochina, held from 16 November 1902 to 15 February 1903 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). Paul Bourgeois, secretary of the French Committee on Foreign Expositions, directed it, and Guillaume Capus was director general (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 387). Its planners meant it to show French colonial prowess and the resources of Southeast Asia (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). Robert W. Rydell reads it as one more link in a chain of colonial fairs that gave "lessons in imperial hubris" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The idea of an international exhibition at Tonkin (now Hanoi) dates from the 1890s, and the Paris Universal Exposition of 1900 gave it new impetus (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). French colonial authorities and the French Committee on Foreign Expositions then decided to hold a fair in French-controlled Southeast Asia. Its aims were to show the economic resources of the region, to examine trade with the colonies of other nations and to underscore French colonial prowess (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). The fair presented the products, agriculture and industries of France's far-eastern colonies (Allwood et al. 2001p. 82).

Organisers and finance

Paul Bourgeois directed the complex (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). The officials appendix of 39 gives him the title secretary of the French Commission on Foreign Expositions and names Guillaume Capus as director general (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 387). The officials appendix of 35 spells the second name Guillame Capus and gives the dates as 1902-1904 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420). Neither encyclopedia entry gives a cost, profit or loss (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). The statistics table of 50 has a row for the fair, but only the figure 228 is legible in the extraction (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Site and buildings

The complex covered 41 acres near the railway station in Hanoi (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). Its buildings were in the beaux-arts style, with pavilions for displays from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). The fair also had galleries of machines and agriculture and a beaux-arts pavilion designed by Roger Marx, which housed displays assembled by nearly 200 French artists (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). The Palais des Beaux-Arts was built specially, on the model of the one at the Paris exposition of 1900 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 82).

Exhibits and attractions

Over 4,000 exhibitors sent exhibits, which the planners grouped under fine arts and sciences, natural resources and machinery (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). The range ran from musical instruments and theatrical art to forest products, perfumes, electrical generators and early airplanes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). Entertainments included cabarets and ethnological parades of indigenous people (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). A Philippine village, organised by the French vice-consul in Manila with the approval of the U.S. government, was among the attractions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170).

Visitors and reception

The official history gives no attendance figures. Reports indicate that attendance sharply declined after opening day, although admission was free (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170). The texts say no more than this about visitors.

Aftermath and legacy

Most of the exhibition buildings and galleries were demolished after the close. The exception was the Grand Palace, which was meant to be a permanent structure housing a colonial museum (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). The texts do not say whether that museum was opened.

Its place in the series

Chronologies list Hanoi 1902-03 after Charleston 1901-02 and before St Louis 1904 (Lawn 2009p. 13). The Allwood, Allan and Reid table lists Tonkin between Turin 1902 and St Louis 1904 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Rydell records that the naturalist Gustavo Niederlein was advising on exhibits for the Hanoi exposition of 1902 when the United States War Department appointed him to William Powell Wilson's staff in the Philippines (Rydell 1984).

How historians have interpreted it

Robert W. Rydell wrote the Hanoi entries in both encyclopedias (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 170; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 177). He sees the fair as "one more link in the chain of colonial fairs" that Western imperial powers stretched across the Third World. He says it "provided lessons in imperial hubris" and, if reports of limited attendance are any indication, colonial resistance (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). His reading rests on the drop in attendance, which the texts do not measure (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). Michael G. Vann, as Hur reports him, argues that the exposition was meant as a metaphor for the whole colonial order of Indo-China but was in fact a disguise masking the chaos of French Indo-China (Hurp. 169). Hur cites Vann in a note to the conclusion of the dissertation and calls the Hanoi exposition a good example of colonial exhibition (Hurp. 169). Rydell reads the fair as evidence of resistance; Vann reads it as a false front (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Hurp. 169).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants from other parts of Asia

The pavilions at Hanoi represented Japan, China, Siam, Korea, Malaya, Formosa and the Philippines (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Allwood et al. 2001p. 82). Hur records one Chinese success. Huang Xiubo won the First Award, and a lieutenant colonel, Chen Jingru, recited a commendatory ode at a banquet to celebrate it (Hurp. 81). Zhongwai ribao printed the ode in March 1903 (Hurp. 81). The ode praised Japan, "a country of the same race and same letter with China", for its active part in exhibitions since 1872 (Hurp. 81). Qingyibao had already reported on the Hanoi fair in November 1901, under the Chinese name Henei Faya Bolanhui (Hurp. 34). The texts name no visitors from other parts of Asia (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176). Hotta-Lister's appendix of international exhibitions, 1873-1910, with expenditure in yen, has an entry for 1902 at Tongking (French territory): an Oriental Agricultural and Technical Ex. of Hanoi, at 11,900 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The book does not say whether this is the Hanoi exposition.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial display — The fair was built to show the resources of French-controlled Southeast Asia and the products of France's far-eastern colonies (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Allwood et al. 2001p. 82).
  • Imperial hubris and resistance — Rydell takes the fair as a lesson in imperial hubris and, from the drop in attendance, as a sign of colonial resistance (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176).
  • Beaux-arts and art — The beaux-arts style, Roger Marx's pavilion of displays by nearly 200 French artists, and the Palais des Beaux-Arts (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Allwood et al. 2001p. 82).
  • Chinese participation and press — Huang Xiubo's award, the ode in Zhongwai ribao, and the Qingyibao article of 1901 (Hurp. 34p. 81).
  • Attendance — Attendance sharply declined after opening day, but no figures are given (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176).
  • Colonial museum — The Grand Palace was meant to house a permanent colonial museum (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176).

Questions for study

  1. Rydell reads the fall in attendance as colonial resistance, but no text gives figures. What other explanations would the evidence allow? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176)
  2. Why did a Chinese ode celebrate a Chinese award at a French colonial fair, and what does that suggest about Chinese views of French exhibitions? (Hurp. 81p. 34)
  3. Vann and Rydell read the same fair as a masked colonial order and as a link in a chain of colonial fairs. Which reading do the attendance reports support better? (Hurp. 169; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176)
  4. The pavilions included Japan, China, Siam, Korea, Malaya, Formosa and the Philippines. Which of these displays showed French reach, and which showed the exhibiting country's own progress? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 176; Allwood et al. 2001p. 82)

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Lawn's introduction ends with a chronology of international exhibitions, and Hanoi 1902-03 appears there as the Exposition Frangais et Internationale. The entry is a title line with no further detail, and the text makes no argument about the fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.
