San Diego · 1915–1916

Panama-California Exposition

The Panama-California Exposition was a regional fair held in San Diego’s Balboa Park in 1915 and 1916 to commemorate the opening of the Panama Canal (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 222; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 227; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). It stayed open for a second season and closed on 31 December 1916 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Its Spanish colonial buildings became the core of Balboa Park, and its promoters chose anthropology rather than technology as the emphasis of the displays (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Historians use it to show how fairs fixed regional memory and carried racial and imperial ideas to the public (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226).

Origins and the rivalry with San Francisco

George Aubrey Davidson first proposed a fair tied to the canal’s opening on 9 July 1909 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 227). The Chamber of Commerce then formed the Panama-California Exposition Corporation to plan it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 222). Rydell links the idea to hopes for higher real-estate values, a larger population, Latin American markets and canal traffic through San Diego’s harbour (Rydell 1984).

New Orleans also sought the fair. At a convention in Santa Barbara in March 1910, Benedict writes, “The San Francisco Chamber of Commerce overwhelmed San Diego’s claims by lobbying every one of the one hundred commercial delegates present” (Benedict 1983p. 79). A settlement followed: San Diego endorsed San Francisco’s international exposition in return for a smaller fair on Latin America and the American Southwest (Benedict 1983p. 80). Federal recognition was harder. The House voted for San Diego in 1911, but a Senate committee killed the measure in January 1912 (Rydell 1984). William Kettner’s bill, which authorised a federal warehouse on the grounds, was signed in May 1913 (Rydell 1984). Allwood, Allan and Reid date the rivalry to 1906, when a bill for a San Francisco international exhibition followed Los Angeles interest in a San Diego celebration (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89). The truce came in 1916, when many San Francisco exhibits went south and the fair was renamed the Panama-California International Exposition (Rydell 1984).

Money, site and politics

The reference works differ on private money. Bokovoy reports that Collier told a House committee in May 1911 that San Diego had raised $2,000,000 privately, and that the city approved a $1,250,000 bond plus $5,000,000 for city and county improvements (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 223). Starr gives private pledges of over $1 million, matched by a city bond of the same size (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 227).

The site was contested. Starr says the Olmsted Brothers wanted the fair tucked into a corner of the park, while business figures including Joseph Sefton, Collier and John D. Spreckels wanted it on Viscaino Mesa. By withholding pledges they drove the fair toward near-bankruptcy, the site was changed, and the Olmsted firm resigned in protest (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 227). Bokovoy gives the same episode another emphasis: Spreckels-led supporters took control of the location, defeated the Olmsted plan and refused to pay the firm (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 224). Fair America puts the fair on four hundred acres of the fourteen-hundred-acre park and says the Olmsteds withdrew after local politicians insisted on a central site (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

An earlier civic plan had already failed. John Nolen’s plan to remake downtown San Diego was defeated by real-estate interests and the coming exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 223).

Architecture and the Spanish colonial city

Early planners expected a plain mission revival style. Bertram Goodhue, who preferred Spanish Colonial to Mission Revival, designed a Mediterranean Renaissance city on the central mesa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 224; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 227). Fair America says he lobbied successfully for an elaborate baroque Spanish-Mexican style, with the main buildings along a single street, the Prado (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Boone argues that the Spanish colonial architecture let visitors imagine a new national history, one that differed from the histories promoted at the 1876 Centennial and the 1893 Columbian Exposition (Boone 2019p. 175). Postcards show visitors on the Prado, the fair’s main promenade, leaving the Foreign and Domestic Arts Building, whose doorway was loosely derived from the Hospital de Santa Cruz in Toledo with its Castilian religious imagery removed (Boone 2019p. 175).

Exhibits and the science of man

Starr describes the theme as the progress and possibilities of the human race, and a permanent building of human-evolution exhibits, assembled with Smithsonian help, formed the core of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Collier hired Edgar L. Hewett to direct the exhibits, promised at least $100,000 for the ethnology and archaeology displays, and pledged a permanent museum in San Diego (Rydell 1984). Fair America dates Hewett’s appointment to 1911 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The physical anthropology displays of Aleš Hrdlička filled five rooms of the Science and Education Building (Rydell 1984). Rydell says they divided humanity into White, Yellow-Brown and Black groups, subdivided into secondary races (Rydell 1984). Hrdlička also defined physical anthropology to include man’s future evolution and its possible regulation (Rydell 1984). Fair America links these exhibits to the eugenics displays at San Francisco (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The midway, called the Isthmus, followed the same anthropological themes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Its Painted Desert, a ten-acre Indian village with about 100 Southwestern Indians, was financed by the Santa Fe Railway and stood opposite International Harvester’s Model Farm (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 224; Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Mička and Riley Moore of the National Museum studied the Eskimos and the Sioux (Rydell 1984). Voltjetch Schuck travelled to Africa to photograph and measure Pygmies, Zulus and Bushmen, and was arrested in British East Africa when the war began (Rydell 1984). Carlos Vierra's frescoes of ancient American cities and Donald Beauregard's Catholic paintings filled the Indian Arts and Fine Arts buildings, and the Malvina Hoffman bronze busts of the world's peoples were shown in pairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 225; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 225). William Holmes made sculptures from Vierra's murals of the temple cities of Quirigua, Copan, Palenque, Tikal, Uxmal and Chichén Itza (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226).

Spanish fantasy heritage and the peoples on display

Helen Hunt Jackson’s novel Ramona helped promote what Carey McWilliams called a Spanish fantasy heritage (Boone 2019p. 168). The groundbreaking festival of July 1911 linked New Spain to the twentieth century, and its pageant moved from Aztec sacrifice through Cortés and Cabrillo to the raising of the US flag (Boone 2019p. 168; Boone 2019p. 170). Boone quotes Phoebe Kropp’s view that Anglo-Americans were inheriting the conquistadors’ mantle (Boone 2019p. 170).

Mexican workers were the fair’s unseen labour. Four men who had worked as gardeners in Balboa Park returned to a ranch in Jalisco after the fair (Boone 2019p. 155). Boone writes that “Mexicans, many of whom worked as gardeners and construction workers in San Diego, provided the invisible labor that made the California fairs possible” (Boone 2019p. 184). Mexican migrants were not in the fair’s plans, which took shape as the Mexican Revolution began in 1910 (Boone 2019p. 168).

Other displays tell a similar story. The Liberty Bell visited for three days in November 1915 but was silent because a crack prevented it from being struck (Boone 2019p. 181; Boone 2019p. 182).

The Spanish heritage theme reached real Californios as well as visitors. José Guadalupe Estudillo had sat on the San Diego board that voted in 1868 to set aside fourteen hundred acres for what became Balboa Park, and the fair held Estudillo Day on 26 May 1915 (Boone 2019p. 176; Boone 2019p. 176). The Estudillo house in Old Town, restored by Hazel Wood Waterman, had reopened in 1910 as Ramona's marriage place (Boone 2019p. 176). On the Isthmus, the actor Thomas P. Getz charged ten cents to see a mission play (Boone 2019p. 180). Indigenous performers were also on display. Anglo fairgoers could "play Indian" for an evening at a costume carnival in August 1915, and the composer Charles Wakefield Cadman brought the Cherokee singer Princess Tsianina Redfeather to perform (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226). Rydell's notes cite a San Diego Union item of 28 August 1915 on dressing for the Indian costume evening (Rydell 1984). The Liberty Bell's three-day visit in November 1915 was much quieter than its San Francisco welcome (Boone 2019p. 181). The tea room that the Daughters of the American Revolution ran in the Indian Arts Building was largely ignored (Boone 2019p. 181), and marketers reissued San Francisco images of the bell for San Diego (Boone 2019p. 181).

Aftermath and legacy

Starr says the fair probably cost about $5 million, ended with a profit of unknown size, and was attended by 3,747,916 people (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229). Bokovoy gives the same attendance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226); the statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives 3,800,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Most of the Spanish colonial buildings were preserved over Goodhue’s objections and became the core of Balboa Park (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229). The San Diego Zoo and the Museum of Man grew out of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 41). Rydell notes that the California Building was given to the San Diego Museum Association as display space for the fair’s ethnological and archaeological collections (Rydell 1984). Hispanic architecture came to predominate in the town (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229). Benedict argues that the San Diego fair had far more influence on architecture than the San Francisco fair, popularising the Churrigueresque style into the 1920s (Benedict 1983p. 108).

Gardens and planting

The fair was built as a garden as much as a city. Starr says the Spanish city's concept rested on extensive landscaping to create a garden atmosphere, and that over two million plants were put in before the opening (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Visitors were encouraged to use the lawns: the fair had a firm policy against signs forbidding them to walk on the grass (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Boone records the organisers' promise in 1911 that the fair would be a floral and horticultural wonder (Boone 2019p. 189). Their landscape gardeners reported that over fifty thousand trees and shrubs had been placed in 1912 alone, which they called only a small part of the plan (Boone 2019p. 189). The two counts measure different things. Maloney notes that San Diego's fair, like Philadelphia's Centennial of 1876, included permanent structures for horticulture (Maloney 2012p. 218).

Workers' bunkhouses and the Mexican gardeners

Boone describes the Mexican gardeners as among the most recent arrivals in California, temporary workers whose status was insecure and whose presence older residents regarded with ambivalence (Boone 2019p. 188). Their housing was apart from the fair. About a hundred small bunkhouses, with four people to each house, stood in the middle of the park, far from downtown (Boone 2019p. 188). Workers who rented them bought their meals at a large segregated restaurant built next door (Boone 2019p. 189). An economical observer approved of the arrangement: "A shifting force means loss of efficiency in the organization and loss of efficiency means increased cost" (Boone 2019p. 189). The bunkhouses, the restaurant and a hospital for injured workers were paid for from fees that the workers themselves paid (Boone 2019p. 189). Crossings from Mexico into the United States rose from 49,500 in 1901–1910 to 219,000 in 1911–1920 (Boone 2019p. 189). Textual evidence about these workers is scarce, and Ralph Stineman's photographs of 1910 to 1915 are among the few traces left (Boone 2019p. 189).

The fair's directors and their allies

Collier enlisted Charles F. Lummis, librarian, amateur ethnologist and founder of the Southwest Museum in Los Angeles, for publicity and organisation (Rydell 1984). Lummis told Collier that "you have mixed Science with business so splendidly that it becomes really a man’s game" (Rydell 1984). Edgar Hewett, the director of exhibits, also spoke at the Race Betterment Congress in San Francisco. There he said that Mexicans "imperil in some measure the health of the race in its onward march" (Rydell 1984). Set beside Boone's account of Mexican labour, the remark shows how one of the fair's directors regarded the people who built the park. Joseph Thompson, a United States naval surgeon and Hrdlička's assistant, installed the anthropology exhibits in three buildings (Rydell 1984).

How historians have interpreted it

Starr treats the fair as one of the major factors shaping San Diego’s identity, and sees its architecture as fixing the city’s Hispanic past (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229). Bokovoy reads the fair as an invention of tradition: its Spanish fantasy-heritage theme created a modern regional tradition, set in the Indian and Spanish past (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226). Rydell argues that fairs preserved faith in progress while promoting racism and imperialism, and that the Panama-California Exposition gave the public the evolutionary racial doctrines of Hrdlička (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle present the fair as an attempt to reinvent San Diego’s history (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Boone argues that Spain’s erasure and reimagining made it hard for the United States to embrace its Spanish-speaking population (Boone 2019p. 157; Boone 2019p. 188).

The readings agree that the Spanish architecture was the fair’s most lasting feature (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229; Benedict 1983p. 108), and that its displays of race were ideological (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They differ over the anthropology. Bokovoy says Hrdlička and Holmes found racial distinctions uncomfortable and arbitrary, and undertook expeditions to counter them, but that the message was lost on the average tourist (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 225; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226). Rydell reads the same displays as teaching a racial hierarchy with eugenic overtones (Rydell 1984).

The sources also disagree on scale. Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the fair as hardly international in scope, yet also as a second international exhibition in California in 1915 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 94; Allwood et al. 2001p. 89). Starr notes that, as the fair was not a certified international one, foreign governments had no pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Greenhalgh places the San Diego fair with San Francisco among the two most aesthetically resolved American exhibitions, and sets its picturesque planning against the monumental fairs of Chicago, St Louis and Buffalo (Greenhalgh 1988p. 46; Greenhalgh 1988p. 47).

The Isthmus and the peoples of Asia

The Isthmus, the San Diego midway, presented Japanese and Chinese people among others as descendants of prehistoric forebears, illustrating stages of progress. Rydell names the Japanese living in the Oriental Joy Garden, the Chinese in Underground Chinatown, and Singhalese working on Sir Thomas Lipton’s model tea plantation, alongside Afro-Americans and Native Americans. Rydell reports that the fair’s directors planned these displays (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Starr lists the same scenes: Japanese in a garden, Chinese in an underground Chinatown, and Singhalese working on a tea plantation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). Bokovoy lists the underground Chinatown among the fair’s entertainments (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226). Fair America says the Japanese Streets of Joy, Chinatown and the Painted Desert reinforced the racial stereotypes shown in the anthropology exhibits (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell describes ethnological slumming along the Isthmus (Rydell 1984).

The texts do not say who financed or ran the Japanese, Chinese or Singhalese displays. Starr says the fair was not a certified international one, so foreign governments had no pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). The texts give no sign of official Japanese, Chinese or other Asian government participation at San Diego.

Chinese participants and the 1914 regulation

Peterson’s note cites a Department of Labor circular of 25 March 1914 that regulated the admission and return of Chinese participants in both the Panama-Pacific and the Panama-California expositions (Peterson 2020p. 93). The text describes a special federal regulation governing the entry, exit and movement of such participants, intended to stop them taking American jobs (Peterson 2020p. 93). Peterson’s discussion of Chinese government exhibits concerns San Francisco (Peterson 2020p. 93). The texts do not describe the Chinese participants at San Diego.

Anthropological collecting in Asia

Hrdlička explored Siberia, Mongolia and Peru to gather material for the exhibits (Rydell 1984). Hrdlička and other anthropologists travelled around Europe and Asia in search of skeletal evidence of racial types for the fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell says Philip Newton went to the Philippines to study the Negritos and surreptitiously desecrated graves in search of skeletal material for the fair (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the anthropology exhibits won praise from lay visitors and scientists (Rydell 1984), but does not say which peoples were shown in the Yellow-Brown group (Rydell 1984). The texts say nothing about how Asian visitors, commissioners or observers responded to the San Diego fair.

Private exhibitors on the Isthmus

Starr says the fair was not a certified international exhibition, so foreign governments had no pavilions. Its foreign displays came from private organisations and firms instead (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228). The texts do not name the firms or people who ran the Japanese, Chinese or Singhalese scenes. In San Francisco, the directors closed one part of the Chinese Village, the Underground Chinatown concession, after objections from the local Chinese business community and the Chinese consul (Rydell 1984). None of the San Diego texts says whether the Isthmus Chinatown faced the same protest.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Spanish fantasy heritage — The Spanish colonial architecture and pageantry are read as a romantic version of California’s past that set Mexico aside, and both Boone and Bokovoy treat it as the fair’s central idea (Boone 2019p. 168; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 226).
  • Anthropology and race science — The physical anthropology displays were the centre of the exhibits; Rydell sees them teaching racial hierarchy and eugenics, while Bokovoy stresses the anti-racist aims of Hrdlička and Holmes (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 225).
  • The midway and peoples on display — The Isthmus staged Japanese, Chinese, Singhalese, Afro-American and Native American scenes, which scholars link to racial stereotypes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 228; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Mexican labour and erasure — Boone places Mexican gardeners and workers behind the fair’s Spanish scenery, outside its plans (Boone 2019p. 184; Boone 2019p. 168).
  • Rivalry with San Francisco — The fair took shape in a contest with San Francisco that ended in a truce and an exchange of exhibits (Benedict 1983p. 79; Rydell 1984).
  • Architecture and the Balboa Park legacy — The Spanish colonial buildings outlasted the fair and became the core of Balboa Park (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 229; Benedict 1983p. 108).

Boone, M. Elizabeth. “The Spanish Element in Our Nationality”: Spain and America at the World’s Fairs and Centennial Celebrations, 1876–1915. University Park: Pennsylvania State University Press, 2019.
Introductionp. 1

Boone opens with Walt Whitman's 1883 insistence that Spanish character must be recognised in American identity, and argues that an English-only national story has erased Spain from United States history. The book examines Spain's presence at five celebrations: Philadelphia 1876, Chicago 1893, San Francisco and San Diego in 1915, the Barcelona and Paris fairs of 1888 and 1889, and the 1910 centenaries in Buenos Aires, Mexico City and Santiago. Using paintings, ephemeral architecture, posters and press responses, it asks why Spain was displayed, refused or misread at each event, and how these displays helped shape national identities across the Americas. Boone draws on theories of exhibition, memory and transnational study, treating fairs as exercises in using the past to imagine a national future. The introduction ends by considering present-day consequences, including the contested reputations of Junípero Serra and Bernardo de Gálvez and the large Spanish-speaking population of the United States.

5Using Spain to Ignore Mexicans at the 1915 California Fairsp. 155

Boone opens with a 1915 photograph of four Mexican gardeners posed in suits before a painted backdrop reading 'Meet Me in San Diego', and uses it to ask how Spanishness in the United States spoke to Mexican migrants. The chapter compares the two California fairs of 1915. In San Francisco, the Panama-Pacific International Exposition went ahead without a Spanish government pavilion, despite years of lobbying by local boosters; Spain's presence was reduced to past conquistadors in sculpture and a generalized Mediterranean style. In San Diego, the Panama-California Exposition built a Spanish colonial city, drawing on the Portola Festivals, the novel Ramona, the restored Estudillo House and mission bells. Boone reads postcards, photographs and newspapers closely, showing how visitors were pictured inside the architecture and how the Mexican workers who built the park remain nearly invisible. The chapter concludes that erasing Spain's modern role left the United States unable to embrace its Spanish-speaking population, and it argues for recognising Spanishness in all its complexity.

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
8The Expositions in San Francisco and San Diego: Toward the World of TomorrowAsia

Rydell examines the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco and the Panama-California Exposition in San Diego, both of 1915, which celebrated the opening of the Panama Canal. Rydell argues that the two fairs staged a racial history of the American West in which white conquest appeared as natural progress, and that their wealthy promoters used this message to present an orderly, racially ranked future. The chapter reconstructs the rivalry between the two cities and the federal politics that gave San Diego only a narrower recognition. Its central evidence is San Diego's physical anthropology display, assembled with Smithsonian anthropologists, whose busts and skulls ranked peoples within an evolutionary and eugenic framework. Rydell then turns to San Francisco's Race Betterment congress and to the Joy Zone and Isthmus concessions, where Mexican, Samoan, African, Chinese, Japanese and Native American villages were staged as entertainment. The chapter closes with the fairs' legacy in Balboa Park and Forest Lawn cemetery, where the vision outlived the exhibitions.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
2Fairs of the Imperial Erap. 45Asia

Rydell, Findling and Pelle examine how American fairs from 1898 to 1915 presented the new overseas empire as natural and the racial hierarchy that justified it. They begin with the Trans-Mississippi Exposition in Omaha, where an Indian Congress, staged battles and an Old Plantation Village taught visitors about conquest and slavery, and move to Buffalo in 1901, where a color scheme ranked races from dark to light and a Filipino village cast American rule as uplift. St. Louis in 1904 receives the most space, for its anthropology displays and the Olympic events held alongside the fair. The authors then cover the Portland and Seattle fairs, which relied on Igorot and Eskimo villages, and the 1915 fairs in San Francisco, with its eugenics congresses, and San Diego, which reinvented the city's Spanish past and displayed human evolution. The evidence comes from fair records, newspapers and visitors' recollections. The chapter argues that these fairs were designed to show the desirability of colonialism and to justify racial hierarchy, and it closes by noting that the war which followed would reshape the next generation of fairs.

8The Louisiana Purchase Exposition and Two Great Firesp. 83

Allwood sets two American fairs against the difficulties of organizing them. The St Louis Louisiana Purchase Exposition of 1904 is described as bigger than any previous exhibition, spread over 1,272 acres with a walk of thirty-five miles around it. Its white Ivory City, the Festival Hall and the Pike amusement area are compared with Chicago, and the automobile, wireless and aeronautics displays are used to argue that St Louis excelled Chicago in showing science in motion. Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
A Twenty-Five-Million-Dollar MirageMarjorie M. Dobkinp. 66

Dobkin argues that the Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915 was an attempt to restore confidence in San Francisco after the 1906 earthquake, fire and graft trials, and that its promised permanent improvements were dropped as business and political interests pulled apart. She traces the fair from Reuben Hale's 1904 proposal and the failed Burnham plan, through the split between reform leaders such as James Phelan and the business elite who formed the Board of Directors, which excluded the prosecutors. The main case study is site selection: Golden Gate Park, Lincoln Park and a composite plan were each argued for until Charles Moore's support secured Harbor View. Dobkin also covers the rivalry with New Orleans and San Diego for federal recognition, the Board's opposition to Governor Johnson's anti-alien land acts, the labour truce with unions, and a publicity campaign built on projected investment. Her conclusion is that the economic claims were inflated, that the fair cost about half of what had been predicted, and that the First World War, not the exposition, delivered the prosperity its sponsors wanted.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Boone follows Spain's presence at a sequence of international expositions and national centenaries between 1876 and 1915: Philadelphia 1876, Barcelona and Paris in 1888 and 1889, Chicago 1893, the 1910 centenaries in Buenos Aires, Santiago de Chile and Mexico City, and the San Francisco and San Diego fairs of 1915. Its argument is that these displays helped define national identities in the United States and Latin America, and that Spain was repeatedly refused, misread or pushed into the past, so that its modern role and its links to the Americas were forgotten. Using paintings, ephemeral architecture, posters, postcards and press responses, the chapters proceed chronologically, each a case study of one fair or pair of fairs. Wikipedia

The Panama–California Exposition was a world exposition held in San Diego, California, between January 1, 1915, and January 1, 1917. The exposition celebrated the opening of the Panama Canal, and was meant to tout San Diego as the first United States port of call for ships traveling north after passing through the canal into the Pacific Ocean. The fair was held in San Diego's large urban Balboa Park. The park held a second Panama-California exposition in 1935.
