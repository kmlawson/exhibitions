Where the reference works list it

Both reference works give the exhibition one line in an appendix of fairs excluded from the main body. In the 2008 Encyclopedia the line is on printed page 427, between the 1932 Bucharest International Children’s Exposition and the 1934 Oporto exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427). In the 1990 Historical Dictionary it is on printed page 402, between the same two entries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402). The introduction says the appendix lists fairs that fell short of the qualifications for the main body, many of them specialised in nature and others without a suitable international scope (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). Neither entry gives a reason for the exclusion of this fair.

Dates, organisers and status

Both texts give the name as Women’s and Children’s International Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402). The appendix gives names in the host country’s language unless that language is not a common Western tongue (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). The entry therefore has only an English title, and no Japanese title is given. Neither text gives dates, a venue, organisers, exhibits or attendance, and neither says the exhibition opened or was cancelled.

Other Tokyo fairs in these books are placed differently. A fair planned for Tokyo in 1912 and postponed to 1917 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404), and a Tokyo 1940 entry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405), appear in Appendix E, the list of fairs planned but never held (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 403; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 403). The 1933 exhibition appears in Appendix D instead (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402). That placement is the only sign in the texts that it took place.

Its place in the series

The texts connect it with no other exhibition. In both books it sits between the 1932 Bucharest International Children’s Exposition and the 1934 Oporto exposition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 402). The Bucharest title also names children, but neither text says whether the two events were related.

  • Exclusion from the main reference works — both books list it only in an appendix of fairs left out of the main body, and give no account of its content (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395).

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 4

Shepherdson-Scott, Kari. “Conflicting Politics and Contesting Borders: Exhibiting (Japanese) Manchuria at the Chicago World’s Fair, 1933–34.” Journal article.
Introductionp. 539Asia

Shepherdson-Scott introduces a small Manchuria pavilion at Chicago's 1933-34 fair, A Century of Progress, built on the Japanese exhibition grounds months after the Kwantung Army had created Manchukuo and Japan had left the League of Nations. The argument is that this modest building, which drew only about two percent of fairgoers, carried great diplomatic weight in a bid to sway American opinion. The paper traces how the Japanese government and army, the Manchukuo government and the South Manchuria Railway Company (Mantetsu) shaped the pavilion's sponsorship and name, and how Mantetsu's role let the exhibit present Manchuria as an economic project rather than a political one. The introduction then previews the paper's three parts: the building and its displays, the Japanese bargaining over how Manchuria should appear, and the American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol, which contested Manchukuo's borders.

In the contents of this work

MANCHUKUO: CHILD OF CONFLICTp. 542Asia

Shepherdson-Scott sets the Manchuria pavilion against the diplomatic crisis of 1931 to 1933. The chapter reviews the Mukden Incident, the founding of Manchukuo in February 1932, the Stimson Doctrine, the Lytton Commission's report and Japan's withdrawal from the League of Nations, and shows that few powers had recognized the new state by the time the fair opened in May 1933. It then turns to the decisions behind the exhibit. Japan had first declined to exhibit, changed course when China planned a display, and saw a film on Manchukuo rejected for American screening. Mantetsu and the Manchukuo government funded the pavilion, which was named the South Manchuria Railway Building and attached to the Japan pavilion. Shepherdson-Scott describes Mantetsu's role as a masterstroke: as the company that dominated the region, it let the exhibit promote development and Open Door trade without foregrounding the new state's sovereignty.

In the contents of this work

CONTESTED BOUNDARIES: THE GOLDEN TEMPLE OF JEHOLp. 557Asia

Shepherdson-Scott turns to the Golden Temple of Jehol, a replica of the Potala of Jehol sponsored by the Chicago industrialist Vincent Bendix and placed where the roads to the Japan and Chinese exhibits met. Built in Beijing by Chinese craftsmen, with plans prepared by the architect W. H. Liang on the advice of the explorer Sven Hedin, the temple took on an aura of authenticity that the fair exploited. Its gilded interior made it one of the premier attractions, and the official guidebook gave it far more pages than the Manchuria pavilion. Shepherdson-Scott argues that Bendix cast himself as a conservator of Sino-Mongolian culture, challenging Japan's claim to be the patriarchal guide of East Asia. The temple's location and its politics, sharpened by the Kwantung Army's occupation of Jehol in May 1933, contested the borders that the Manchuria displays had drawn. The section ends by noting that the official View Book of the fair removed the Manchuria exhibit altogether.

In the contents of this work

CONCLUSIONp. 561Asia

Shepherdson-Scott concludes that the Manchuria exhibits could not sell a single, settled idea of Manchuria at Chicago in 1933. The official literature never used the name Manchukuo, and the Japanese displays were undermined by the American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol and by the fair's own catalogues. The geopolitical idea of Manchuria was therefore fluid, held between multiple perspectives and interests in Chicago and beyond.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
