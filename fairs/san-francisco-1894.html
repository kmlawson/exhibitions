San Francisco · 1894

California Midwinter International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The California Midwinter International Exposition, known as the Midwinter Fair, was San Francisco's first world's fair. It opened in Golden Gate Park on 27 January 1894 and ran for about five months (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Michael H. de Young, a newspaper publisher who had been California's commissioner at the Chicago fair of 1893, organised it to promote the city and California's mild winter (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001). The texts treat it as a civic booster event, an imitation of the Chicago fair, a site for displays of non-European peoples, and a precedent for the 1915 Panama-Pacific fair (Rydell 1984; Benedict 1983; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Michael H. de Young had been California's commissioner at the Chicago fair of 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001). Returning home, he found San Francisco in a severe recession, with failed banks and labour conflict, and he took up the idea of a fair of its own (Findling & Pelle 2008). He called his scheme the Sunset City (Findling & Pelle 2008). Holding it in midwinter was meant to set California's mild season against the snow- and ice-bound rest of the country (Findling & Pelle 1990). The grounds opened on 27 January 1894, although de Young had first hoped for 1 January (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990). At the close, he claimed: "There is a complete restoration of confidence; business is progressing as of yore" (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Site and architecture

The fair occupied 160 acres in Golden Gate Park. The site was chosen in July 1893, and ground was broken on 24 August (Findling & Pelle 1990). Park superintendent John McLaren opposed the scheme, and a critic named Stow denounced it; de Young answered: "What is a tree? What are a thousand trees compared to the benefits of the exposition?" (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid place the fair in Piedmont Park, near the Golden Gate (Allwood et al. 2001), while the other texts name Golden Gate Park (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990).

De Young banned classical references from the main buildings, and local architects produced eclectic designs with a colour scheme drawn from the Pacific evening sky (Findling & Pelle 2008). The dictionary describes a local competition that produced Romanesque, Moorish, Renaissance and Egyptian buildings, with five principal buildings planned round a quadrangle (Findling & Pelle 1990). The central tower's height is disputed. The encyclopedia gives a 272-foot steel tower by the Frenchman Leopold Bonet, modelled on the Eiffel Tower and with more spectacular lighting (Findling & Pelle 2008). The dictionary and Fair America give 266 feet (Findling & Pelle 1990; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Exhibits and attractions

Much of the display came from Chicago. Many exhibits were shipped west and installed there (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Boone 2019), and horticultural displays from Chicago went to San Francisco (Maloney 2012). Exhibitors could keep their goods on the West Coast for another six months (Findling & Pelle 2008). The amusement section, called the Midway as at Chicago, held the Haunted Swing, where visitors seemed to swing on a stationary seat inside a revolving room (Findling & Pelle 2008). Other attractions included a Ferris Wheel, a reconstruction of a '49 mining camp and an elephant of walnuts from Los Angeles County (Allwood et al. 2001), and a knight of prunes from Santa Clara County (Benedict 1983).

The Fine Arts Building, an Egyptian-style temple, held foreign paintings, particularly from France and Poland. There, according to the dictionary, "impressionist paintings by Auguste Renoir and Edouard Manet were the first to be publicly exhibited in the West" (Findling & Pelle 1990). Maloney says the fair added the Japanese Tea Garden to Golden Gate Park, though it was a minor fair (Maloney 2012). Fair America calls the display "an intriguing mix of technological and ethnological exhibits in a garden setting" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Peoples on display

Rydell reads the Japanese Village as placing Japanese visitors in a subordinate role. The Japanese were portrayed as cousins of the Chinese, and the racial attitudes visible at Chicago became explicit at San Francisco (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia records a Japanese delegation's objection to plans for local Japanese to pull visitors in jinrikishas; the crisis was averted by hiring Germans in Oriental costume (Findling & Pelle 2008). It also notes that an Eskimo child died in the fair's Esquimaux village and was buried in Golden Gate Park without officials' knowledge (Findling & Pelle 2008). Peterson's footnote mentions the Japanese showman Kushibiki, who worked on the Midwinter Fair, where many San Franciscans first saw the Japanese Garden (Peterson 2020). The texts say little about how the performers themselves experienced these displays.

Visitors, labour and controversy

The publicity department had eight members, who produced about 10,000 words a day. Some 2,462 publications worldwide carried articles about the fair, none of them paid advertisements (Findling & Pelle 2008). Attendance figures differ: the dictionary gives 1,355,889 patrons (Findling & Pelle 1990), Fair America nearly 1.4 million (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and Maloney's footnote just over one million (Maloney 2012). The Great Exhibitions table adds a further 119,059 visitors before and after the fair, but its main figure is garbled in the extract (Allwood et al. 2001).

Labour critics said business leaders were moving workers from other projects into temporary fairground jobs rather than creating permanent work (Findling & Pelle 2008). The Woman's Congress criticised the absence of women from the planning committees (Findling & Pelle 2008). A scandal over a planned nude dancer at the aquarium led to arrests (Findling & Pelle 2008), and the directors suspected vendors of under-reporting receipts and set rotating clerks to collect them (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Money, closing and legacy

The encyclopedia gives revenues of $1,260,112.19 against expenditures of $1,193,260.70, a net profit of $66,851.49, and no government money (Findling & Pelle 2008). The dictionary gives the same profit (Findling & Pelle 1990); the Great Exhibitions table gives $32,464 (Allwood et al. 2001). Fair America calls the profit modest (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The closing dates differ too: 4 July 1894 in the encyclopedia (Findling & Pelle 2008) and 30 June in the dictionary (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Afterwards, the Fine Arts Building stayed in the park as the city's first municipal museum, and the Japanese Tea Garden remained (Findling & Pelle 2008). Maloney says the garden was preserved for the public and inspired estate owners to build their own Japanese gardens (Maloney 2012). A building boom followed, producing the Emporium, the new Cliff House, the Sutro Baths and the Ferry Building (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The fair's influence on later exhibitions is also a theme. Fair America says it proved that a western fair could succeed and inspired planners in Portland, Seattle, San Francisco and San Diego (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell traces the origin of the Portland fair to it (Rydell 1984). Benedict says de Young conceived the 1915 Panama-Pacific fair as a grand version of this one (Benedict 1983). Boone reports that the 1906 earthquake and fire ended plans for a follow-up, and the city held the Portola Festival instead, commemorating Gaspar de Portolá's 1769 arrival (Boone 2019).

How historians have interpreted it

The reference works and Fair America take a broadly favourable, civic view. The encyclopedia entry by Chandler and Nathan is the fullest, presenting the fair as de Young's bid to promote San Francisco and make money for local business, while setting out its disputes (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rydell, Findling and Pelle place it among the fairs of 1876 to 1897 that marked the end of an era, stressing its promotion of California's climate and its modest profit (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Rydell argues that the fair belongs to a larger American vision of empire and race, and in these passages the Japanese Village is his example (Rydell 1984). Boone argues that Spain was absent from the Panama-Pacific fair, and he treats the 1894 fair only as background, a precedent organised by de Young (Boone 2019). Benedict argues that fairs used sheer quantity to impress visitors, and his examples from 1894 are a prune knight and a walnut elephant (Benedict 1983). Maloney treats the fair as part of garden history, and Peterson mentions it only in a footnote (Maloney 2012; Peterson 2020).

The texts agree that de Young ran the fair and that it grew out of Chicago. They disagree on the profit, the closing date, the tower's height and the park's name, and the notes do not settle these conflicts. The dictionary's attendance is the most precise figure, but the encyclopedia's statistics table could not be read in the extract used here.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

The Japanese presence is the one the texts describe. Wilson's dictionary entry lists "a Japanese tea garden" among the fair's important exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 135). Maloney says the fair "added" the Japanese Tea Garden to Golden Gate Park (Maloney 2012p. 134), and that the garden was afterwards preserved for the public there (Maloney 2012p. 81). The encyclopedia entry by Chandler and Nathan says the garden remained in the park after the fair closed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 132). None of the texts says who built the garden or who paid for it.

Peterson, in a footnote, lists among the achievements of the Japanese showman Kushibiki "the 1894 Midwinter Fair... in Golden Gate Park, where many San Franciscans first experienced the Japanese Garden" (Peterson 2020p. 61). Peterson gives no further detail of Kushibiki's work at the fair.

Japanese delegation and performers

The encyclopedia records that a delegation from Japan objected to a plan for local Japanese to pull visitors around the grounds in jinrikishas. The delegation vowed openly that it would kill any Japanese found degrading the honourable reputation of the nation by such menial tasks. The organisers averted the crisis by hiring Germans, who painted their faces and dressed in Oriental costume (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 131). The texts do not say who sent the delegation, or how the local Japanese themselves responded.

China

Wilson's dictionary entry also lists "a Chinese pagoda" among the fair's exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 135). The texts give no further account of Chinese displays, performers or visitors at this fair. Rydell's passages on Chinese theatre and performers concern the Midway Plaisance of the Chicago fair (Rydell 1984), not San Francisco.

Scholars' readings

Rydell places the San Francisco Japanese Village within a racial order. Visitors were invited to view "part and parcel of the home life of the little brown men," and the Japanese were portrayed as "cousins" of the Chinese. Rydell says that the nuance seen at the Columbian Exposition "became explicit" at this fair, and that the possibility of Japanese becoming full citizens of the future City Beautiful was "increasingly problematic" (Rydell 1984). The texts offer no Japanese or Chinese account of how these displays were received.

Maloney reads the garden as a legacy. It inspired many estate owners in the area to install Japanese gardens on their own grounds (Maloney 2012p. 153). The encyclopedia treats the protest as one of the fair's disputes, next to the labour criticism and the scandal over the aquarium (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 131).

The encyclopedia says the fair drew participants from 38 nations, but it does not name them (Findling & Pelle 2008). On the evidence of these texts, the Asian presence rests on Japan and China alone.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Chicago's shadow — The texts see the fair as de Young's answer to the Columbian exposition, with its exhibits, its promoter and its model of display (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Site and architecture — Golden Gate Park, the mixed styles and the tower are the physical centre of the texts, though they disagree on the tower's height (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Peoples on display — Japanese, Samoan and Eskimo displays appear in several texts, mainly as incidents or as examples of racial framing (Rydell 1984; Peterson 2020; Rydell 1993; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Labour, women and scandal — The encyclopedia alone records these disputes in detail (Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Profit and figures — Profit, attendance and closing date are the most contested facts across the reference works (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).
  • Legacy for later fairs — Later western fairs, the 1915 fair and the Portola Festival are presented as successors (Rydell 1984; Benedict 1983; Boone 2019; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Questions for study

  1. Why do the reference works disagree on the closing date and the tower's height, and which evidence would a student check first?
  2. If the Japanese Village placed Japanese visitors in a subordinate role, what would the performers' own account look like, and do these texts offer it?
  3. Was the profit the right measure of the fair's success for San Francisco, or do the labour and social disputes change that judgement?
  4. How far did the fair's Chicago origins shape its architecture and exhibits, and do the texts agree on that?
  5. Why do several texts mention the fair only in passing, and what does that say about its place in the history of world's fairs?

On the web

Wikipedia

The California Midwinter International Exposition of 1894, commonly referred to as the "Midwinter Exposition" or the "Midwinter Fair", was a World's Fair that officially operated from January 27 to July 5 in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park. In 1892, U.S. President Benjamin Harrison appointed Michael H. de Young as a national commissioner to the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition held in Chicago. During the exposition in Chicago, de Young recognized an opportunity to stimulate California's economy in its time of depression. In the summer of 1893, de Young announced his plans for the California Midwinter International Exposition to be held in Golden Gate Park. One of the draws, according to de Young, was California's weather, which would allow for a fair in the middle of winter.
