Expo 67 (Universal and International Exhibition of 1967)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

Expo 67, the Universal and International Exhibition of 1967, ran at Montreal from April to October 1967 on islands in the St Lawrence River (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). It was Canada's centenary world's fair. The Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), the body that authorised international fairs, awarded it to Montreal in November 1962 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 180). Riar calls it a turning point (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Its theme, Man and His World, and its passport admission ticket drew wide attention, and with Paris 1900 it was one of the two fairs to pass 50 million visits (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334).

Origins and aims

Canada's first bid lost to Moscow. Prime Minister John Diefenbaker endorsed the bid in February 1960, and when Moscow withdrew two years later for financial reasons, Canada bid again (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335). The BIE awarded Montreal the fair on 13 November 1962, the third exhibition of its first category and the first held in North America (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The idea had been raised at Brussels in 1958, when a senator proposed an international exhibition for Canada's centenary and Mayor Jean Drapeau backed it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137). The theme came from Saint-Exupéry's Terre des Hommes (Findling & Pelle 2008). Bennett finds the exclusive title jarring for modern readers, but calls it "the language of an era" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Allwood, Allan and Reid place 1967 as a watershed between a conservative postwar Canada and the livelier 1960s (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137).

Organisation and finance

Montreal's organisers learned from the New York World's Fair of 1964-65. Meeting a week after New York closed, they insisted on official BIE endorsement (Samuel 2007) and chose a theme they judged more achievable: "The official theme of the exhibition, Man and His World, may have been less ambitious than New York’s theme of Peace Through Understanding, but it was certainly more achievable." (Samuel 2007). Many corporate exhibitors that had overspent in New York stayed away (Samuel 2007).

Money came from the city, the province and the federal government. Allwood, Allan and Reid say Montreal had no hunt for foreign participants and no grave financial problems, and its planners used a computer critical-path method, which orders tasks by the sequence they depend on (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138). The Expo-Express cost $18.3 million, included in the admission price (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The reference tables record a loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 209; Roche 2000p. 43).

The pavilions were not grouped as private or international (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 330). Riar argues that the planned "Man in the City" master plan, a megastructure or large flexible framework for city life, was never achieved, and that the fair was "largely atomized by national, cultural, and corporate pavilions" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258).

Site, transport and architecture

The fair covered 988 acres, much of it new land made from 17.6 million cubic yards of fill (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The Expo-Express carried about 44 million passengers (Findling & Pelle 2008). Moshe Safdie's Habitat 67, 354 precast concrete boxes forming 158 apartments, cost $20 million according to one reference work and $21 million according to another (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 330; Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe its production-line construction as troubled (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138).

Buckminster Fuller's United States dome, 250 feet across, was the most popular and most widely recognised pavilion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 331; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 339). Rain leaked through it and its sun blinds jammed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 139). Man the Producer, a space frame of tetrahedral steel cells, drew attention, but the architecture press looked at its frame and largely ignored the exhibits inside (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 260p. 266).

Allwood, Allan and Reid give a different history of Habitat 67's cost. The original estimate was $40 million. The federal government cut the project to one-sixth of its size and fixed a ceiling of $11.5 million, but the building finally cost $22 million, mostly because the method of construction needed as much equipment and expertise for the 158 units as the larger first scheme would have (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138). This runs against the $20 million and $21 million figures given above. The interiors fared worse: many were designed under the auspices of Canadian popular women's magazines and were filled with some of the worst examples of North American interior design (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138).

Caramellino, De Magistris and Deambrosis place the fair in the longer history of the island. The plan directeur tied Expo to a new administrative centre on Ile Sainte-Hélène, and the fair was used to advance the Underground of Montreal, the extension of Ile Sainte-Hélène, the creation of Ile Notre-Dame, and new bridges and transport such as the Expo-Express (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 318). The same pattern returned for the 1976 Olympic Games and fed a culture of management across Canadian public administration (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 318).

Pavilions, attractions and nations on show

Benedict says the fair avoided a single focal building and organised five themes in separate complexes (Benedict 1983p. 14). Its communications section, "Man in Control?", asked visitors to judge technology for themselves (Benedict 1983p. 36). The World Festival staged more than 20,000 performances before an audience of 16.5 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 333).

The American pavilion's guides were 56 young, French-speaking Americans in uniforms designed by Bill Blass and paid for by Burlington Industries (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 129; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 133). The Soviet pavilion held daily fashion shows (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 136). The Moroccan pavilion, a square building with a sixty-five-foot minaret, recycled Islamic motifs of the kind long used in exposition displays (Çelik 1992p. 185–186).

Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the Labyrinth, a building in three chambers where a National Film Board film ran on two screens, one set into the floor, so that the viewer was moved from screen to screen (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138). The Czech pavilion's Diapolyecran used a screen of 112 two-foot cubes that glided back and forth to show The Creation of the World with more than 15,000 slides (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). The American and Ontario pavilions were the only ones to run the fair's monorail trains through their exhibitions, a unique opportunity that the authors say the other participants missed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). Fuller, they note, saw his dome's controlled interior as a model that might one day be taken to hostile places such as the moon (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). The Western Provinces used smell, with a logging truck among Douglas pines in a central courtyard (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). The La Ronde Gyrotron, designed by Sean Kenny, absorbed so much of the budget that money ran short for interior details (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140).

Visitors, reception and controversy

The sources differ on attendance. Warner gives 50,306,648 admissions in six months, the largest attendance at such an event in North American history (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 50.9 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 209), and Roche gives 51.0 million (Roche 2000p. 43). The busiest day, 30 April, brought 530,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329).

A month-long transit strike halted city buses and the Metro, and the fair lost an estimated 5 million admissions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 333; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Charles de Gaulle's speech in Montreal fuelled demands for Quebec sovereignty (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341), and protests against United States policy in Vietnam were banned on the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Riar says critics, including Giedion, Banham and Lindsay, read Man the Producer as a sublime or playful experience rather than as an institution that normalised people's relations with technology (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 267). Samuel calls the fair an amazing success with little of New York's drama (Samuel 2007).

Reyner Banham, writing for New Society, spent late spring 1967 among the Expo crowds. He complained that the fair had spent its energy on the earth, dredging islands and lagoons in the St Lawrence, and very little on people (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 264). Architecture, he thought, had failed to put people on stage, and man was largely written out of the act (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 264). Banham still found Man the Producer striking. He described it as having the "dumb authority of a primitive industrial plant", with staircases and escalators that carried visitors past other visitors and into dark galleries (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 265). He said it was so vast that it swallowed queues before they formed (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 265).

Banham judged a different building the one place where people were fully part of the fair. That was the Canadian theme pavilion L'Homme à l'Oeuvre, by the Affleck-Desbarats partnership, a structure of rusting steel tetrahedra that he compared with Piranesi, the Eiffel Tower and Price's Fun Palace (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 265).

Legacy

The pavilions were given to Montreal for a Man and His World exhibition, and Habitat 67 remained as a cooperative (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334). Roche counts the island site and Habitat as physical event heritage, and suggests the fair's success encouraged Montreal's bid for the 1976 Olympics (Roche 2000p. 133; Roche 2000p. 90). Osaka 1970's organisers had sent representatives to New York and Montreal, and were so careful to avoid their mistakes that their site planning lacked bold originality (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141).

Man the Producer and the space frame

Arcop, a Montreal firm founded in 1957, received the commission for Man the Producer and Man the Explorer from the CCWE in July 1964 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 260). Its early studies were large trabeated boxes, which Riar finds largely uninspiring. A July 1964 report on a truncated tetrahedron, whose cells could form walls and floor slabs, changed the direction of the design (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 260). The partner Guy Desbarats then brought in Jeffrey Lindsay, a space-frame designer (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 260). Lindsay had flown bombers in the Royal Canadian Air Force, had no architectural training, and worked for two years under Buckminster Fuller in Chicago (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 261). In 1950 he built the Weatherbreak, a 49-foot geodesic dome in Montreal's western suburbs, with six untrained assistants (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 261).

The tetrahedral pavilion was bolted together from steel plate chords joined by gusset plates angled at 70 and 110 degrees. Dominion Bridge began bolting the first sections to concrete footings in April 1966 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 263). The oversized gussets made the frame feel dense rather than light, and Lindsay admitted that the rough look was unintended (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 263). He nonetheless read the roughness as enriching the usual symmetry of space structures (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 263). The building ran to seven levels and 190,000 square feet (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 263).

Picnics and the lunch hour

Van Troi Tran shows that picnics were part of the fair's daily life. Montreal visitors, a press report says, ate sandwiches at picnic spots that Expo provided, while others made do with snacks from hot dog and hamburger stands (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 436). The Boston Globe's advice column told readers to plan a leisurely lunch each day, at a restaurant or from a picnic basket, because the senses could be satiated (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 439). Grassy banks, canal slopes and the woods of St Helen's Island, where the American pavilion had grills, drew picnickers (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 439). The Ontario pavilion sold a 95-cent picnic box with a hot dog, hamburger or meat pie, and the dish of the day (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 441). Flight bags, most often carried by mothers, held sandwiches and cold drinks (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 441).

Tran sets this against Auguste Escoffier's attack on picnics in Paris in 1889. Escoffier said the alleys and lawns had become dining rooms, and that a picnic basket cost as much as an ordinary restaurant meal. He tied his distaste to French ideas of national cuisine (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 442p. 443). American writers, by contrast, described the fair as a Sunday-school picnic, a pastoral image (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 443).

Women guides and the fashion industry

Jack Masey, chief of design at the United States Information Agency, moved the fair's displays away from technology and commerce towards fashion (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 128). He chose Bill Blass to design the guides' uniforms. Masey and Blass had served in the same camouflage battalion in the Second World War (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 129). Planners discussed training the guides on the model of NASA, or recruiting them through the Peace Corps (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 130). The women wore knit tent dresses with navy piping, and the men wore white knit slacks and a blue blazer over a red, white and blue shirt (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 130).

Masey saw the rival as the French Pavilion, with its couture from Dior, Givenchy and Hermès, rather than the Soviet Union (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 131p. 132). Burlington Industries funded the uniforms, made with DuPont's Dacron yarn, and DuPont, Chemstrand and Kodel gave money for the pavilion (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 133). In September 1967 a Life spread presented the US Pavilion as a showcase for fall fashions, and showed a Blass evening coat in front of five US Marines on an escalator (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 131). Sheinin reads the Marines' popularity with foreign visitors, four months before Tet, as a public relations coup (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 131). The Soviet, Scandinavian and Czechoslovakian pavilions also held daily fashion shows, and over six hundred people came to the Soviet shows (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 136).

How historians have interpreted it

Most readings are positive. Warner calls the fair an outstanding success in attendance that met its goal of bringing people together (Findling & Pelle 1990). Bennett calls it a success that carried Montreal towards the 1976 Olympics (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Allwood, Allan and Reid explain the success through theme, finance and planning, and call Fuller's dome the masterpiece of the show (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137). Massidda reads the fair as "a social glue and anesthetic for political tensions" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185), and argues that the American dome's victory over the Soviet glass pavilion was a cultural turning point of the Cold War (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 188). Sheinin reads the American pavilion's fashion as a Cold War argument in which fabric makers and designers were the weapons (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 132).

Riar is more critical of the architecture, arguing that the megastructure ambition was not realised and that critics misread the pavilions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258p. 267). Benedict sees the amusement-zone display techniques of corporate pavilions as making fairs resemble television advertising (Benedict 1983p. 59). Geppert uses Eco's view that the fair can be read through cultural history, sociology, architecture or communication (Geppert 2010p. 241). Harvey, against Eco and Benedict, argues that expositions are hybrid institutions that keep traces of earlier fairs (Harvey 1996p. 109). Mandell reads the fair as an affirmation of Canadian vigour that denied the strain of keeping a bilingual federation together (Mandell 1967p. 166).

Eco, the special reporter for the Montreal fair, found in its accumulation of goods an "unsurpassed, quintessential, classic World’s Fair" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 186). Massidda argues that Montreal restored the technological marvels that had made earlier fairs successful, so that where Paris had introduced the Metro, Montreal made it run underwater, and the Bell Pavilion's 360-degree projection was the ultimate technological celebration of the screen (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 186). Rydell et al. describe Montreal's questioning subtheme, "Man in Control?", as a more chastened vision of the future than the fairs before it (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The authors of a chapter in Lawn's volume recall the 1967 Montreal aspiration that future events would be an "educational instrument, a teaching device", and call it naive in the face of commercialisation (Lawn 2009p. 197).

The texts say more about Osaka 1970, the first world's fair held in Asia, whose organisers studied Montreal (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that the Osaka organisers followed the lead of Expo 67 in providing pavilions for developing countries and in helping those nations gain a significant standing in the exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). The journal Japan Architect devoted an entire August 1967 issue to Expo 67, but the texts do not say what it made of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 335).

Traditional buildings from Asia

Allwood, Allan and Reid name Asian buildings at Montreal. They say that the traditional buildings of Thailand, Burma, Iran and China added a great deal to the scene (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). They also say that smaller countries, which could not afford the recent expositions, exhibited in buildings supplied by the Canadian organisers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140). This runs against the statement above that the texts do not name the countries with pavilions at Montreal. The text does not say who designed, paid for or occupied the Asian buildings.

Themes in the scholarship

Questions for study

  1. How far can a 1967 theme such as Man and His World be judged by the standards of later audiences, and what does its exclusive title show about the era that chose it? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341; Allwood et al. 2001p. 195)
  2. If the master plan was abandoned and the site split into national and corporate pavilions, does a fair still have a single argument to make? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258; Benedict 1983p. 14)
  3. Should a fair's success be measured by attendance and its passport ticket, or by what it leaves behind, such as Habitat 67? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334; Roche 2000p. 133)
  4. Why did two superpowers use fashion and pavilion design to argue about the Cold War, and what did visitors take from it? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 132; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 188)
  5. Is Expo 67 best read as an affirmation of Canadian national confidence or as an event that masked deep political division? (Mandell 1967p. 166; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185)
  6. What did Osaka 1970 learn from Montreal and New York, and what did its caution cost in originality? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141; Samuel 2007)
  7. Who designed, built and paid for the traditional buildings from Thailand, Burma, Iran and China, and were they among the buildings the Canadian organisers supplied to smaller countries? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 140)

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
12Madison Avenue, the Computer and the Rising Sunp. 132Asia

Allwood traces how exhibitions of the late 1960s and early 1970s moved from national statement towards commercial showmanship. The main case is New York 1964-65, where Robert Moses's fair, without major foreign exhibitors, filled its grounds with company pavilions, among them IBM's building by Eero Saarinen, Ford's Disney-designed display and the Johnson Wax film To Be Alive, and was hit by protests, poor site planning and a financial loss. Montreal's Expo 67 is presented as a high point, with Moshe Safdie's Habitat 67, Buckminster Fuller's geodesic American pavilion and the Labyrinth film. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event of record attendance, organised around Kenzo Tange's Festival Plaza and the Tower of the Sun, and remembered for the rush to see the American pavilion's moon rock. The chapter ends with a run of picture captions on later fairs and legacies, including Expo 98, Hanover 2000 and the London Dome, which carry its argument forward without comment.

4.4Expo 67, or Megastructure ReduxInderbir Singh Riarp. 255

Riar examines Expo 67 in Montreal and its theme pavilions, which were conceived as megastructures: flexible, cellular frameworks that would house a mass public and embody the optimism of 1960s architecture. The chapter traces the idea to the van Ginkels, Montreal architects and Team 10 members, who wanted the fair organized around a city theme rather than national pavilions, and to Giedion's reading of nineteenth-century exhibition buildings such as the Crystal Palace, the Galerie des Machines and the Eiffel Tower. Riar then follows the collapse of the master plan into five theme pavilions. The central case is Man the Producer, designed by Arcop with the space-frame engineer Jeffrey Lindsay, whose tetrahedral cells owed much to Buckminster Fuller and whose oversized gusset plates were bolted together in 1966. Riar draws on Reyner Banham's 1967 review, which found the tetrahedral pavilion the only place where people were written into the act, and on the critical reception that photographed it as a dematerializing dreamscape. The chapter concludes that the utopian promise of the megastructure lasted only the six months of the fair.

5.4Beyond the Event: World’s Fairs and Urban Transformation Since WWIIGaia Caramellino, Alessandro De Magistris, Federico Deambrosisp. 311

Caramellino, De Magistris and Deambrosis argue that world's fairs should be studied not as enclosed spectacles but as urban transformations that continued long after the events closed. They trace a chronology from the late nineteenth-century exhibitions, which were closely tied to the growth of their host cities, to the postwar fairs, in which planning and architecture became central. Their main cases are Brussels 1958, where the fair helped drive the remaking of the city centre, and Turin 1961, whose durable buildings such as Nervi's Palazzo del Lavoro were linked to the urbanisation of a southern periphery. They also consider the New York fair of 1964-65, which the authors treat as a missed opportunity for urban planning, and Montreal 1967, whose infrastructure reshaped Ile Sainte-Hélène. The chapter then moves through Genoa, Seville and Lisbon in the 1990s and Shanghai 2010, where fairs served as triggers for port, waterfront and metropolitan regeneration. The authors end by noting that European fairs since 2000 have shifted toward smaller, more dispersed projects, while Shanghai 2010 carried the older model of large-scale city regeneration.

7.4A History of Picnics at World’s FairsVan Troi Tranp. 435

Tran argues that picnics, though a visible presence on fairgrounds, have been largely ignored in histories of world's fairs. Using press reports, official reports, caricatures and advice columns, the chapter reconstructs picnicking at Paris 1889 and 1900, Chicago 1893, New York 1964-65, Montreal 1967 and Shanghai 2010. It reads the picnic in several ways: as a social leveler that inverted class distinctions, with millionaires in the Eiffel Tower and workers on the lawns; as a thrifty alternative to crowded and costly restaurants, which guidebooks promoted; and as a source of sociability and calm amid the sensory strain of the fair. It also traces the market for picnic gear and box lunches. The sharpest episode is Auguste Escoffier's attack on picnicking in Paris, set against French ideas of national cuisine and contrasted with the pastoral, Sunday-school image of the American picnic. The chapter ends with Shanghai 2010, where airport-style checks barred cooked food and take-away meals for fear of food poisoning.

Molella, Arthur P., and Scott Gabriel Knowles, eds. World’s Fairs in the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of Progress. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2019.
11“Massy and Classy”: Dressing American Women at Expo ’67Daniela Sheininp. 128

Sheinin examines the US Pavilion at Expo '67 in Montreal, where women guides wore uniforms designed by the fashion designer Bill Blass. The argument is that the United States Information Agency, under its design chief Jack Masey, used fashion rather than technology or commerce to claim American leadership, and that the fashion industry presented American ready-to-wear clothing as a Cold War asset. The chapter draws on the pavilion's planning, the newsletters of the Fashion Group International, and fashion and news magazines, and sets the pavilion against the French Pavilion and the Soviet Pavilion's fashion shows. Sheinin traces how the 'Sputnik style' rhetoric of the 1950s, with its moon-themed cosmetics and talk of fabric laboratories, framed American clothing as modern and scientific, and how DuPont and Burlington Industries backed the pavilion and its uniforms. The later sections follow Blass's rise as a designer celebrity, which the chapter presents as the start of a fashion economy in which the designer's name mattered as much as the fabric. The chapter ends by describing Expo '67 as a turning point in the marketing of American women's fashion.

15The Cold War, a Cool Medium, and the Postmodern Death of World ExposLuca Massiddap. 183

Massidda asks why world expositions disappeared after Osaka 1970, with no BIE-recognised universal exposition until Seville in 1992. The argument is that the expo's cultural form was structurally incompatible with late capitalism, the crisis of the city and the dominance of television, which made the Olympics a more effective 'media event'. The chapter has two parts. The first examines Brussels 1958, Montreal 1967 and Osaka 1970 at their peak, with attendances of 41 to 64 million, Cold War superpower pavilions, and new technology such as the Bell Pavilion's 360-degree screen. The second, covering 1970 to 1992, draws on McLuhan and Meyrowitz to argue that television dissolved the physical public space on which expos depended, and it presents a table of smaller specialised fairs in the period. Massidda concludes that the digital and networked media of the 1990s returned the city to the centre of the social system and gave the expo a new opportunity, as Seville showed.

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
Epiloguep. 181

Çelik examines what happened to the representational habits of the nineteenth-century fairs in the twentieth century, arguing that the non-Western quarters persisted even as fairs became more specialised. The chapter describes the colonial exhibitions in France, notably the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, whose Tunisian quarter reconstructed a souk peopled by Tunisian craftsmen, and the Museum of the Colonies, which mixed Greco-Latin architecture with colonial motifs. It then shows how the Turkish pavilion at New York in 1939 and the Moroccan pavilion at Montreal in 1967 reused familiar North African imagery, and how the Musée de l'Homme of 1938 presented other cultures while leaving the modern West unexhibited. The second half compares Albert Laprade's new madina at Casablanca and Hassan Fathy's village of New Gourna with the paternalism of colonial planners. Çelik ends with the restored Soğukçeşme Street in Istanbul and the immigrant quarters of European cities, arguing that staged authenticity and power relations still shape how Islam is represented.

Wikipedia

The 1967 International and Universal Exposition, commonly known as Expo 67, was a general exhibition from April 28 to October 29, 1967. It was a category one world's fair held in Montreal, Quebec, Canada. It is considered to be one of the most successful world's fairs of the 20th century with the most attendees to that date and 62 nations participating. It also set the single-day attendance record for a world's fair, with 569,500 visitors on its third day. Expo 67 was Canada's main celebration during its centennial year. The fair had been intended to be held in Moscow, to help the Soviet Union celebrate the Russian Revolution's 50th anniversary; however, for various reasons, the Soviets decided to cancel, and Canada was awarded it in late 1962. The project was not well supported in Canada at first.
