Expo 67 (Universal and International Exhibition of 1967)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

Expo 67, the Universal and International Exhibition of 1967, ran at Montreal from April to October 1967 on islands in the St Lawrence River (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). It was Canada's centenary world's fair. The Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), the body that authorised international fairs, awarded it to Montreal in November 1962 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 180). Riar calls it a turning point (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Its theme, Man and His World, and its passport admission ticket drew wide attention, and with Paris 1900 it was one of the two fairs to pass 50 million visits (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 183; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Canada's first bid lost to Moscow. Prime Minister John Diefenbaker endorsed the bid in February 1960, and when Moscow withdrew two years later for financial reasons, Canada bid again (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335). The BIE awarded Montreal the fair on 13 November 1962, the third exhibition of its first category and the first held in North America (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The idea had been raised at Brussels in 1958, when a senator proposed an international exhibition for Canada's centenary and Mayor Jean Drapeau backed it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137). The theme came from Saint-Exupéry's Terre des Hommes (Findling & Pelle 2008). Bennett finds the exclusive title jarring for modern readers, but calls it "the language of an era" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Allwood, Allan and Reid place 1967 as a watershed between a conservative postwar Canada and the livelier 1960s (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137).

Organisation and finance

Montreal's organisers learned from the New York World's Fair of 1964-65. Meeting a week after New York closed, they insisted on official BIE endorsement (Samuel 2007) and chose a theme they judged more achievable: "The official theme of the exhibition, Man and His World, may have been less ambitious than New York’s theme of Peace Through Understanding, but it was certainly more achievable." (Samuel 2007). Many corporate exhibitors that had overspent in New York stayed away (Samuel 2007).

Money came from the city, the province and the federal government. Allwood, Allan and Reid say Montreal had no hunt for foreign participants and no grave financial problems, and its planners used a computer critical-path method, which orders tasks by the sequence they depend on (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138). The Expo-Express cost $18.3 million, included in the admission price (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The reference tables record a loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 209; Roche 2000p. 43).

The pavilions were not grouped as private or international (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 330). Riar argues that the planned "Man in the City" master plan, a megastructure or large flexible framework for city life, was never achieved, and that the fair was "largely atomized by national, cultural, and corporate pavilions" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258).

Site, transport and architecture

The fair covered 988 acres, much of it new land made from 17.6 million cubic yards of fill (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329). The Expo-Express carried about 44 million passengers (Findling & Pelle 2008). Moshe Safdie's Habitat 67, 354 precast concrete boxes forming 158 apartments, cost $20 million according to one reference work and $21 million according to another (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 330; Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe its production-line construction as troubled (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138).

Buckminster Fuller's United States dome, 250 feet across, was the most popular and most widely recognised pavilion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 331; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 339). Rain leaked through it and its sun blinds jammed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 139). Man the Producer, a space frame of tetrahedral steel cells, drew attention, but the architecture press looked at its frame and largely ignored the exhibits inside [13, l. 12895-12898; 13204-13207].

Pavilions, attractions and nations on show

Benedict says the fair avoided a single focal building and organised five themes in separate complexes (Benedict 1983p. 14). Its communications section, "Man in Control?", asked visitors to judge technology for themselves (Benedict 1983p. 36). The World Festival staged more than 20,000 performances before an audience of 16.5 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 333).

The American pavilion's guides were 56 young, French-speaking Americans in uniforms designed by Bill Blass and paid for by Burlington Industries (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 129; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 133). The Soviet pavilion held daily fashion shows (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 136). The Moroccan pavilion, a square building with a sixty-five-foot minaret, recycled Islamic motifs of the kind long used in exposition displays (Çelik 1992p. 185–186).

Visitors, reception and controversy

The sources differ on attendance. Warner gives 50,306,648 admissions in six months, the largest attendance at such an event in North American history (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 50.9 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 209), and Roche gives 51.0 million (Roche 2000p. 43). The busiest day, 30 April, brought 530,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329).

A month-long transit strike halted city buses and the Metro, and the fair lost an estimated 5 million admissions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 333; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Charles de Gaulle's speech in Montreal fuelled demands for Quebec sovereignty (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341), and protests against United States policy in Vietnam were banned on the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Riar says critics, including Giedion, Banham and Lindsay, read Man the Producer as a sublime or playful experience rather than as an institution that normalised people's relations with technology (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 267). Samuel calls the fair an amazing success with little of New York's drama (Samuel 2007).

Legacy

The pavilions were given to Montreal for a Man and His World exhibition, and Habitat 67 remained as a cooperative (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334). Roche counts the island site and Habitat as physical event heritage, and suggests the fair's success encouraged Montreal's bid for the 1976 Olympics (Roche 2000p. 133; Roche 2000p. 90). Osaka 1970's organisers had sent representatives to New York and Montreal, and were so careful to avoid their mistakes that their site planning lacked bold originality (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141).

How historians have interpreted it

Most readings are positive. Warner calls the fair an outstanding success in attendance that met its goal of bringing people together (Findling & Pelle 1990). Bennett calls it a success that carried Montreal towards the 1976 Olympics (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341). Allwood, Allan and Reid explain the success through theme, finance and planning, and call Fuller's dome the masterpiece of the show (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137; Allwood et al. 2001). Massidda reads the fair as "a social glue and anesthetic for political tensions" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185), and argues that the American dome's victory over the Soviet glass pavilion was a cultural turning point of the Cold War (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 188). Sheinin reads the American pavilion's fashion as a Cold War argument in which fabric makers and designers were the weapons (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 132).

Riar is more critical of the architecture, arguing that the megastructure ambition was not realised and that critics misread the pavilions [13, l. 12810-12816; 13254-13258]. Benedict sees the amusement-zone display techniques of corporate pavilions as making fairs resemble television advertising (Benedict 1983p. 59). Geppert uses Eco's view that the fair can be read through cultural history, sociology, architecture or communication (Geppert 2010p. 241). Harvey, against Eco and Benedict, argues that expositions are hybrid institutions that keep traces of earlier fairs (Harvey 1996p. 109). Mandell reads the fair as an affirmation of Canadian vigour that denied the strain of keeping a bilingual federation together (Mandell 1967p. 166).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say little about an Asian presence at Expo 67, and none of them names an Asian commissioner, visitor or observer at Montreal. The one direct mention is passing. Samuel records that Thailand's eighteenth-century Buddhist shrine was taken apart for reassembly in Montreal at Expo 67 (Samuel 2007). The sentence sits in a passage on what became of buildings after the 1964-65 New York fair (Samuel 2007). The text does not say who sent the shrine, who paid for it or how visitors saw it.

The texts say more about Osaka 1970, the first world's fair held in Asia, whose organisers studied Montreal [50, l. 6633-6634; 6640-6658]. Allwood, Allan and Reid say that the Osaka organisers followed the lead of Expo 67 in providing pavilions for developing countries and in helping those nations gain a significant standing in the exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 146). The texts do not name the developing countries that had pavilions at Montreal, so they cannot say how many were in Asia.

The journal Japan Architect devoted an entire August 1967 issue to Expo 67, but the texts do not say what it made of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 335).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Canadian identity and the centenary — the texts read the fair as Canada's coming of age and a watershed, with the Quebec crisis as its political background (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 335; Allwood et al. 2001p. 137; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185)
  • Man and His World — Samuel judges the theme more achievable than New York's, while Bennett and Allwood, Allan and Reid criticise its exclusive title (Samuel 2007; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341; Allwood et al. 2001p. 195)
  • Architecture and the megastructure — Riar says the master plan gave way to atomised national and corporate pavilions, while Allwood, Allan and Reid call Fuller's dome the show's masterpiece (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • National display in the Cold War — the American and Soviet pavilions competed through fashion and architecture, and Massidda reads the American dome's victory as a cultural turning point (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 136; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 188)
  • Finance and planning — public money from three governments, computer scheduling and the lessons of New York, although the reference tables still record a loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 138; Samuel 2007; Allwood et al. 2001p. 209)
  • Crowds and transport — the Expo-Express, the record attendance and the transit strike shaped what visitors saw (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 329; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 333; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334)
  • Legacy and heritage — Habitat 67, the 1976 Olympic bid and Osaka 1970 are the fair's afterlives in the texts (Roche 2000p. 133; Roche 2000p. 90; Allwood et al. 2001p. 141)

Questions for study

  1. How far can a 1967 theme such as Man and His World be judged by the standards of later audiences, and what does its exclusive title show about the era that chose it? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 341; Allwood et al. 2001p. 195)
  2. If the master plan was abandoned and the site split into national and corporate pavilions, does a fair still have a single argument to make? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 258; Benedict 1983p. 14)
  3. Should a fair's success be measured by attendance and its passport ticket, or by what it leaves behind, such as Habitat 67? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 334; Roche 2000p. 133)
  4. Why did two superpowers use fashion and pavilion design to argue about the Cold War, and what did visitors take from it? (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 132; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 188)
  5. Is Expo 67 best read as an affirmation of Canadian national confidence or as an event that masked deep political division? (Mandell 1967p. 166; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 185)
  6. What did Osaka 1970 learn from Montreal and New York, and what did its caution cost in originality? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 141; Samuel 2007)

A multi-author volume on world's fairs whose one chapter devoted to Expo 67, by Inderbir Singh Riar, argues that the Montreal fair was planned by architects who wanted it to showcase the 'megastructure', a flexible framework for city life and social interaction. Riar traces how the master plan of 'Man in the City' gave way under political pressure and the atomisation of the site into national and corporate pavilions, leaving Man the Producer, a tetrahedral steel structure, as the most noticed experiment. His larger case is that the megastructural aim of an architecture that would reform social and technical life was not realised: critics read the pavilion as sublime or ludic experience and so refused to see it as an institution in which people's relations with technology were normalised. Shorter passages elsewhere treat the fair as a turning point towards environmental concern (Introduction), a high point of the fair era (Bransford), a fair whose lasting heritage was its infrastructure (Caramellino, De Magistris and Deambrosis), and an example of iconic architecture (Lukas).

Allwood, Allan and Reid treat Expo 67 as the high point of the modern international exhibition movement and as a turning point in Canadian national confidence. They explain its success by its theme, its financing (provided by city, province and federal government without the competition for foreign participants that troubled New York 1964-65) and its planning by computer critical-path method, and they single out the architecture, above all Habitat 67 and the Fuller dome (which they call the architectural masterpiece of the show), as the fair's signature. They also use Montreal as the benchmark for Osaka, whose organisers studied the mistakes of New York and Montreal, and in their closing chapter they note that a theme like Man and His World would be unacceptable today.

Moderate discussion 2

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
Single-author book; PDF pp. 31 (focal structures), 41-49 (Tables 1-3), 54 (the five themes), 73 and 77 (amusements and commercial pavilions).

Benedict's anthropological study of world's fairs treats Expo 67 as an example of the shift away from a single focal structure and from ordered displays of the world towards a set of separate themed complexes. He describes its five themes, in particular Man the Producer, whose communications section was called 'Man in Control?', and notes that the Montreal fair asked visitors to decide for themselves whether modern technology was worth it. He also argues that corporate pavilions at recent fairs, including Expo 67 and Osaka 1970, adopted the techniques of the amusement zone, so that fairs 'begin to resemble the sponsored commercials of television'. For Benedict, the New York fair of 1939 marks the point after which fairs became less important events.

Geppert uses Expo 67 twice, both in passing comparison. He notes that the 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle, with more than 50 million visitors over seven months, held an attendance record that was broken only at Montreal's fair. In his conclusion he quotes Umberto Eco, who was asked about Expo '67's meaning and answered that the fair could be interpreted through cultural history, sociology, architecture or communication; Geppert uses this to present expositions as multi-faceted and open to several readings.

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
Epilogue, pp. 184-185 (Moroccan pavilion); Fig. 122, p. 188; index entry 'World Exhibition of 1967 (Montreal), 185, 188'.

Çelik's book studies how Western fairs and museums displayed Islamic and Oriental architecture, and its epilogue follows these displays into the twentieth century. Montreal 1967 appears as one case: a Moroccan pavilion, a square building with a sixty-five-foot minaret whose decoration recycled North African Islamic motifs. Çelik reads this recycling as part of a long pattern in which modernisation and a historically defined cultural image were held in tension. The Montreal pavilion is therefore cited as evidence that the pattern persisted.

Wikipedia

The 1967 International and Universal Exposition, commonly known as Expo 67, was a general exhibition from April 28 to October 29, 1967. It was a category one world's fair held in Montreal, Quebec, Canada. It is considered to be one of the most successful world's fairs of the 20th century with the most attendees to that date and 62 nations participating. It also set the single-day attendance record for a world's fair, with 569,500 visitors on its third day. Expo 67 was Canada's main celebration during its centennial year. The fair had been intended to be held in Moscow, to help the Soviet Union celebrate the Russian Revolution's 50th anniversary; however, for various reasons, the Soviets decided to cancel, and Canada was awarded it in late 1962. The project was not well supported in Canada at first.
