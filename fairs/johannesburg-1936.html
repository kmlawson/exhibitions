Johannesburg · 1936–1937

Empire Exhibition (Johannesburg)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Johannesburg Empire Exhibition was the first British empire exhibition in Africa, held at Milner Park in Johannesburg in the Union of South Africa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). City leaders and industrialists from across the British Empire organised it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). One text gives the run as 16 September 1936 to 15 January 1937 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288); another gives the official opening as 15 September 1936 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). The texts report about 1.5 million visitors and an estimated loss of £70,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). Only two reference entries treat it at length (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The exhibition marked the fiftieth anniversary of Johannesburg's founding (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). A citywide jubilee was the first plan, but it grew into an empire exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). In January 1935 the Grand Council of the Federation of British Industries adopted a resolution supporting a proposal for an Empire Exhibition in Johannesburg in 1936 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). The 1928 International Convention Relating to International Exhibitions meant that an international exhibition would have had to be imperial (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288).

The organisers set six objects (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). The first was to present the history and development of the Union (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). The last aimed to gain worldwide publicity and draw tourist traffic from abroad (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289).

The texts differ on the opening. Text 39 says Lord Clarendon, governor-general of South Africa, opened the exhibition on 15 September 1936 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). Text 35 says the opening day brought speeches from the king of England, the prime ministers of the dominions and Great Britain, and the governor-general (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). Text 39's appendix notes that the ceremonial opening and the first public admission may fall on different dates (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 375).

Organisers and finance

Propagandists travelled through South Africa and the empire to recruit manufacturers, trade groups and agriculturalists, while the city of Johannesburg built the exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). The Pretoria government appropriated £50,000 for the host nation's pavilion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). Despite an extensive advertising and propaganda campaign, the exhibition did not succeed financially (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). Gate receipts failed to cover costs, and the Union government had to bail out the city to pay its creditors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). Text 39 gives an estimated loss of £70,000 against a total cost of £2 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282).

Site and exhibits

The grounds were a 100-acre section of Milner Park, about a mile from the city centre (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). Visitors entered on Empire Road and walked along Prosperity Avenue (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). The five major halls included the Hall of Industries, covering 5 acres, and a hall with the first ice rink in Africa (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). Foreign and corporate pavilions lined the avenue, among them the Cape House, notable for its wine cellars (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281).

Empire and colony

Text 35 calls the exhibition imperial in form but largely national in scope. It tried to balance South Africa's imperial connection with its economic, military and political autonomy within the empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288). The National Party government, led by Prime Minister J.B.M. Hertzog, had sought independence from Britain in steps that imperial circles often saw as provocative (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288).

The colonial exhibits in the L-shaped building included New Zealand, Ceylon, Australia and Canada (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). The East African pavilion represented Kenya, Uganda and Tanganyika (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). Southern Rhodesia, Trinidad, Nigeria, Seychelles, Swaziland, Bechuanaland, Nyasaland and Basutoland were also represented (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). The host nation's pavilion was the largest of the colonial pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282).

The texts say little about how colonised and Indigenous people took part. The arena, which seated 20,000, hosted "native war dances" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282), as well as pageants of African history and a rodeo (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). The texts do not say who performed in these events (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). They do not name settlement among the organisers' aims (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288).

Visitors and reception

The organisers expected 2 to 2.5 million regional and overseas visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). About 1.5 million came (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). Text 35 says the expected audience did not materialise and suggests two causes: racial politics in South Africa that put restrictions on the attendance of indigenous Africans and the travel needed to reach Johannesburg (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). The sympathetic English-language press gave the event positive reviews (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289).

Its place in the series

Text 39 presents the exhibition as one of a series of British empire celebrations, after a Festival of Empire in London in 1911 and the Wembley empire exhibition of 1924-1925, where South Africa had been represented (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). Text 50 places it among the British Empire exhibitions held between the wars (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). Text 42 lists it among exhibitions staged to mark the development of dominions and colonies (Roche 2000p. 58).

Aftermath

No international exposition has been held in South Africa since (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282). In the late 1970s the university incorporated the remaining permanent buildings, including the ballroom, the ice rink and the Tower of Light (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282).

How historians have interpreted it

Deborah L. Hughes, who wrote the Johannesburg entry in the encyclopedia edited by Findling and Pelle, reads the exhibition as imperial in framing but largely national in scope. She concludes that it "did little to influence anti-imperial sentiment" in South Africa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289). She also says its historical recognition is almost entirely obscured by the Great Trek Centenary held in Pretoria two years later (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289).

Michael L. Gregory, who wrote the entry in the historical dictionary edited by Findling and Pelle, presents it as one of a series of British empire celebrations held in the face of the empire's decline (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282).

The only Asian exhibit the texts name is Ceylon's, in the L-shaped building of colonial exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281). The passages say nothing about other Asian sections, commissioners, performers or visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial framing and national scope — an exhibition planned under imperial conditions but built around the Union's own resources and trade (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288).
  • Colonial pavilions — colonies and dominions had their own displays along the avenue, and the East African pavilion represented three colonies (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 281).
  • Race and attendance — the shortfall in visitors is linked to racial restrictions on the attendance of indigenous Africans (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289).
  • Finance and failure — the advertising campaign and gate receipts did not cover costs, and the Union government bailed out the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289).

Questions for study

  1. Why did an exhibition in a self-governing country present itself as imperial, and whose interests did the balance between imperial connection and national autonomy serve? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 288)
  2. Which visitors did the organisers expect, and which did the racial restrictions and the cost of travel keep away? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 289)
  3. What evidence would show who performed the arena's events? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 282)

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Wikipedia

The Empire Exhibition, South Africa, held in Johannesburg, was intended to mark that city's jubilee and was opened by the Governor-General of the Union of South Africa on 15 September 1936. It was the first exhibition held in the Union of South Africa following two earlier exhibitions in Cape Colony in 1877 and 1892. The idea of an empire exhibition in South Africa was first discussed in 1934 by the Buy Empire Committee of Johannesburg. On 9 January 1935, the Grand Council of the Federation of British Industries passed a resolution for a proposal to hold an Empire Exhibition in Johannesburg in 1936 in conjunction with the Golden Jubilee of the city. A site of 100 acres in Milner Park was secured for the exhibition. Here were built about 100 buildings including eight pavilions from foreign nations and eight main exhibition buildings, the largest being the Hall of Industries.
