International Exposition of Great Industry, Science and Walloon Art

The International Exposition of Great Industry, Science and Walloon Art was a Belgian international exhibition held at Liège in 1930, during the centenary of Belgian independence. It formed one half of a single exposition held on two sites, the other half being the colonial exposition at Antwerp (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258). Liège concentrated on science, industry, social economy, agriculture and music, and it ran for six months (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Historians use it mainly as the contrast to Antwerp: a trade-minded fair beside an imperial one (Rydell 1993p. 69; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and the split between Antwerp and Liège

Belgium marked the hundredth anniversary of its independence in 1930 with two linked expositions. Rydell records that the Belgian government opened two expositions that year to celebrate the centenary of national independence: the colonial and maritime exposition at Antwerp, and the Exposition Internationale de la Grande Industrie, Science et Application d'Art Wallon at Liège (Rydell 1993p. 68). Findling and Pelle's 1990 dictionary treats the arrangement as one exposition on two sites (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258).

The split came from rivalry. The cities disputed which of them should be the focus of the centenary celebrations. Brussels had staged the most impressive earlier expositions, and Brussels built the Heysel Stadium to mark the anniversary, while Antwerp and Liège agreed to divide the exposition between them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258). The 1935 Brussels entry adds that the national centenary, first planned for 1930, was finally given to Antwerp and Liège (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 277).

Site, length and the missing attendance

Allwood, Allan and Reid's table of fair statistics gives the Liège site as 165 acres and the exposition as open for six months (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Attendance is the figure the texts leave out. Greenhalgh's joint entry in the 1990 dictionary states that "The size of the attendance is not contained within the official reports" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259). The entry judges that both sites had good attendance throughout and were officially considered successful (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259). The 2008 encyclopedia entry makes the same point (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258).

The texts say little about the buildings at Liège. The architectural discussion in the 2008 encyclopedia concerns Antwerp, where the architecture is compared with the modernist work shown at Stuttgart in 1927 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258).

Allwood, Allan and Reid's statistics table gives a visitor figure for Liège of 27,000,000, set beside the 165-acre site already cited (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). This goes against the statement above that the texts leave out attendance. The table gives no source, and the profit figure in the same row is hard to read in the text, so the visitor figure needs a second source before a student relies on it.

Industry, science and the trade fair

The two sites were divided by theme. Greenhalgh's 2008 encyclopedia entry says that Antwerp was the bigger and more lavish venue, "while Liége had the sciences, industry, social economy, agriculture, and music" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). Rydell likewise says that the Liège exposition stressed Belgium's industrial potential (Rydell 1993p. 69).

The Liège half had a different character from Antwerp's. Greenhalgh writes that it had "more of the air of a trade fair or conference" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). A trade fair is one aimed at buyers and sellers. Liège had been stripped of the features normally thought most colourful at such events (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259). Even so, Greenhalgh judges that in international participation and the trade it created, Liège remained a significant event in the recent history of the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259).

Two texts disagree about the slogan of the fair. Hollengreen and colleagues record that the 1930 exposition at Liège offered the slogan 'See the World in a Day' (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 406). Allwood, Allan and Reid attach the same slogan to the Paris colonial exposition of 1931 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107), so the sources do not agree on which fair used it.

Imperial displays and the Antwerp comparison

The texts disagree about whether Liège had imperial displays. Greenhalgh's 1988 book, Ephemeral Vistas, says that the 1930 Liège and Antwerp expositions both had extensive imperial sections, and that they were held simultaneously as a joint celebration of the centenary (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Greenhalgh's later entries and Rydell's account place the empire at Antwerp instead. Rydell says the Antwerp exposition highlighted Belgium's imperial conquests in Africa (Rydell 1993p. 69). Greenhalgh's 2008 entry says Antwerp staged the most lavish display of empire ever held on Belgian soil (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257). Its Palais du Congo was meant to be the centrepiece (Rydell 1993p. 69).

Italy took part in both Belgian shows, together with 22 other states. Filipová's volume reads this participation as part of an intensified colonial propaganda, and as a move against other colonial powers (Filipová 2015p. 316).

Greenhalgh's account of the colonial side is the most detailed in the set, and it is about Antwerp. The main Belgian building there, the Palais du Congo, had a "curiously hybrid" appearance (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). The General Report says its ornament drew on engravings used on the weapons, utensils and mats of Congolese peoples, and Greenhalgh notes that the pavilion looked more oriental than Congolese (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). The architect decided that the Congo could supply only decorative trimmings for a building that housed its produce (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72).

The Antwerp colonial section also followed a list of display categories that every visiting nation was asked to use. The list ran from the history of colonisation, indigenous politics and missions to mining, transport and agriculture, and prizes went to the best display in each category (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Greenhalgh links this rational scheme to British Victorian events and to the Paris show of 1867, which set it apart from fairs held after 1918 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72).

The British made a special effort at Antwerp because they were not going to take part in the Paris colonial fair of 1931 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72). Their section used a "parade of dioramas, models and electrically lit maps" to show the empire (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). The French joined mainly as a courtesy to the Belgians, and the Portuguese decided late, so their buildings were small (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). Even so, Greenhalgh judges that the colonial sections as a whole were "proudly showing to the Belgian population" the riches that empire had brought (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73).

The chapter's only statement about Liège's empire is the general claim that the 1930 Liège and Antwerp fairs had extensive imperial sections. It then lists Liège's exhibits as science, industry, social economy, agriculture and music, and it describes no imperial exhibit at Liège (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Its only Liège-specific reference is Léon Michel's book of 1929, which was printed a year before the fair opened (Greenhalgh 1988p. 81).

Visitors and reception

Greenhalgh's 1990 entry reports that the two sites were officially considered successful, and that the large sums spent on their contributions by the British, French, Italians and Portuguese show that these governments expected massive audiences (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259). The 2008 entry makes the same argument about the money spent (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). Beyond these claims of success, the texts give little on how the Liège exposition was received by its visitors.

The wider interwar circuit

Rydell lists the Liège exposition among the European fairs that followed the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley (Rydell 1993p. 3). Geppert records that when the Paris colonial exposition of 1931 was postponed, some invited nations pointed to the rapid succession of comparable events, Liège (1930) among them, as reasons for delay (Geppert 2010p. 181). Rydell also records that Chicago's exposition executives made pilgrimages to fairs including Liège, but that the Paris colonial fair captured their attention most (Rydell 1993p. 82). Roche counts the Belgian expositions of 1930 at Liège and Antwerp among the interwar expositions with colonial themes (Roche 2000p. 59).

Chicago's executives made pilgrimages to Barcelona, Seville, Antwerp, Liège and Stockholm. None of these fairs held their attention as the Paris colonial fair did, except possibly Antwerp's colonial fair (Rydell 1993p. 82). Henry Cole, who had been the guiding hand behind the British exhibits at Antwerp, opened doors for the Chicago executives in Europe (Rydell 1993p. 82).

Ganz says that tours of Antwerp, Liège and other fairs shaped the Chicago organisers' ideas about display, and that the diorama was a new method of display they should consider. Lenox Lohr was captivated by the detail of British dioramas (Ganz 2008). After Rufus Dawes and other fair representatives visited Europe, the Old European Village idea was developed further; at the Antwerp exhibition they saw an old Belgian village with people in period costume (Ganz 2008). The plan as originally conceived failed (Ganz 2008).

Crew, who planned the science displays for Chicago, visited Antwerp and Liège with the directors (Rydell 1993p. 97). At Antwerp he saw the British map of the empire and proposed a similar horizontal panorama for the history of science (Rydell 1993p. 98). Geppert adds that Britain chose to contribute to the Antwerp exhibition of 1930 rather than to the Paris colonial fair, and that it feared looking diminished beside the French (Geppert 2010p. 194).

Records and the press

Rydell describes the City Archives of Liège as holding a "mountain of disintegrating manuscript and print material" from fairs held in that city, and calls the sight one of the most disheartening he has met in researching fairs (Rydell 1993p. 219). The records of the Brussels fairs, including 1958, are kept at Exposition Park (Rydell 1993p. 219).

Greenhalgh's bibliographic notes in the 2008 encyclopedia and the 1990 dictionary list several articles on the joint exposition in The Times of London in 1930. The issues named run from 4 March to 9 October (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259).

How historians have interpreted it

The main reading in the set is that the Belgian exhibitions of 1930 served imperial purposes, with Antwerp at the centre of the argument. Greenhalgh argues in Ephemeral Vistas that Belgium's own exhibitions are a better guide to its imperial consciousness than other evidence, and that the Belgian empire received its greatest embellishment at them (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Rydell argues, through the Antwerp exposition, that modernism and colonialism were two sides of one coin, and that the modernist national pavilions reinforced the imperialist message (Rydell 1993p. 69).

Greenhalgh's dictionary and encyclopedia entries are more restrained about Liège. They treat it as the trade-minded half of the event and keep the argument about empire for Antwerp (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258). Filipová's volume reads the Italian presence in both shows as colonial propaganda (Filipová 2015p. 316). Allwood, Allan and Reid use the Liège exposition only as a line in a table of statistics (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

The sources agree that Antwerp carried the imperial argument and that Liège was the secondary site. They disagree on whether Liège had imperial sections at all. Greenhalgh's 1988 book says it did (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71), while Rydell and Greenhalgh's later entries describe Liège through its industry and science (Rydell 1993p. 69; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Antwerp-Liège split — The 1930 exposition was one event on two sites, divided after rivalry between the cities over the centenary celebrations. (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 258; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257)
  • Industry, science and trade — Liège's programme is described as industrial, scientific and commercial rather than imperial. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257; Rydell 1993p. 69; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 258)
  • Imperial sections (disputed) — Greenhalgh's 1988 book says Liège had extensive imperial sections; later accounts place empire at Antwerp. (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 257)
  • Attendance and records — The official reports did not record attendance, and the statistics table gives the site as 165 acres. (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 259; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208)
  • Colonial propaganda — Italy's participation in both Belgian shows is read as part of an intensified colonial propaganda. (Filipová 2015p. 316)
  • Fairs as a circuit — Liège appears as one of the interwar expositions cited for comparison, notably in the Paris 1931 debates. (Geppert 2010p. 181; Rydell 1993p. 82)
  • Slogans — The texts attribute 'See the World in a Day' to different fairs. (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 406; Allwood et al. 2001p. 107)

Questions for study

  1. Why does Greenhalgh's 1988 book describe Liège as having extensive imperial sections, when the same author's later entries describe a trade-minded, industrial exposition? What evidence would settle the question?
  2. The official reports did not record attendance. How should a historian judge the success of a fair whose attendance is unknown?
  3. What did the rivalry between Antwerp and Liège over the centenary celebrations reveal about how a national anniversary was organised?
  4. If the trade-fair character was Liège's main feature, why have most accounts of the 1930 Belgian expositions concentrated on Antwerp?
  5. Italy took part in both Belgian shows. What does that participation tell us about colonial propaganda beyond Belgium?
  6. Greenhalgh's 1988 book says the 1930 Liège fair had extensive imperial sections but describes no Liège exhibit. Which Liège records or press reports could test that claim, and where are they kept?
  7. Allwood, Allan and Reid print a visitor figure for Liège with no source. How should a student weigh a figure that the dictionary and encyclopedia do not give?
  8. Chicago's organisers took ideas from Antwerp's colonial village and from the British map there, but no source says they took anything similar from Liège. What does that suggest about which Belgian fair had more to teach American planners?

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
ThreeRecurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904p. 86Asia

Hur looks at China's official participation in the Louisiana Purchase Exposition at St. Louis in 1904, the first Qing participation in a Western world fair. The chapter argues that Chinese newspapers read the fair through the Jinruikan trauma of Osaka, so that a run of episodes at St. Louis were framed as national shame. It works through four cases: a portrait of the Empress Dowager painted by the American artist Katharine Carl, which Chinese papers denounced as an insult; a tableau of a bound-foot woman and an opium smoker in the Palace of Liberal Arts; a 'Chinese Village' on the Pike where a bound-foot woman was displayed; and the Japanese section, with its wooden map that included parts of China. Hur also weighs the rumors of embezzlement against Huang Kaijia, the vice commissioner who built the Chinese Pavilion, and finds the charge uncorroborated. The chapter concludes that the fair left a lasting image of official incompetence, which later reappeared at the Liege exposition of 1905.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Rydell's World of Fairs (1993) sets the two Belgian expositions of 1930 side by side: Antwerp displayed Belgium's imperial conquests, while Liège emphasised the industrial potential of the country. It also notes that Chicago's fair executives made visits to European fairs, including Liège, but were most struck by the Paris colonial fair of 1931. Rydell's wider point, made through Antwerp, is that modernism and colonialism were 'two sides of the same coin'.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

Ganz's book is a history of A Century of Progress, the 1933-34 Chicago fair, which was privately financed during the Depression to revive the city's economy and reputation. Its argument is that the organisers used cooperative science exhibits and the idea of progress to generate optimism, presenting technology rather than people as the driver of progress, while Midway entertainment, women, African Americans, ethnic communities and aviation pressed their own meanings on the fair. Chapters move from the Sally Rand shows and the Chicago boosters who financed the fair to its architecture and corporate exhibits, then to women's spaces, the Du Sable cabin, ethnic pavilions and the Graf Zeppelin and stratosphere balloon, and end with an epilogue on the fair's objects and afterlife.

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

Wikipedia

The Liège International Exposition (French: Exposition internationale de Liège) of 1930 was a world's fair held in Liège, Belgium, between 3 May and 3 November 1930, to mark the centenary of Belgian independence. The Liège exposition was one of two simultaneous fairs: one in Liège focused on industry and science from 1830 to 1930 and one in Antwerp concerned with colonial and maritime themes. The two expositions are the more specialised type of world's fairs that Belgium hosted in addition to its hosting of 6 larger expositions. The official name for the Liège exposition was the Exposition internationale de la grande industrie, sciences et applications, art wallon ancien ("International Exposition of Large Industry, Sciences and Applications, Art of Old Wallonia"), though it is also known retroactively as the Exposition Internationale de Liège ("Liège International Exposition").
