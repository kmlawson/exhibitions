Japan-British Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Japan-British Exhibition ran at the White City, Shepherd's Bush, London, from 14 May to 29 October 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 3). It was a joint venture between the entrepreneur Imre Kiralfy and the Japanese government under Komura Jutaro, which paid for the Japanese side (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 54). Japan used it to present itself as an imperial power and as Britain's equal ally, with displays from Taiwan, Korea and Manchuria (Hurp. 146; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83). Its colonised peoples on show, including Ainu and Taiwanese performers, make it a case for studying empire at world's fairs (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 69).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Japan and Britain were bound by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and official speeches before and during the exhibition called the two countries the "Island Empires" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 1). Hotta-Lister reads that phrase as a claim to equal footing, and as a reflection of the colonial roles both countries held (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 2). The Japanese government's aims were mainly diplomatic: to ease anti-Japanese feeling in Britain and to support the Alliance, with trade and loans as secondary goals (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187). Tariff revision was pending, and Japan needed City loans to settle its trade deficit with Britain (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). Komura had also proposed in 1908 that the London show advertise the Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1917, and Hotta-Lister says that plan was still current in 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). Korea was annexed on 22 August 1910, while the exhibition was open (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 23). Hotta-Lister suggests Komura may have seen the exhibition as a platform for justifying annexation, though the government did not use it directly to promote it (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 86). One source differs on the date: Greenhalgh's index gives 1911, while his notes give 1910 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 240; Greenhalgh 1988p. 110).

Organisers and finance

Kiralfy initiated the exhibition from the British side. Hotta-Lister says he was motivated by profit but inspired by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, as the Entente Cordiale had prompted his Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187). The author argues that the plan depended on members of the British Empire League, who wanted exhibitions that promoted empire (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187). The Japanese side was financed by the state. A bill for 1.8 million yen passed the Diet in January 1909, and a further 280,000 yen was approved in January 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 54; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 55). Taiwan's government added 120,000 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 54), and the appendix gives total expenditure as 2,080,000 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222). The British government gave moral support, not money (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187). The Duke of Norfolk presided over the British Exhibition Committee (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 1). Wada and Beppu were the commissioners in London (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 48), and Mutsu Hirokichi is described as the Japanese government's Commissioner (Hurp. 147). A Tokyo municipal councillor later complained that the government paid 800,000 yen in rent and received no share of profit, and blamed Kiralfy for overshadowing the Japanese officials (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 133).

Empire and colony

The Palace of the Orient was the government's main imperial display. It presented Korea as if already annexed, beside Taiwan and the South Manchurian Railway (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83). Korea was shown through the Residency-General, models of houses and schools, and a hospital in Seoul, all meant to show Japanese rule improving Korean life (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83). Hotta-Lister argues that the Palace was meant to show the progress of these areas since they came under Japanese rule, in contrast with their former primitive state (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 84).

Taiwan was the largest colonial display. It had more than 70 exhibits, including a model of Takow Harbour, and the Taiwanese colonial government's exhibits, with a life-like model of an aboriginal village, took up half the Palace (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 84; Hurp. 146). The South Manchuria Company used its display to encourage City loans and to attract tourists to Manchuria (Hurp. 146). A Daily Telegraph article of 16 May 1910, quoted in Mutsu Hirokichi's collection of British press reactions, said: "Japan has now won her place as a Colonial Power, and an elaborate exhibit shows what she has accomplished in Manchuria, Korea, and Formosa." (Hurp. 147; Hurp. 147). Greenhalgh quotes the Official Guide describing the Japanese Colonial Palace: "show what they have accomplished in their colonies and in Formosa, Korea" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 74). He also calls the Taiwanese village the last village that a foreign power brought to Britain as part of an imperial display (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93).

Hotta-Lister argues that the Japanese government deliberately presented its colonial subjects, as British and French exhibitions had done (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 84). Japan followed suit in including villages of colonised people, while Britain in 1910 included only an Irish village and a New Zealand pavilion (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). The author suggests that Komura, who had watched the Franco-British Exhibition's colonial displays in 1908, saw Japan as a member of the imperial club (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 85; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 86). The exhibition, on this reading, was a platform for Japan's imperial mission, and it was used to ease tension before the annexation was announced (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 86).

Exhibits and attractions

Some 235 entertainers and artisans came from Japan, and the Ainu and native Taiwanese were counted among them. The Exhibition Company took over their management and salaries (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 69; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 69). The Times reported the Ainu party's arrival at Albert Docks in April 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 71). The Japanese gardens and the Chokushimon gate, later re-erected at Kew, drew British praise (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 3; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 126). Sumo wrestlers were also sent to show the national sport (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 118). The Ainu left a message of thanks to the British public, translated by Bronislav Pilsudski (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 133).

Visitors and reception

The estimated number of visitors was more than 8,000,000 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 3). Hotta-Lister reports that attendance was far above that of the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 111). Most British newspapers praised the Japanese fine arts, gardens and buildings. Some critics judged them by European standards and called them barbaric or childlike (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 125; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 126). Japanese opinion was largely hostile. Japanese correspondents in London, including one for the Mainichi Dempo, described the Japanese side-shows as a national disgrace (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 132). The Diet member Kurahara Korehiro told the Diet in January 1911 that the Ainu, Taiwanese, sumo and artisans made Japanese life look humble, and that the exhibition had aggravated anti-Alliance feeling in Britain (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 111). Hasegawa Nyozekan's diary judged that a village of such low standing "could only exist at the Japan-British Exhibition" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 145). A Japanese visitor to London in 1910 found that many Britons thought Japan part of China (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 6).

Politics and controversy

Hotta-Lister argues that the Japanese elite's complaints followed from Kiralfy's approach. Kiralfy wanted popular attractions, while the Japanese authorities wanted to show Japan's history and modern systems with dignity (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 190). The author adds that Japanese critics accused the government of deception in presenting a joint venture when Kiralfy and the Japanese government had organised it (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187). The elite's anger at the Ainu and Taiwanese displays is explained as the sense that these people were fellow citizens, and that the display exposed Japan's backwardness (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192).

On Brussels, Hotta-Lister says Japan decided against exhibiting at the Brussels exhibition of 1910 to concentrate on London (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 107). Hur gives a fuller account. The Japanese government had decided not to take part officially, but when the Chinese pavilion at Brussels was praised, Japanese exhibitors complained, and Prince Fushimi went to Belgium to make the Japanese section official (Hurp. 145; Hurp. 145).

Its place in the series

The exhibition used the White City buildings of the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 and the Imperial International Exhibition of 1909 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 5). Greenhalgh says the few villages of 1910 were probably due to Kiralfy planning larger peopled displays for the Coronation Exhibition of 1911 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 93). Geppert places the 1910 show in a series of bi-national White City exhibitions that followed the 1908 pattern, contrasting Britain with another nation, and lists the Latin-British Exhibition of 1912 and the Anglo-American Exhibition of 1914 after it (Geppert 2010p. 117). Hur notes that Japan's Taiwan Pavilion became a standard feature after the Osaka exhibition of 1903, including at the Japan-British Exhibition (Hurp. 73). Hotta-Lister calls the exhibition the first in which Japan took part in such a major event, not repeated or surpassed until Osaka 1970 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 186). The book also compares the exhibition with the Great Japan Exhibition of 1981/82 and the 1991 Japan Festival in London (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 193).

How historians have interpreted it

Hotta-Lister argues that the exhibition was on balance a success, though the diplomatic results were limited (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 190; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192). Greenhalgh argues that empire became visible in Japanese display only after the Russo-Japanese War, and that the British had to adjust their phrenological arguments about race when Japan became an imperial power (Greenhalgh 1988p. 74; Greenhalgh 1988p. 96). Hur reads the exhibition through the Chinese press, which saw Japan's new status with anxiety (Hurp. 147). Peterson argues that Japan's displays at expositions were staged to match a Western image of a quaint and picturesque Japan (Peterson 2020p. 65). Hotta-Lister and Greenhalgh read the same Official Guide statement differently. Greenhalgh treats the claim of similarity as a case of racial theory being adjusted (Greenhalgh 1988p. 96). Hotta-Lister reads it as Japan's effort to present its people as racially acceptable allies, and argues that the roles of coloniser and colonised were reversed in this exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 177).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The Japanese commission brought performers, artisans, Ainu people and Taiwanese people to London (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 69). Japanese elite visitors reacted with embarrassment. Hasegawa Nyozekan found the Japanese entertainments embarrassing, although he was proud of Japan becoming one of the imperial powers with colonies (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 145; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 146). The painter Miyake Katsumi wanted nothing to do with the exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 132).

Chinese readers saw it from a different position. The Shanghai paper Shenbao judged it the most critical event of 1910 for world politics, commerce and international relations, and its article was mainly about China's anxiety about catching up with Japan (Hurp. 147; Hurp. 147). The same paper objected to the Manchurian exhibits in the Palace of the Orient as a sign of Japanese ambition toward Chinese territory (Hurp. 147). The exhibition is also important for Japanese-Chinese rivalry at Brussels, where the Chinese pavilion was praised (Hurp. 145; Hurp. 145).

Japanese decorative goods were another Asian presence. Peterson notes that Japanese vendors won awards at the 1910 exhibition, as they had at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900 (Peterson 2020p. 73).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — The Palace of the Orient and the colonial villages made the exhibition an imperial show, with Korea displayed as already annexed (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117).
  • Island Empire and equality — The Japanese and British speakers called both countries Island Empires, and the author reads the phrase as a claim to equal footing (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 1; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 2).
  • Ainu and Taiwanese people on show — The Ainu and Taiwanese performers were the displays that Japanese elites found most embarrassing, and the author ties this to Japan's place in the imperial club (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 69; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 191).
  • Race and similarity — The Official Guide stressed similarity with the British, and Greenhalgh and Hotta-Lister read this claim in different ways (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 177; Greenhalgh 1988p. 96).
  • Chinese reception — The Chinese press saw the exhibition as a sign of Japan's new status and a cause for concern about China (Hurp. 147; Hurp. 147).
  • Commerce and loans — Trade and City loans were part of the aim, but the author argues that the exhibition had a limited effect on exports and finance (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 187; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192).

Questions for study

  1. Why did Japanese elites object to the Ainu and Taiwanese displays when British audiences accepted similar village displays? Does the author's answer about Japan as a late imperial power fit the evidence? (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 191; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 191)
  2. How far did the Korean displays in the Palace of the Orient prepare opinion for the annexation? Hotta-Lister and the Japanese government's own publications suggest different answers (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 86).
  3. Greenhalgh and Hotta-Lister both quote the Official Guide's similarity claim. Why do they read it so differently? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 96; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 177)
  4. Why did Chinese newspapers see the exhibition as a warning about China, when Japanese newspapers saw it as a success? (Hurp. 147; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 190)
  5. Did the exhibition help Anglo-Japanese relations, and by what measures? (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 202)

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hotta-Lister introduces the 1910 exhibition at the White City in London, which ran from May to October and drew over eight million visitors, and places it within the history of nineteenth-century international exhibitions. The study's argument is that the Japanese government saw the event as a national undertaking meant to educate the British public about Japan and to pursue political and economic aims. Quotations from ministers and ambassadors call the two countries 'Island Empires', and the author reads this language as a claim to equal footing. The introduction then surveys the Great Exhibition of 1851, the spread of thematic and imperial displays, and the Franco-British exhibition of 1908 that used the same site. It sets out the book's plan: background on Anglo-Japanese relations, preparations, the opening period, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath. It also notes that the book is not a report on the exhibition itself, and that the final chapter compares it with the 1991 Japan Festival.

In the contents of this work

1Background of the Time (1905-10)p. 9Asia

Hotta-Lister sets out the diplomatic and economic setting for the exhibition in the years 1905 to 1910. Japan had become a great power after the Russo-Japanese War, and its legations in London, Washington and other capitals were upgraded to embassies between 1906 and 1908. The chapter then traces strains in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which British newspapers and officials increasingly questioned over Manchuria, the 'open door' pledge and Japanese immigration. It goes on to tariff revision, which Komura pursued against British objections, and to Japan's trade deficit and reliance on foreign loans. Its last sections cover the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the rivalry between Katsura and Saionji. It also surveys the Japanese and English-language newspapers on which much of the study relies, including The Times, the Japan Weekly Mail and the Chuo Shimbun. The chapter concludes that these pressures explain why the government was so committed to the exhibition.

In the contents of this work

2Preparationsp. 38Asia

Hotta-Lister shows how deeply the Japanese government committed itself to the London exhibition and how far the two sides differed in approach. The idea came from the entrepreneur Imre Kiralfy, who had staged imperial spectacles and organised the White City site; Komura took it up in Tokyo, while Kato, Mutsu Hirokichi and the commissioners Wada and Beppu handled it in London. The chapter reconstructs the earlier rejections of Kiralfy's plan and the rivalry with possible American and Russian exhibitions. It then covers the 1.8 million yen budget, the exhibitors' association, the selection of 19,943 articles from 1,286 exhibitors, and the gardens, models and entertainers shipped from Japan. British responses were lukewarm: the Board of Trade and Grey gave little official support, while a General Committee under the Duke of Norfolk formed and the Japan Society was given its own space. It ends with the opening on 14 May 1910, two days late after the death of Edward VII.

In the contents of this work

3Exhibition Objectivesp. 74Asia

This chapter asks why the Japanese government sponsored the Japan-British Exhibition and what it hoped to gain. Hotta-Lister divide the aims into political and diplomatic, economic and educational groups. The political case is tied to three issues then pending with Britain: revision of Japan's commercial tariffs, renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and the annexation of Korea, which took place in August 1910 while the Exhibition was open. Working through speeches, newspapers and diplomatic correspondence, the authors argue that Komura and Katsura saw the Exhibition as a platform for Japan's imperial role, and they set the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial exhibits of the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908. The economic section covers City loans, exports to Britain and the trade imbalance. The educational section shows Japanese leaders trying to correct British views of Japan as warlike or backward, through the book Japan Today and The Times Japanese Edition. The chapter ends with these publications.

In the contents of this work

4From the Opening Day Until the End of the Exhibitionp. 100Asia

This chapter narrates the Exhibition from its opening on 14 May 1910 to the receipt of the official reports in 1912. Hotta-Lister estimate attendance at over eight million and describe the evening illuminations, fireworks and package tours that drew crowds. Most of the chapter is a dated record of visits and events: Prince Fushimi's attendance at King Edward's funeral, banquets for British and Japanese dignitaries, Japan Day, and visits by Kitchener, Marie Stopes, the Swedish royal couple and the Brazilian president-elect. It also records a private viewing of retrospective Japanese art for about 350 European experts. The authors then trace the afterlife of the exhibits, which were shipped home, given to British and European institutions, or sent to exhibitions in Budapest, Dresden and Turin. Little is argued here; the chapter is a record of ceremony and diplomacy that frames the reactions discussed next.

In the contents of this work

5Reactions to the Exhibitionp. 110Asia

This chapter weighs the reception of the Exhibition in Britain and Japan. Hotta-Lister conclude that it did not change British attitudes toward Japan in diplomacy or trade, though publications about Japan rose sharply in 1910 and 1911. The British press praised the Japanese fine arts, gardens and wooden buildings, including the Chokushimon gate later re-erected at Kew, while some critics judged them by European standards as barbaric or childlike. Japanese opinion was largely hostile. Elite residents in London were embarrassed by the Japanese Village, the Ainu and Formosan displays and the sumo wrestlers, and their complaints reached the Diet, where Kurahara and Nishimura attacked Komura and the promoter Kiralfy. The authors read the colonial displays as part of a common imperial genre. The chapter ends with British manufacturers doubting that exhibitions paid, and with a 1910 proposal for a British-only exhibition in Japan.

In the contents of this work

6Aftermath of the Exhibitionp. 153Asia

Hotta-Lister asks whether the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London was, as Japanese opinion held, a failure, and tests its effects in economic, financial, diplomatic and racial terms. On trade, the author sets Japanese and British statistics beside the exhibitors' sales figures to show that Japanese exports to Britain fell overall in 1910 and 1911, while matting, raw silk, shell buttons and toys rose, and treats the Exhibition as one plausible contributor. Loans get the same caution: the government's conversion loans and the municipal and South Manchurian Railway issues were raised largely through the usual banks, so the Exhibition at most reinforced existing confidence. The chapter then follows the tariff dispute, the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in July 1911, drawing heavily on British press reactions. Its racial discussion centres on the Official Guide's claims of similarity between Japanese and British people and on Japanese complaints about Chinese and Ainu displays. It concludes that the Exhibition had only a limited effect, though it was not the failure that Japanese critics claimed.

In the contents of this work

7Some Concluding Reflectionsp. 186Asia

Hotta-Lister gives an overall verdict on the 1910 Exhibition, concluding that it was on balance a success rather than the failure of Japanese opinion. The argument rests on the partnership between Imre Kiralfy, the commercial entrepreneur, and the Japanese government under Komura, which the author sees as the seed of later controversy in the Diet. Komura's aims were mainly diplomatic, to ease anti-Japanese feeling and support the Alliance, with trade and loans as secondary goals, and the proposed Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1917 is shown to have been a further reason for the venture. The Japanese negative reviews are traced to Kiralfy's popular attractions and to the elite's embarrassment at Ainu and village displays, which the author compares with exhibitions in the United States. The closing pages set the 1910 event beside the Great Japan Exhibition of 1981/82 and the Japan Festival of 1991 in London, which shared a wish to correct misunderstanding of Japan but differed in scale, funding and educational follow-up. The author concludes that the Exhibition helped turn the Alliance into a link between peoples, though only to a limited extent.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

In the contents of this work

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Introduction: communities real and imagined: world’s fairs and political meaningsDavid Raizman and Ethan Robeyp. 1

Raizman and Robey introduce the volume by asking how the world's fairs of 1851 to 1915 used manufactured goods to express national identity. They argue that earlier design history treated fairs as a foil to modernism, and that the fairs were networks of exchange rather than single, unified displays. Pevsner's dismissal of the Crystal Palace is challenged by later critics, from Banham and Sparke to Greenhalgh, Bennett and Mitchell, the last three reading the fairs as imperial spectacle. The editors use Anderson's 'imagined communities' to tie national identity to material culture. Their evidence is the Albert Memorial, Owen Jones's interior and Grammar of Ornament, and the contributors' cases: the 1888 Copenhagen Nordic Exhibition, the Nanjing and South American centenaries of 1910, Italy at Brussels and Antwerp, and the Finnish pavilion at Paris in 1900. They end with the outbreak of World War I, when the ideal of 'peaceful competition' broke down.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
Ch. 3 Imperial display, pp. 55, 74 and 80 (notes); ch. 4 Human showcases, pp. 92 and 96; notes 19-20 and 55; index (l. 12478-12479).

Greenhalgh treats the 1910 exhibition as the point where Japan's imperial pride became visible at a world's fair. In his survey of empire displays he describes the 'Japanese Colonial Palace' and the Taiwanese 'Formosa Hamlet', and he shows the British adjusting their phrenological arguments about race when they staged the exhibition. His larger claim, from Ephemeral Vistas, is that human displays taught mass audiences to see non-European peoples as inferior, and the 1910 case is used to show that racial theory was adapted when the exhibiting power was itself a rising empire.

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. Wikipedia

The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 (日英博覧会, Nichi-Ei Hakuran-kai) took place at White City, London, from 14 May 1910 to 29 October 1910. It was the largest international exposition that the Empire of Japan had ever participated in. The exhibition was driven by the Japanese government's desire to shake off disparaging views of Japan in the Western world, legitimize Japan's rising prestige as an imperial power in Asia, and to generally develop a more favorable public image in the West following the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance. Japan also hoped that the display of manufactured products would lead to increased Japanese trade with Britain. The formal annexation of Korea occurred during the exhibition in August 1910, and was celebrated with a lantern procession on the site.
