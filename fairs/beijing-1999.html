Background and aims

China joined the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) as a late-comer in 1993, and it had less success than Korea in bending the BIE's rules (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The plan for 1999 was to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the People's Republic with an exposition in Beijing (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The authors say the rules held that time, and China's only option was an International Garden Expo, for which a slot existed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Site and exhibits

The event moved to the city of Kunming, which the authors describe as a semi-tropical centre of a growing horticulture industry and a centre of botanical studies (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). It had 35 international gardens in a park-like setting, where Chinese traditional gardening skills were shown (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Countries contributed small indoor exhibits, 66 in total, in a new exhibition complex (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The authors say the horticultural conferences were of high international standard, and that the event opened the eyes of many Western visitors to south-west China (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The organisation used magnetic entry tickets and electronic gates (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Why Kunming, and what followed

The authors approve of the move: "Wisely, given the hostile climate of Beijing, the event was moved to the city" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). They call the event a small beginning for China's ambitions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The authors write that "The success of Kunming Expo 99 whetted China’s appetite" to move into the big league (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Plans were in preparation for a major event in Shanghai in 2010, in competition with Korea and others (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Korea's competing project was for Yosu City, on oceanology (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

The chapter places the Beijing plan directly after its account of Taejon 1993, where Korean pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The authors read Kunming and the Yosu project as continuing the Asian use of the exposition as a tool of economic and political national and regional development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Expositions as national development — the authors treat Kunming and the Yosu project as part of the Asian use of the exposition for economic and political national and regional development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).
  • BIE rules and China's bid — China's late BIE membership, and rules that left only a garden expo for 1999 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).
  • Garden expositions — Kunming's 35 gardens, indoor exhibits from 66 countries and horticultural conferences (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).
  • Prestige and the next event — the Kunming result as a spur to a Shanghai event in 2010, against Korea (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Questions for study

  1. What did the "hostile climate of Beijing" refer to, and does the passage say whether it was political, administrative or physical? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170)
  2. The plan was tied to the fiftieth anniversary of the People's Republic (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). What did a garden expo offer in its place, and what does the passage say about its aims?
  3. How far does a garden expo prepare a host city for a full exposition, as the authors suggest for Shanghai (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170)?
  4. Compare Kunming with Taejon 1993, where the Korean pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). What did each host's displays say about its nation?

Roche, Maurice. Mega-Events and Modernity: Olympics and Expos in the Growth of Global Culture. London: Routledge, 2000.
7Mega-events and global citizenship: Olympic problems and responsesp. 194Asia

Roche asks whether the Olympic movement lives up to ideals of global citizenship, and sets out four dimensions to test it: universal citizenship based on human rights, mediatised citizenship of audiences, movement citizenship of inclusion and democracy within sport, and corporate citizenship in global governance. The chapter first describes the movement's universalist ideology, the self-recruiting IOC and its growing ties with the UN, UNESCO, the ILO and the WHO. Against this it sets a record of host states with repressive governments, from Mexico 1968 to Seoul 1988, and the IOC's failure to take a firm line on China's bid. It then examines media problems, including the commercialisation of Olympic TV, the drift of rights to pay television, and the limits of internet access. Drug abuse, from East German state doping to Ben Johnson and the 1998 cases, is treated as a failure of IOC testing. The bidding corruption crisis, the Pound Inquiry and the 1999 reforms are followed by the Olympic Truce agreed with the UN. Roche concludes that the IOC's credibility remains doubtful until its reforms are rigorously enforced.

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.

Allwood, Allan and Reid's chapter on Eastern Hemisphere expositions from 1975 to 1999 gives the planned Beijing exposition of 1999 a short passage. The plan was to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the People's Republic with an expo in Beijing, but the authors say the BIE's rules left China only one option, an International Garden Expo, which was moved to Kunming in Yunnan. They present the Kunming event as a small but effective start to China's ambitions and as the step that led to a planned Shanghai event for 2010. The wider argument is that Asian states used the exposition as a tool of national and regional economic and political development.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.
