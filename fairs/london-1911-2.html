Festival of Empire

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Festival of Empire was a five-month imperial exhibition and pageant held from 12 May to 28 October 1911 in and around the Crystal Palace at Sydenham, south London (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). Planned for 1910 as Heart of Empire under the pageant master Frank Lascelles, it was postponed after the death of Edward VII (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). Its officials were Forbes Dennis, Frank Lascelles and H. W. Matthews (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 389). The sources present it as a display of imperial self-sufficiency and a pageant of empire, and Hoffenberg treats its pageant as a case of imperial participation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211; Hoffenberg 2001p. 245). Its losses bankrupted the Crystal Palace Company (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 212).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The Festival was meant to honour a coronation. The dictionary's entry opens with the words "To celebrate the coronation of King George V and to reassert imperial solidarity", and places the event on the eve of the First World War (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). The encyclopedia says it was first planned for 1910 and was postponed when Edward VII died two weeks before its opening, so that it became part of the 1911 coronation celebrations for George V (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). Hoffenberg takes the Festival as the end point of his study of exhibitions in England, Australia and India, which begins with the Great Exhibition of 1851 (Hoffenberg 2001).

Site and buildings

The Festival covered the whole Crystal Palace and its park at Sydenham (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). The Palace held an All-British Exhibition of Arts and Industries, redecorated for the event, with a Women's Work section (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). An amphitheatre designed by the architect Aston Webb was built for the pageant. The encyclopedia gives it seats for 8,000, at a cost of £7,500 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). Hoffenberg and the dictionary give 10,000 seats (Hoffenberg 2001p. 267; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215).

Empire and colony

The Festival's imperial content centred on the All-Red Route. The encyclopedia describes two-thirds scale models of nearly 300 buildings from across the Empire, including parliament buildings, linked by a miniature railway. It says the Festival was designed to celebrate the connections between empire, state and crown (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). The dictionary says the dominion parliament buildings were replicas at two-thirds size, that other colonies appeared through big-game exhibits or historical tableaux, and that South Africa showed diamonds worth $2 million, lent by De Beers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). Canada showed a miniature wheat field, fruit, grain pictures and butter sculptures. Its parliament building was the most popular and stood for a second year (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). The tour passed a South African diamond mine, a Jamaican sugar plantation, a jungle with wild animals, a bazaar and an Indian tea plantation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211).

South Australia's Journal of Industry urged local manufacturers to exhibit, and told readers that every product needed in one part of the Empire could be made in another (Hoffenberg 2001p. 100).

Colonised people were treated differently in the two kinds of display. Hoffenberg says the commissioners kept the old ethnographic entertainment alive with a Giants of Empire competition, in which colonial subjects were types and human exhibits rather than equal participants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 220). In the pageant, Britons, Canadians, Australians, South Africans and South Asians alternated in the acting (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266). The pageant had four parts of eight scenes. It ended with the overseas dominions gathered around the mother country, and its scenes included the Treaty of Waitangi (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266). The closing Imperial Masque set out the advantages of empire in allegory (Hoffenberg 2001p. 268).

Who took part

Counts of the performers differ. The encyclopedia gives 15,000 volunteers from the London boroughs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). The dictionary gives 10,000 actors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215), and Hoffenberg more than fifteen thousand local residents and colonial visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 245). Sydenham residents played the opening scene, Beginnings of Empire, and Hackney residents played the Ancient Britons (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266). Indian visitors and ladies performed for the King and Queen (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266).

Visitors and reception

On opening day, 12 May, 144,234 people attended, possibly hoping to see the newly crowned King George V (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211). The dictionary gives the same figure (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). Crowds stayed large through the summer, despite the smaller Coronation Exhibition at Shepherd's Bush (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215). The radical Clarion attacked the Festival as part of the coronation year's pageantry, and said that adolescent seamstresses hired to make the ceremonial costumes were exploited (Hoffenberg 2001p. 97). M. P. Noel, a pageant player, kept a scrapbook of photographs, leaflets and her own impressions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 269). The post office at Sydenham cancelled letters with a stamp bearing the Festival's name (Hoffenberg 2001p. 269).

Money and aftermath

Costs ran above estimates. The encyclopedia says that preparations for 1910 cost £70,000 against an estimate of £49,000, that a further £50,000 was estimated for 1911, and that receipts were estimated at £119,000, though no record of actual receipts survives (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 212). The losses, it says, brought about the bankruptcy of the Crystal Palace Company, and the Palace passed into public ownership in 1913 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 212). The dictionary says the Company went bankrupt after the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 216). The Palace burned down on 30 November 1936 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 216).

When the British Empire Exhibition was planned for Wembley, the planners could have reused the Festival's pavilions. They chose a new site, first and foremost for financial reasons, and the Palace was already in decline (Geppert 2010p. 144). The encyclopedia's Wembley entry calls the Festival "largely unsuccessful" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231).

Its place in the series

The encyclopedia gives the Festival's chief legacy as the first sporting contests between teams from Britain and the dominions, called Inter-Empire Games, which it says laid the foundation for the Commonwealth Games (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that after 1911 many people felt a nationally organised Empire exhibition on the full international scale was appropriate, and that the First World War overtook the plan (Allwood et al. 2001p. 97).

How historians have interpreted it

Hoffenberg reads the pageant as a history that "suggested a unity and destiny in the unfolding of an invented past" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 267). He argues that its scenes offered an imagined nation and empire to a crowd that took part in them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 267). Susan Bennett, writing in the encyclopedia, reads the Festival's legacy in sport (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). The dictionary's entry, signed at its end by John E. Findling, says historians have not paid the Festival much attention (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 216).

Indian arts drew visitors to the Festival. Hoffenberg says that visitors continued to buy India's exotic art at the Franco-British and Festival of Empire shows, where Australia's displays were more utilitarian (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159). His bibliography lists a guide book and catalogue for the Indian Court at the Festival (Hoffenberg 2001p. 394). Indian visitors took part in the pageant (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266), and South Asians alternated in the acting (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266). The encyclopedia places an Indian tea plantation along the All-Red Route tour (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial spectacle and the All-Red Route — The Festival put dominion parliaments, colonial products and colonial scenes on one tour of models, and the texts list the colonies one by one (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211); (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215).
  • Pageant and invented history — Hoffenberg reads the pageant as a participatory history in which treaties and trade were set against a history of violence (Hoffenberg 2001p. 267); (Hoffenberg 2001p. 268).
  • Colonised people as types and as actors — In the Giants of Empire display colonial subjects were human exhibits, but in the pageant they acted alongside Britons (Hoffenberg 2001p. 220); (Hoffenberg 2001p. 266).
  • Trade and self-sufficiency — Organisers and the South Australian press presented the Festival as proof that the Empire could supply its own needs (Hoffenberg 2001p. 100); (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211).
  • Money and failure — Costs exceeded estimates, and the Crystal Palace Company went bankrupt after the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 212); (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 216).
  • Memory and souvenirs — Scrapbooks, souvenir books and a special post office stamp kept the Festival in memory (Hoffenberg 2001p. 268); (Hoffenberg 2001p. 269).

  1. The sources give different counts for the pageant's performers and for the amphitheatre's seats. How would you decide which figure to trust? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 215; Hoffenberg 2001p. 245; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211; Hoffenberg 2001p. 267)
  2. Hoffenberg describes colonial subjects as types in the Giants of Empire display, but as actors in the pageant. Does taking part make people more equal, or does it still place them in a hierarchy? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 220; Hoffenberg 2001p. 243)
  3. The encyclopedia describes two-thirds scale models of the dominion parliaments, while Allwood, Allan and Reid give three-quarter size. What might the choice of scale have meant for visitors' view of the colonies? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 211; Allwood et al. 2001p. 97)

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
4Human showcasesp. 82Asia

Greenhalgh examines the practice of displaying people at international exhibitions between 1889 and 1914, when colonised and other non-Western peoples were housed in reconstructed villages and watched by paying crowds. He sorts the displays into four types (imperial, educational, commercial and ambassadorial) but argues that they overlapped, and that the imperial type was the most numerous and lavish. The chapter works through the Paris 1889 and 1900 native villages, the Jardin d'Acclimatation's peopled exhibits that preceded them, Imre Kiralfy's London spectacles, and the Chicago, Buffalo and St Louis fairs, where Native American, Filipino and Dahomeyan residents were placed within a supposed evolutionary hierarchy. Commercial examples include the Cairo Street and the Indian tea-houses, and the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes, who toured under private entrepreneurs, receive detailed attention. Greenhalgh concludes that the displays did lasting damage by teaching mass audiences that non-European peoples were inferior, and that this harm was never repaired.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
5Reactions to the Exhibitionp. 110Asia

This chapter weighs the reception of the Exhibition in Britain and Japan. Hotta-Lister conclude that it did not change British attitudes toward Japan in diplomacy or trade, though publications about Japan rose sharply in 1910 and 1911. The British press praised the Japanese fine arts, gardens and wooden buildings, including the Chokushimon gate later re-erected at Kew, while some critics judged them by European standards as barbaric or childlike. Japanese opinion was largely hostile. Elite residents in London were embarrassed by the Japanese Village, the Ainu and Formosan displays and the sumo wrestlers, and their complaints reached the Diet, where Kurahara and Nishimura attacked Komura and the promoter Kiralfy. The authors read the colonial displays as part of a common imperial genre. The chapter ends with British manufacturers doubting that exhibitions paid, and with a 1910 proposal for a British-only exhibition in Japan.

In the contents of this work

6Aftermath of the Exhibitionp. 153Asia

Hotta-Lister asks whether the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London was, as Japanese opinion held, a failure, and tests its effects in economic, financial, diplomatic and racial terms. On trade, the author sets Japanese and British statistics beside the exhibitors' sales figures to show that Japanese exports to Britain fell overall in 1910 and 1911, while matting, raw silk, shell buttons and toys rose, and treats the Exhibition as one plausible contributor. Loans get the same caution: the government's conversion loans and the municipal and South Manchurian Railway issues were raised largely through the usual banks, so the Exhibition at most reinforced existing confidence. The chapter then follows the tariff dispute, the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in July 1911, drawing heavily on British press reactions. Its racial discussion centres on the Official Guide's claims of similarity between Japanese and British people and on Japanese complaints about Chinese and Ainu displays. It concludes that the Exhibition had only a limited effect, though it was not the failure that Japanese critics claimed.

The encyclopedia gives the Festival a dedicated entry of about two printed pages, by Susan Bennett. It presents the Festival as the 1910 'Heart of Empire' plan postponed to the 1911 coronation year, describes its three parts (the Palace exhibit, the scaled-down Empire models and the Pageant of London), and records its costs and the bankruptcy of the Crystal Palace Company. Bennett describes the displays without analysing their colonial content, and she presents the Festival's chief legacy as the first Inter-Empire sporting contests, which she says laid the foundation for the Commonwealth Games. The Wembley entry calls the Festival 'largely unsuccessful' and says its pavilions were not reused, mainly for financial reasons.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. Wikipedia

Festival of Empire was a major imperial exhibition held at London's Crystal Palace from 12 May to 1 October 1911, organized to celebrate the coronation of King George V and to showcase the cultural, industrial, and military accomplishments of the British Empire. It was the largest single event held at the Crystal Palace in London since its opening. The original intention had been that Edward VII would open it in 1910, however, this was postponed after his death shortly before the planned opening day. The Festival contained a display of landscapes and exhibits from the British Empire, mainly the dominion countries, to encourage emigration to those nations; and it contained a large scale pageant dramatising British history.
