Sydney Metropolitan Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Sydney Metropolitan Exhibition of 1854 was a colonial show of New South Wales natural and industrial products, held in Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 279; Hoffenberg 2001p. 292). Its catalogue, printed in Sydney in November 1854, describes the goods as exhibited in the Australian Museum by the Paris Exhibition Commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001p. 323; Hoffenberg 2001p. 323). Two texts list it among colonial exhibitions in Australia (Cotép. 37; Hoffenberg 2001p. 292). Neither says more about its organisers, site, visitors or aims, so this page can describe only its colonial character.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The catalogue and its organisers

Hoffenberg cites the catalogue in two notes, one for colonial wine and one for the New South Wales collection (Hoffenberg 2001p. 323; Hoffenberg 2001p. 332). The bibliography gives the show the heading Sydney Metropolitan Exhibition (1854) and describes the catalogue's goods as exhibited by the Paris Exhibition Commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001p. 383; Hoffenberg 2001p. 383). The texts do not say where the goods went after Sydney, and they give no account of the building or the number of exhibitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 323).

Empire and colony

Hoffenberg places the New South Wales collection in a passage on the Paris Universal Exposition of 1855, where it is set against the East Indian court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 136; Hoffenberg 2001p. 136). The passage describes the New South Wales exhibits as "illustrating the natural resources of the country; rich as it is in mineral wealth, and in all else connected with a bounteous soil," and says useful goods predominated over ornamental ones (Hoffenberg 2001p. 136; Hoffenberg 2001p. 136). The note for that passage cites the 1854 catalogue (Hoffenberg 2001p. 332). Colonial commissioners preparing wine samples reminded jurors that Australian wines were the product of a new country. Hoffenberg reads this as settler optimism, and the note cites the 1854 catalogue (Hoffenberg 2001p. 108; Hoffenberg 2001p. 108; Hoffenberg 2001p. 323). Hoffenberg describes local shows of this kind in general terms: they were often scheduled as preliminaries to overseas international exhibitions and were sponsored by local governments and agricultural societies (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11; Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Commissioners chose objects from them to forward as official representations of the colony to major French, British and North American exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11; Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Coté calls the Australian colonies British colonies (Cotép. 36). The texts say nothing about Indigenous or colonised people at the 1854 show.

Place in the series

Hoffenberg lists Sydney shows of 1854, 1861 and 1870 among the preliminary colonial exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 292; Hoffenberg 2001p. 292). He says Sydney started the series of colonial international exhibitions in 1879 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Coté's footnote lists Sydney shows of 1854, 1861, 1870, 1873 and 1879 among similar colonial exhibitions, citing a 1995 special issue of the La Trobe Library Journal (Cotép. 37; Cotép. 37). The texts do not say that the 1854 show led to the later intercolonial exhibitions.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial goods as settler evidence — the catalogue's useful natural products are set against Indian artware, and the wine remarks are read as settler optimism (Hoffenberg 2001p. 136; Hoffenberg 2001p. 108)
  • Preliminary colonial shows — the 1854 show is listed among local exhibitions that came before overseas ones, not among the first international shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 292; Hoffenberg 2001p. 11)
  • Paris commissioners — the catalogue names the Paris Exhibition Commissioners as those who exhibited the goods; the texts do not say where they were then sent (Hoffenberg 2001p. 323)

Questions for study

  1. Hoffenberg contrasts useful New South Wales goods with ornamental Indian art; what does that contrast assume about settler colonies and subject colonies? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 136; Hoffenberg 2001p. 130)
  2. Why did the book call the local shows preliminary, and what did they prepare the colonies for? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11; Hoffenberg 2001p. 292)
  3. Footnote 7 and footnote 55 give slightly different lists of Sydney shows; which sources would show which of these years were held? (Cotép. 37; Hoffenberg 2001p. 292)
  4. The texts say nothing about Indigenous people at the 1854 show; what kind of source would show whether they were represented there?

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
Ch. 1 (p. 11); Ch. 4, 'Consumers, Producers, and Markets' (p. 108); Ch. 5, 'Terrae Nullius?' (p. 136); Appendix A (p. 279); bibliography (p. 383); index; notes 38, 47, 55

Hoffenberg's study of British, Indian and Australian exhibitions mentions the 1854 Sydney show only in passing: in an appendix, a footnote, a bibliography entry and two notes that cite its catalogue. The book lists the show among the preliminary colonial exhibitions held before overseas shows, and uses the catalogue for remarks on colonial wine and for the New South Wales collection in a passage on the Paris exhibition of 1855. Its wider argument is that overseas displays set Australia, as a settler colony, against India, as a subject one, in images of progress and tradition, and the 1854 material serves that case only as an example.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Coté's article on the Semarang exhibition of 1914 names the Sydney show of 1854 only in a footnote listing colonial exhibitions in the nearby Australian colonies, giving the year and place and nothing more.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.
