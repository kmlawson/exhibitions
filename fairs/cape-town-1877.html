South African International Exhibition

The South African International Exhibition was held in Cape Town in 1877, in the Cape Colony (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). The texts name no organisers and give no visitor figures (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). Four texts mention it, mostly in lists of exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 398; Roche 2000p. 58; Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). One of them places it among exhibitions the British Empire staged to mark the growth of its dominions and colonies (Roche 2000p. 58). The texts say little more about its purpose or content.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The record in the texts

Two reference works list the exhibition only in an appendix. The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions gives it as "Capetown" in Appendix D, "Fairs Not Included" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424). That appendix covers fairs that fell short of the qualifications for the book's main body (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). The Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions gives the same entry with no other detail (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 398).

The fullest account is in Allwood, Allan and Reid, The Great Exhibitions (2001). In a passage on the wave of exhibitions in the 1880s, the authors name the Cape Colony as host and Cape Town as the city (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). Their table gives the months open as 3; its attendance, profit and acreage cells are missing or garbled in the extracted text (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206).

Empire and colony

The texts describe the exhibition as colonial mainly through its host. The Cape Colony held it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). Roche lists it among exhibitions staged in the British Empire to mark the growth of dominions and colonies, alongside Australian, New Zealand, Indian and African examples (Roche 2000p. 58). He then quotes MacKenzie (1984) that "colonial exhibitions were a celebration of the white man’s successful transplantation to the farthest reaches of the globe" (Roche 2000p. 58). The texts say nothing about colonised or Indigenous people at this exhibition, which colonies sent exhibits, or what the organisers wanted it to do for empire, trade or settlement.

Its place in the series

The texts do not present the Cape show as the first colonial exhibition. Roche's list also includes exhibitions in New Zealand (1865) and Sierra Leone (1865) (Roche 2000p. 58). Roche calls London's Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 "the first explicitly imperial expo" (Roche 2000p. 58). Allwood, Allan and Reid quote a contemporary description of the Sydney exhibition of 1879 that lists the Cape Colony among the colonies that lent their aid (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). That was a separate exhibition; no text links it with the Cape show.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial and dominion exhibitions — Roche groups the Cape show with other British Empire exhibitions that marked the growth of dominions and colonies, and he quotes MacKenzie's view that such exhibitions celebrated white settlement (Roche 2000p. 58)
  • Host colony — the Cape Colony is named as host; no text names the organisers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55)
  • Reference lists — the two encyclopedias record the exhibition only in appendices of fairs excluded from their main texts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 398)
  • Exhibition statistics — the one table entry gives three months open, with attendance and profit missing from the extracted text (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206)

Questions for study

  1. Why do the two encyclopedias place this exhibition among excluded fairs when the texts that name it call it an international exhibition?
  2. What sources would show whether the Cape exhibition did what MacKenzie says colonial exhibitions did, celebrating white settlement?
  3. Why do the texts give no account of colonised people at this exhibition, when they discuss colonial exhibitions as displays of settler societies?
  4. How does the 1880s wave of exhibitions described by Allwood, Allan and Reid differ from the earlier colonial exhibitions in Roche's list?

Brief discussion 1

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
Ch. 5, 'Paris, Australia and all the World'; mention at l. 2889-2890; table row l. 10052; index p. 54 (l. 10542)

Allwood, Allan and Reid place the Cape Colony's exhibition in Cape Town within a wave of exhibitions that they date to the 1880s and call an epidemic. The Cape show gets one sentence, which names the host colony, city and year, and a table entry that gives its months open. The authors do not describe its exhibits, organisers or visitors, and they make no argument about its meaning; their concern is the spread of exhibitions across the British colonies.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Roche, Maurice. Mega-Events and Modernity: Olympics and Expos in the Growth of Global Culture. London: Routledge, 2000.
Ch. 2, 'Expos and cultural power: capitalism, nationalism and imperialism', printed p. 58 (l. 3227-3231)

Roche names Cape Town 1877 once, in a list of exhibitions staged in the British Empire for dominions and colonies, and takes the view that such exhibitions celebrated white settlement from MacKenzie (1984), without applying it to the Cape show.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.
