Tokyo · 1877

First National Industrial Exposition

第一回内国勧業博覧会 (Dai-ichi-kai Naikoku Kangyō Hakurankai)
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The First National Industrial Exposition (Daiichi Naikoku Kangyō Hakurankai) was the first of the Meiji government's national domestic industrial expositions. It was held at Ueno Park in Tokyo from 21 August to 30 November 1877 (Lawn 2009p. 74; Filipová 2015p. 334), while the Satsuma Rebellion was being fought (Filipová 2015p. 334). The texts give its attendance as 500,000 in three months (Filipová 2015p. 334) or as more than 450,000 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 220). Its success led the state to declare a domestic exposition every four years (Filipová 2015p. 334). Every text that mentions it treats it as held.

Where the dates and figures come from, and where the texts disagree

Visitors conflict: 500,000 (Filipová 2015) against 'more than 450,000' (Hotta-Lister 1999). One Hur mention gives no year and says 1877 is from general knowledge; the dates rest on Lawn 2009 and 06. Lawn 2009 says Tokyo is inferred; Filipová 2015 and 16 name Tokyo.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Hur places the exposition within Japan's experience abroad. Sano Tsunetami, Japan's commissioner to the Vienna Exposition, committed himself to launching domestic exhibitions (Hurp. 41). Members of the Iwakura Embassy who saw Vienna, among them Itō Hirobumi and Ōkubo Toshimichi, also promoted them on their return (Hurp. 41). Hur states that "four years later in 1877, Japan held its first Domestic Encouragement of Industry Exhibition in Tokyo" (Hurp. 41), and argues that the leaders' enthusiasm came from Vienna (Hurp. 41).

Daykin sets the exposition in an earlier line of Japanese exhibiting. A Western-style exposition held in 1872 under the Ministry of Education launched the Tokyo National Museum and collected items for Vienna (Filipová 2015p. 334). Daykin notes that the 1877 exposition went ahead as planned during the Satsuma Rebellion, which, according to Noriko Aso, cost the government about 80 per cent of its annual budget (Filipová 2015p. 334). Daykin reads the early domestic expositions as serving the government's slogan shokusan kôgyô, encouraging domestic production and enterprise (Filipová 2015p. 334).

Organisers and finance

Hotta-Lister describes the five large domestic exhibitions held before 1910 as official government shows, administered by the Ministry of Home Affairs and generally running for three to four months (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). The same passage says that private companies, public bodies and newspaper companies organised most domestic exhibitions of the period (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). The passages do not name who ran the 1877 exposition. Komatsu says only that design sketches prepared by the Exhibition Bureau for craft wares were used for the first and second Domestic Exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 74).

Site and buildings

The 1877 exposition had a building of its own. Hotta-Lister states that, unlike earlier shows in existing buildings, a new exhibition building was erected specifically for the first National Industrial Exhibition at Ueno Park in 1877 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). A footnote in Hur gives the Tokyo site as approximately 25 acres, against 82 acres for the Osaka site of 1903 (Hurp. 137; Hurp. 48). Komatsu records that the Educational Museum, which opened on 18 August 1877 (Lawn 2009p. 76), also received a new building in Ueno Park (Lawn 2009p. 76).

Exhibits and classification

The exposition followed the classification of the 1876 Philadelphia exhibition, except that the third division, which the Philadelphia scheme called Education and Knowledge, became Fine Arts, and educational exhibits were placed under the second division, Produce (Lawn 2009p. 74). Hotta-Lister lists the categories as mining, metallurgy, manufacture, fine arts, machinery, agriculture and horticulture (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 220). Daykin reports a regulation that foreign goods could be shown only by exposition officials, while Japanese goods, including copies of international products, were shown by their makers (Filipová 2015p. 334).

Visitors and reception

The attendance figures differ. Daykin gives 500,000 visitors in three months (Filipová 2015p. 334). Hotta-Lister says the exhibition is believed to have drawn more than 450,000 people from all over Japan (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 220). Daykin measures Osaka's 1903 exhibition against it: on Osaka Day, with 93,464 paid admissions, the Osaka exposition reached about one fifth of the 1877 total (Filipová 2015p. 343). Komatsu's table records 176,748 spectators at the Educational Museum in 1877 (Lawn 2009p. 79); that is a count for the museum, not for the exposition.

Its place in the series

Daykin says the second national exposition took place as planned in 1881, the third was postponed to 1890, and the fourth was held in Kyoto in 1895 (Filipová 2015p. 334). Hotta-Lister gives the second as 1882 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). Hur also dates it to 1881 (Hurp. 47). Hur describes the domestic series as a sprout of a future world exposition in Japan (Hurp. 47), and calls the Osaka exposition of 1903 the fifth and last domestic exhibition, which became semi-international (Hurp. 47).

Aftermath

Komatsu says that in 1881 the showroom for the second Domestic Exhibition was changed to show only domestic products (Lawn 2009p. 80). The Educational Museum was amalgamated with the Tokyo Library in 1885, and its building went to the Tokyo Art School (Lawn 2009p. 81). Hotta-Lister says that a shop called Kankojo sprang up around Ueno after the first domestic exhibition closed and sold its unsold exhibits at reduced prices (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).

How historians have interpreted it

Four authors interpret the exposition or its setting in different ways. Daykin reads the domestic series through the aim of shokusan kôgyô (Filipová 2015p. 334). Komatsu, citing Kuni, argues that exhibitions converted people's sense of value and drove Japan towards a modern nation-state, with control of manufacturing passing to the central government (Lawn 2009p. 75). Hur attributes the Meiji leaders' commitment to the domestic exhibitions to their experience at Vienna (Hurp. 41). Hotta-Lister says only that the national exhibitions helped to promote enterprise (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Domestic exhibition series and state policy — the state set the 1877 exposition as the first of a series, to be held every four years, with later postponements (Filipová 2015p. 334).
  • Models from Vienna and Philadelphia — the Meiji leaders' experience abroad is presented as the source of the domestic plan (Hurp. 41; Hurp. 42).
  • Classification, display and imitation — the exposition used the Philadelphia classification, and the Educational Museum showed foreign objects beside their domestic copies (Lawn 2009p. 75; Lawn 2009p. 78).
  • Education and the Educational Museum — the museum opened three days before the exposition began and was stocked largely from international exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 76).
  • Attendance and scale — the texts differ on the 1877 attendance and compare it with Osaka in 1903 (Filipová 2015p. 334; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 220; Filipová 2015p. 343).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the government go ahead with the 1877 exposition during the Satsuma Rebellion, and what did that choice reveal about its priorities? (Filipová 2015p. 334)
  2. How far did the Educational Museum's display of foreign objects beside domestic copies serve the aim of promoting domestic production? (Lawn 2009p. 78; Filipová 2015p. 334)
  3. The texts give 500,000 and 450,000 visitors, and 1881 and 1882 for the second exposition. How should a reader decide between these figures?
  4. Hur links the exposition to Vienna and Philadelphia, while Daykin links the series to shokusan kôgyô. Which account better explains why the government held the exposition at all?

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Formation and Transformation of Education in Japan through Exhibitions: focused on the Educational Museum founded in 1877Kayoko Komatsup. 73Asia

Komatsu asks how international and domestic exhibitions helped to modernise education in early Meiji Japan, using the Educational Museum that opened in Ueno Park in 1877 as the main case. Japanese participation in the Vienna (1873) and Philadelphia (1876) fairs and the first Domestic Exhibition supplied the museum with foreign apparatus and books, many bought at fairs and displayed beside Japanese copies made from them. Komatsu uses the museum's guidebook, visitor figures and departmental reports to show how this arrangement pointed Japan towards European materials, and how private makers were encouraged to imitate European physics and chemistry machines for sale to schools. The second half traces the museum's decline after 1885, when it was incorporated into the Tokyo Library and its building passed to the Tokyo Art School, and relates this to the replacement of the Pestalozzian 'Developmental Mode of Teaching' by Herbartian method. The conclusion presents the fairs and the museum as two 'nodes' in a network that carried modern educational materials into Japanese schools.

ch. by Kayoko Komatsu, pp. 73-79 (sections 'Exhibition and Education in Japan' and 'The Educational Museum'; printed page numbers 74, 76-78 visible at ll. 3454, 3562, 3617, 3669)

Komatsu's chapter is about the Educational Museum, which opened in Ueno Park on 18 August 1877, three days before Japan's first Domestic Exhibition began. The author treats the exhibition as one of the state's instruments for making Japan a modern nation, and as the occasion on which the museum was founded and stocked. The argument is that exhibitions converted people's sense of value, and that the museum's display of European originals beside Japanese copies showed visitors which direction Japan should take. The chapter gives the exhibition's classification and its design sketches, but it does not describe the exhibition's own buildings, exhibits or visitors.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

Brief discussion 3

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
ch. 15 by Jeffer Daykin, 'International Ambitions of an Exhibition at the Margin', section 'Japan's Domestic Expositions', pp. 333-335; comparison p. 343

Daykin's chapter on the 1903 Osaka exposition opens with a few paragraphs on the earlier national domestic expositions and names the 1877 Ueno Park exhibition only as the first of them. Daykin presents the series as a route by which Japan's domestic fairs gradually took on international ambitions. Later Daykin measures Osaka against 1877: on one day in 1903 Osaka's paid admissions were about a fifth of the 1877 total. For Daykin the 1877 fair is a starting point and a yardstick for the argument that Osaka was a marginal international exposition, not a subject in its own right.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
running head 'Japan's participation in exhibitions', pp. 219-20

Hotta-Lister's book on the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition begins its account of Japanese exhibiting with a short survey of the government's domestic series. The 1877 Ueno Park exhibition is described as the first national industrial exhibition, held in a purpose-built building and arranged by category, and the survey notes the shops that sold its leftover goods. The author argues only that the national exhibitions helped to promote enterprise. The passage is descriptive, offers no interpretation of the 1877 fair itself, and gives the fair's visitor figure (more than 450,000) as an estimate.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
Ch. 1 ('One'), pp. 41-42 (Vienna and Philadelphia passage); Ch. 2 ('Two'), pp. 46-47 (opening section I and note 99); note 294, p. 137

Hur's dissertation on China at the world exhibitions uses the 1877 Tokyo exhibition as a Japanese benchmark and as the first step in Japan's domestic series. In Chapter One the author argues that Japan's experience at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876 led Meiji leaders to launch domestic exhibitions, and that members of the Iwakura Embassy were inspired at Vienna. In Chapter Two, on Osaka in 1903, the Tokyo exhibition of 1877 appears as the smallest of the domestic series that Osaka outdid. The 1877 fair is background to the author's case about China, and the texts give it no further analysis.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
