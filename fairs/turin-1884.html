General Italian Exhibition (Turin)

Esposizione Generale Italiana
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Esposizione Generale Italiana was a national industrial and artistic exhibition held in 1884 in the Parco del Valentino in Turin (Della Coletta 2006p. 21; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 53). Tommaso Villa led its executive committee (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 69). The texts say it confirmed Turin as Italy's industrial capital and began mass attendance at Italian fairs, with a record three million visitors (Della Coletta 2006p. 21). Its reception led Italy to join the 1885 Antwerp fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55). None of the texts gives its opening or closing dates.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Turin lost its role as capital in 1864 (Della Coletta 2006p. 15). Della Coletta argues that the city went on presenting itself as Italy's moral and intellectual core, and used national exhibitions to make that claim (Della Coletta 2006p. 16). The 1884 fair was proudly defined as the revenge of the Turinese twenty years after the capital moved to Florence (Della Coletta 2006p. 21).

The minister of agriculture, Luigi Miceli, said in his inaugural speech that expositions were complete reviews of all that civilised societies produce (Della Coletta 2006p. 22). The official guide called the fair a dress rehearsal for a future international exhibition, but the texts do not say which one was meant (Della Coletta 2006p. 23). Villa, whom Della Coletta calls the grand patron of many of Turin's expositions (Della Coletta 2006p. 46), planned a Mostra del Risorgimento that asked the provinces for documents covering the period from the start of the Revolution to unification (Della Coletta 2006p. 46). The response exceeded expectations, and a special pavilion was built for it (Della Coletta 2006p. 46). Villa placed a reproduction of the Temple of Vesta in front of the pavilion, which Della Coletta reads as a challenge to Rome's right to be the capital (Della Coletta 2006p. 46). Prina describes the Padiglione del Risorgimento as a display of a modern Italy, with industrial galleries and a theatre (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54).

Site and attractions

Turin chose the Valentino park for symbolic as well as practical reasons. Della Coletta describes it as an idealised, man-made pleasure ground (Della Coletta 2006p. 24). Pavilions and kiosks in the gardens were lit by electric light (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). Della Coletta calls the 1884 fair the first Italian exposition to use electric light in some areas, and says it had a large International Electricity Exposition section (Della Coletta 2006p. 29). Saragat admired a Mongolian-style kiosk and a Chinese-style pagoda restaurant with a tower 22 metres high (Della Coletta 2006p. 26). A funicular ran to the Superga hill, which Della Coletta reads as a pilgrimage to the House of Savoy (Della Coletta 2006p. 27).

The Artistic Section, designed on the basis of archaeological study by Alfredo d'Andrade, offered a reconstructed medieval village in the park (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). Prina says its buildings were faithful copies of structures in Piedmont and the Valle d'Aosta (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). Its workshops, in pottery, blacksmithing, weaving and carpentry, let visitors watch production (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54).

Della Coletta gives a different account of the castle. Boito, whom Della Coletta quotes, explained that d'Andrade had assembled elements from numerous Piedmontese castles (Della Coletta 2006p. 40). Costumed pharmacists, potters and a weaver worked in the village's shops (Della Coletta 2006p. 39). Boito praised the result: "The work of at least a century is redone in a few months" (Della Coletta 2006p. 41). Smith names the Castello di Fenis as the building reproduced at the 1884 Turin exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 207). The texts therefore do not agree on what the castle copied. The castle was not demolished after the fair and became a feature of the park (Della Coletta 2006p. 39).

Visitors and reception

Della Coletta gives a record total of three million visitors and a daily average of between eleven and twenty thousand, depending on the source (Della Coletta 2006p. 21). A Turin publisher issued a weekly illustrated chronicle of the fair (Della Coletta 2006p. 21). Belgian visitors, quoted by Prina, called the exhibition brilliant and said that Italy had provided itself with "a complete and perfected apparatus" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). Saragat criticised the lack of well-informed guided visits to the exhibition (Della Coletta 2006p. 278).

Empire and colony

The colonial side of the fair is small and rests on few passages. Della Coletta says that the villaggio Assabese was one of the most visited attractions at the 1884 exposition (Della Coletta 2006p. 94). He argues that it gave a way to test Italian opinion on the colonial plan that Prime Minister Agostino Depretis had just begun to unveil (Della Coletta 2006p. 94). A note adds that Italy declared sovereignty over the Bay of Assab in June 1882 (Della Coletta 2006p. 293). The texts do not say who lived in the village, how they came to Turin, or how they were presented.

Robustelli, reviewing the 1884 exposition, praised its "Mostra Africana" (Della Coletta 2006p. 294) as small but eloquent, and urged Italy to test its strength by conquering the African desert (Della Coletta 2006p. 294). Geppert lists Abbattista's article on Turin in 1884 among studies of people shown as living exhibits (Geppert 2010p. 296). The bibliography gives the article's title as Torino 1884: Africani in mostra (Geppert 2010p. 360). Geppert does not discuss its content. The texts record no organisers' statement of aims for empire, trade or settlement at this fair (Della Coletta 2006p. 24).

Art, industry and the state

Prina presents the 1884 exhibition as part of a state programme linking art, industry and education. The Artistic Section was followed by a royal decree of 29 November 1884 that created royal delegations for the conservation of monuments (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). A decree of 23 October 1884 set up the Central Commission for Artistic and Industrial Education, to improve industrial art museums, organise schools of applied art and extend drawing lessons to primary schools (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55). Prina says the exhibition showed that a leading Italian goal was to recover an artistic supremacy that Italy had not held among its European neighbours since the seventeenth century (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54).

Aftermath and series

The favourable reception encouraged the Italian government to join the 1885 Antwerp fair, which it saw as a chance to re-launch and re-brand Italian artistic and industrial production (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55). Camillo Riccio, who had designed the Turin buildings, decorated the Italian section at Antwerp, and its 665 producers were mostly chosen from among the Turin prize-winners (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 57; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 69). After the fair the Town Hall gave the village to the Civic Museums of Turin, which made it a separate branch of the museum (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). The 1902 decorative arts exposition stood on the same Valentino site, which had earlier housed expositions in 1884 and 1898 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 169). Geppert places the Turin castle among three mid-1880s replicas, after which the retrospective principle spread to later fairs (Geppert 2010p. 52).

How historians have interpreted it

Della Coletta treats the fair as part of Turin's nation-building, describing fairs in general as consensus-building machines and reading the medieval village as a polemical reworking of the Piedmontese late Middle Ages against Rome (Della Coletta 2006p. 32; Della Coletta 2006p. 40). Prina treats it as the first stage of a programme that linked art, industry and education to a national style (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54). Wilson Smith classes the castle as a signature building, distinct from historic-city reconstructions (Filipová 2015p. 207). Geppert places it at the start of the retrospective principle (Geppert 2010p. 52).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts name no Asian sections, commissioners, performers or people on display at the Turin fair. The only Asian reference is architectural: a Mongolian-style kiosk and a Chinese-style pagoda restaurant with a 22-metre tower (Della Coletta 2006p. 26).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Nation building and Turin's rivalry with Rome — Della Coletta sees the fair as Turin's claim to lead the nation, through the Risorgimento pavilion, Villa's collection of documents and the Temple of Vesta (Della Coletta 2006p. 46; Della Coletta 2006p. 46).
  • The medieval past as display — the village is treated as a faithful, idealised reconstruction by Prina and as a composite by Della Coletta, with Smith placing the castle among signature buildings (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54; Della Coletta 2006p. 40; Filipová 2015p. 207).
  • Industry, spectacle and mass attendance — the texts stress record crowds, electric light and novel attractions, which Della Coletta reads as tools of consensus (Della Coletta 2006p. 21; Della Coletta 2006p. 26; Della Coletta 2006p. 29).
  • Art, industry and state education — two royal decrees of October and November 1884 tied the fair to new institutions for conservation and art education (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 54).
  • Colonial display — the villaggio Assabese and the 1884 Mostra Africana are the only colonial material, and the texts do not say how the people shown were presented (Della Coletta 2006p. 94; Della Coletta 2006p. 294; Geppert 2010p. 296).

Questions for study

  1. Della Coletta argues that pleasure and an idealised past made industrial progress and monarchy seem natural. Is there evidence in the texts that visitors accepted that message, or only that they came in large numbers?
  2. The only colonial evidence is one village and one critic's review. What other sources would show how the villaggio Assabese was staffed and presented?
  3. The texts disagree on what the castle copied. Which reading better explains the castle's later survival in the park?
  4. The Guida called the fair a dress rehearsal for a future international exhibition. Did the Antwerp fair of 1885 meet the ambitions the texts attribute to Turin's organisers?

Della Coletta, Cristina. World’s Fairs Italian-Style: The Great Expositions in Turin and Their Narratives, 1860–1915. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2006.
1Prologues to World’s Fairs: National Expositions and Nation Building in Turinp. 15

Della Coletta argues that Turin's decline after 1864, when it lost the capital to Florence and then Rome, has been overstated. The city kept presenting itself as the moral and industrial core of Italy, and it used national expositions to do so. The chapter traces this through the Turin fairs of 1850, 1858 and especially 1884, held in the Valentino park, whose pleasure gardens, exotic kiosks, toboggans and electric illumination are read as tools for turning industrial display into popular amusement. Strikes and revolts at the Palermo, Milan and Turin fairs of 1891 to 1898 show the limits of that consensus. The chapter also reads De Amicis's guide Torino 1880 and Manzotti's ballet Excelsior as versions of the same message. The argument is that fairs fused nationalism, bourgeois consensus and a Western story of progress. The chapter ends by setting Turin's rivalry with Rome against the wider nation-building programme of the expositions.

Prina examines how Italy and Belgium, two young nation-states, each tried to link art, industry and education, and how the Italian government used foreign models to reform its own system. The starting point is a mission of 1880 by Baldassarre Odescalchi and Raffaele Erculei, who inspected the Brussels Jubilee Exhibition and the art-industry institutions of Belgium. The chapter then follows Italy's display at the Turin exhibition of 1884, notably the Borgo Medievale that Alfredo d'Andrade built as a recreation of medieval Piedmont, and its large showing at the Antwerp world's fair of 1885, where 570 of the 665 Italian exhibitors received awards. A Belgian architect, Théophile Fumière, wrote a book on the Italian section that praised Italian ceramics and the regional school system as a model for Belgian design reform. Prina ends with the Brussels decorative arts school and museum of the 1880s and with Italian interest in Belgian religious art and Art Nouveau, leading towards the Turin decorative arts exhibition of 1902.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This book argues that Italy's exhibitions between 1860 and 1915 were tools of nation building rather than imitations of French and British fairs. Della Coletta reads the fairs as consensus-making machines that repeated ideas about national identity, imperial competition and progress, while their counter-narratives survived as silences and contradictions. Turin, which sponsored more national and international fairs than any other Italian city, is the main case: chapters cover the Turin fairs from 1850 to 1911, including 1911, which marked fifty years of unity. Two chapters then read texts that carry exposition habits into literature, Emilio Salgari's adventure novels and Guido Gozzano's writing on India and the 1911 fair. An introduction and a conclusion frame the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present but not central. Their fullest treatment is chapter 3, Emilio Salgari: Writing Exposition Style, where the Malay archipelago, Borneo and Siam supply the settings and ethnic inventories of the jungle novels, and Sandokan's Borneo kingdom, destroyed by British and Dutch allies of the sultan, is read against Garibaldi's campaigns. Here the concern is fiction and the European ranking of peoples, not exhibitions. Chapter 1 mentions a Chinese-pagoda kiosk at the 1884 Turin fair. Chapter 2, Turin 1911, has the most exhibition material: Pourtauborde's Oriental fairground, which evoked China, Siam, Japan and Indochina as 'Oriental peoples'; the Filipino villages at the 1904 Saint Louis fair, cited as a model for 'native' displays; and Barzini's Beijing-to-Paris race reports, read as a fair-like image of a motionless East. Chapter 4 touches Japan only through war photographs in illustrated magazines. Asia appears as a theatre of display and fiction, never as exhibitor: no Asian state, commissioner or merchant is discussed, no fair held in Asia is considered, and Asian observers barely appear. South Asia gets much more space, through Gozzano's India letters in chapter 4, which lie outside this question. The colonial sections of Turin 1911 concentrate on Eritrea and Somalia, so Southeast Asia is reduced to a list.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
