The texts record an international exposition held in Amsterdam in 1895. The two encyclopedias list it as the "International Exposition" in appendices of excluded fairs, and Geppert gives it as the Wereld-Tentoonstelling (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400; Geppert 2010p. 251). The texts say very little more. Geppert's only substantive statement is that the retrospective principle was developed and popularised there (Geppert 2010p. 52). Organisers, site, exhibits and visitors are not described (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Name, date and status

The texts agree on the year and city. Both reference works place the fair in appendices of fairs that fell short of the qualifications for their main entries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). Geppert's appendix list gives "Wereld-Tentoonstelling. Amsterdam" for 1895 (Geppert 2010p. 251). Geppert treats the fair as having taken place, since he names it among expositions where the principle was popularised (Geppert 2010p. 52).

The retrospective principle

Geppert links the principle to three earlier fairs: a replica of a medieval castle at Turin in 1884, an "Old London Street" at London in 1886, and a replica of the Bastille at Paris in 1889 (Geppert 2010p. 52). After them, he writes, it was developed and popularised at Bremen (1890), Antwerp (1894), Amsterdam (1895) and Berlin (1896) (Geppert 2010p. 52). He uses "old villages" for Old London, Vieux Paris, Vieil Anvers and Alt-Berlin, and does not apply that term to Amsterdam (Geppert 2010p. 52).

Empire and colony

The texts show no imperial or colonial side to the 1895 exposition. The colonial exhibition the texts name at Amsterdam is a separate fair of 1883, the Internationale Koloniale en Uitvoerhandel Tentoonstelling (Geppert 2010p. 251).

What the texts do not record

The texts give no organisers, finance, site, attendance or reception for the 1895 fair. The reference works give only its year, city and name (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400), and Geppert's chapter gives it one sentence (Geppert 2010p. 52).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Retrospective principle — Geppert counts Amsterdam 1895 among the fairs where the principle was developed and popularised, with Berlin 1896 following (Geppert 2010p. 52).
  • Precursor fairs — Geppert traces the principle to Turin 1884, London 1886 and Paris 1889 (Geppert 2010p. 52).
  • Self-reference of the medium — Geppert argues that by 1896 the exhibition medium was more concerned with itself than with its socio-political context; the passage is about exhibitions in general, not Amsterdam (Geppert 2010p. 52).
  • Reference-work coverage — both encyclopedias record the fair only in appendices of fairs excluded from their main entries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395).

Questions for study

  1. Geppert names Amsterdam 1895 as a site where the retrospective principle was popularised but gives no detail of it. What other evidence would a student need to test that claim?
  2. Why do the reference works list the 1895 fair only in an appendix of excluded fairs, and what does that imply about how they defined an international exposition?
  3. How did the retrospective principle spread from one exhibition to the next, and what did organisers gain by copying it?

Brief discussion 1

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
Ch. 2, Berlin 1896 chapter, p. 52 (l. 2752-2754); appendix list, p. 251 (l. 10962)

Geppert's chapter on Berlin 1896 uses the Amsterdam exposition of 1895 only as one case in a wider history: the 'retrospective principle', which re-created old quarters inside exhibitions, was developed and popularised at several fairs, including Amsterdam and then Berlin. He traces the device back to Turin 1884, London 1886 and Paris 1889. His larger argument is that by 1896 the exhibition medium was more concerned with its own self-reference than with its host society's politics. Amsterdam receives no further treatment, and his appendix list gives only its name, city and date.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
