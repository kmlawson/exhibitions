International Garden and Greenery Exposition (Osaka 1990)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Garden and Greenery Exposition, Osaka 1990, was a horticultural fair in Osaka, Japan, scheduled for 1 April to 30 September 1990 on some 340 acres of parkland (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409). The Bureau of International Expositions registered it as a specialised exposition in June 1986 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe a garden festival that Japan turned into a hybrid world exposition, which drew over 23 million visitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Its organisers were Japanese (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The authors note that nothing could be done about the change, and that no rules had been broken (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The texts give the title in several forms. The dictionary calls it Osaka 1990: International Garden and Greenery Exposition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409). The encyclopedia's appendix lists it as the International Horticultural Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427). Allwood, Allan and Reid call it Expo 90 and Osaka Expo 90 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165) and in their table give the theme as Horticulture — Man and Nature (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210).

The dictionary describes the fair as scheduled, with the aim of helping visitors to appreciate the value of gardens and greenery to human life (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409). The dictionary was first published in 1990 (Findling & Pelle 1990), so its dates are plans, not a record of what happened. Allwood, Allan and Reid say only that the gardens were maintained for six months (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Allwood, Allan and Reid place the fair after Expo 70 at Osaka. They write that Expo 70 was a revelation to Japan, and that Japan then became a determined bidder in a nationally controlled programme for BIE slots, devising new variants when slots were unavailable (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161). When the BIE calendar was full, Japan used the category of international garden expositions, which had been pioneered in Holland and Germany and which limited foreign participants to providing a garden (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Osaka proposed a horticultural spectacle, and the proposal received a fair hearing (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Site, buildings and theme

The authors describe the site as a rundown 140-hectare city park, Tsurumi-Ryokuchi, whose redevelopment was to be a legacy for the crowded city (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The texts disagree on size. The dictionary gives some 340 acres (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409), and the table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives 250 under acreage (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210).

Japan's ambition went beyond the gardens agreed with the BIE. The authors say the result was a hybrid expo, in which domestic pavilions in full expo style were added to the international greenery (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The Japanese Government Complex, a series of interrelated pavilions and gardens designed under Shinya Izumi, took up the theme of the pursuit of harmony with nature in the 21st century (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The Great Conservatory, contributed by Osaka city, was a 6,900 square metre structure with eight climatically controlled zones, and it became a permanent feature of the site (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Because of Japanese concern about over-commercialisation, most exhibitors took the Expo theme of the symbiosis of man and nature (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Many gave the theme a serious environmental treatment, using new audio-visual methods (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Participants and visitors

The authors say 82 nations were featured in varying degrees (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Many countries evaded full cooperation by passing the funding and provision of a garden to states, provinces, cities or private sector groups (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Hawaii, California, Chicago and San Francisco provided the US garden, and Paris represented the French (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The table gives 70 international participants (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210). The two counts differ, and the texts do not explain the gap.

The authors say the flamboyant pavilions of Japanese industry were the main attraction for a record number of over 23 million visitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The table gives 23.1 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210).

Organisers and the BIE

The authors say the BIE debated the Japanese invitation in 164 capitals, while 123 diplomatic missions sent from Tokyo were politely received, but nothing could be done (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). They also say that no rules had been broken, and that the organisers were punctilious in respecting the rights of international exhibitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Visitors and reception

Allwood, Allan and Reid judge the fair as follows: "Osaka’s Garden and Greenery Expo was not a great exhibition, in comparison with some." (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). They still give it credit for its management. The authors say the disadvantaged, children, family groups and foreign visitors were given a clean and welcoming experience, which set high standards in handling mass audiences (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Profit and legacy

The substantial profits were dedicated, through a commemorative foundation, to promoting the Expo theme internationally (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). One annual project is the award of the Cosmos Medal and prize to a scientist judged to have given outstanding service to understanding the interrelationship of man and nature (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166).

Its place in the series

Allwood, Allan and Reid place the fair in a sequence. Okinawa 1975 was the first project in Japan's programme (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161). Kobe Portopia 81 invited seaports that traded with Kobe to take part, which the authors say side-stepped the BIE convention (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Tsukuba Expo 85 aimed to make Tsukuba a science city (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Osaka 1990 then used the garden category (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

The authors write that "only Japan could turn a garden festival in Osaka in 1990 into a hybrid world exposition and get away with it" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). China later used the same category. The BIE line held against a Beijing expo for 1999, and China's option was an International Garden Expo at Kunming (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The authors report 35 international gardens and 66 countries that contributed small indoor exhibits (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Themes in the scholarship

  • State-led expo strategy — the authors present Osaka 1990 as part of a nationally controlled programme that sought BIE slots and devised new variants when none were free (Allwood et al. 2001p. 161p. 165).
  • Garden category and BIE rules — the garden category limited foreign participants to a garden, and the authors say nothing could be done about the fair, since no rules had been broken (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).
  • Hybrid expo — domestic corporate pavilions in full expo style stood among international gardens, and the pavilions of Japanese industry were the main attraction (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).
  • Nature and the environment — the Expo theme of the symbiosis of man and nature and the Japanese Government Complex's theme of harmony with nature (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165); the dictionary's planned aim was to show the value of gardens and greenery (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409).
  • Management of mass audiences — the authors praise the handling of crowds and of disadvantaged, child, family and foreign visitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).
  • Profit and legacy — the profits went to a foundation, and permanent gardens remain in Tsurumi Park (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Questions for study

  1. The authors say no rules were broken, yet the fair was a world exposition under a garden category. Where does a garden exposition end and a world exposition begin? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165)
  2. The authors praise Osaka's management and its record attendance. Does a record count of visitors show what a fair achieved for its host city? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165)
  3. The texts give three different site figures: some 340 acres, 140 hectares, and 250 acres in a table. What evidence would settle which is correct? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 409; Allwood et al. 2001p. 165; Allwood et al. 2001p. 210)
  4. Kunming's International Garden Expo of 1999 used the same category as Osaka. How far did it follow the Osaka model? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170p. 197)

Allwood's chapter covers the exhibitions held in Japan, Australia and Korea between 1975 and 1999, and argues that Japan turned the medium into a state-led tool of regional and economic development, which its neighbours then copied. It opens with Okinawa 1975, where an ocean theme and the Aquapolis floating platform were set on a former battlefield, and moves through Tsukuba 1985, which used computers, robots and audio-visual display to promote a science city. Its main case is Osaka 1990, a garden exposition that the organisers turned into a hybrid expo within the BIE's rules, with 82 nations and a record attendance. The chapter then surveys Brisbane 1988, whose shaded riverside site and state pavilions are described, Taejon 1993, where Korean corporate pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors, and the Kunming 1999 garden expo. It ends with the Philippines' withdrawal from a recognised exposition for Manila in 1992, and concludes that developing nations were still denied a realistic route to hosting.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

On the web

Wikipedia

Expo '90 (国際花と緑の博覧会, Kokusai Hana to Midori no Hakurankai), formally The International Garden and Greenery Exposition, was the first large-scale international horticultural exposition in Asia. Organized as a part of the International Expositions Convention, it focused on the theme of the "Harmonious Coexistence of Nature and Mankind." The exposition was held in Tsurumi Ryokuchi, Osaka for 183 days, from Sunday, April 1 to Sunday, September 30, 1990. The convention attracted 23,126,934 visitors. One of its main activities was to establish the annual International Cosmos Prize. It was recognized by both the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) and the International Association of Horticultural Producers.
