Festival of Britain

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Festival of Britain was a national exhibition held from 4 May to 30 September 1951, with its central site on the South Bank of the Thames in London and a wider programme of events across Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316p. 317). The Royal Society of Arts proposed it to mark the centenary of the Great Exhibition of 1851, and the government paid for it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). Historians value it because it turned the world's fair model around: it showed one nation, Britain, to the world instead of gathering exhibits from many countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The Labour government under Clement Attlee agreed to hold and pay for the Festival and set up a department to oversee it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). The organisers decided early that it would celebrate British achievement alone, in the phrase of the time an autobiography of the nation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). The decision was tied to the economic pressures of the years after the Second World War (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316).

Allwood and colleagues place the Festival among exhibitions that helped a nation recover from war: "In each case the exhibition helped to restore national pride and faith after a period of war and upheaval" (Allwood et al. 2001). Although only national in scope, it set the scene for the international exhibitions that followed after the war (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125).

Organisation and finance

The Festival had government authority and government finance behind it, a point the book ties to Herbert Morrison (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). A panel of specialists was formed under Gerald Barry's chairmanship, and the planners had only three years from the panel's setting-up to the opening (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). The designers included Misha Black, Ralph Tubbs, Hugh Casson and James Gardner (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). Casson became the Festival's Director of Architecture and was knighted in 1952 for his work on it (Geppert 2010p. 264).

Allwood names Cecil Cooke as Assistant Chairman of the panel of specialists, a man who had been Director of the Central Office of Information since 1946 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). Peter Kneebone, the panel's secretary, was a graphic designer and illustrator (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). Badger's table gives the Festival's receipts as £2,560,000 and its cost as £11,500,000 (Badger 1979p. 131), so the cost was more than four times the receipts.

Site and architecture

The South Bank exhibition stood on the south side of the Thames, with the Houses of Parliament as its backdrop, and was built around 27 pavilions on every aspect of British life (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). Its site covered 29 acres, against the 1,216 acres of the 1939 New York World's Fair, so the designers used informal, meandering routes rather than the grand axes of earlier fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The landscaping drew on the picturesque, an eighteenth-century English style of landscape made to look like a painting (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317).

Two structures were singled out: Ralph Tubbs's Dome of Discovery, described as the largest dome in the world, and Powell & Moya's Skylon, a 320-foot aluminium tower (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). The Dome stood at the centre as a symbolic brain, and the Hungerford railway bridge divided the two main themes (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). The small site also produced what the Architectural Review named townscape: buildings whose exteriors reflected their displays, arranged to shape how crowds moved (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125).

Allwood gives the South Bank site as 27½ acres, a figure that differs from the 29 acres given above (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The scan prints the fraction as a percentage sign, so the figure should be checked against the printed page. The Skylon was flanked by the Dome of Discovery and, on the near side of the railway line, by the Royal Festival Hall (Allwood et al. 2001p. 124). This qualifies the statement above that visitors could enter in any order. The planners aimed to show the sections in sequence, using water, cobbles, grass or concrete underfoot to steer people (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). The guide book nonetheless welcomed visitors who went through the story backwards (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). Felix Samuely, a British structural engineer, designed the space-frame roof over the Pavilion of Transport. In a lead article for Architectural Forum in 1953 he wrote that he felt himself "on the eve of a great revolution" in building (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 262). Geppert says the planners brought the spectacle back into the city, with a riverside waterfront London had lacked for two centuries (Geppert 2010p. 224).

Exhibits and the national story

Each display told an aspect of a British story, and words, images and objects carried the message, a legacy of wartime government poster design (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). Ian Cox, who compiled the theme, divided the story into three sections: the land of Britain, the people of Britain, and British discoveries and invention (Allwood et al. 2001p. 123). Visitors could enter the displays in any order, which the book says gave the designers major advantages (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125).

Outside London, the Festival ship Campania, a converted aircraft carrier, carried part of the story around Britain, and two touring exhibitions went by land and sea (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Allwood et al. 2001p. 124). Benedict helped bring a painted canal boat to London to be moored on the Thames as an example of British tradition (Benedict 1983).

Nations, empire and peoples on show

The Festival showed one country. Its exhibits came from Britain alone and were presented without reference to the British colonies or Commonwealth (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). Grosvenor argues that representations of the Empire or Commonwealth were almost absent, and that the People of Britain section imagined an all-white, self-contained nation (Lawn 2009p. 124). Grosvenor links this to the schoolchildren who saw the 1924 Wembley exhibition and later took their own children to the Festival (Lawn 2009p. 124).

Hughes's essay names the post-war British event of 1951 the 1951 Century of Progress Exhibition, a label the other texts do not use. Of the seven British imperial exhibitions from 1851 to 1951, Hughes notes, all but this one included a live display of colonial people (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62).

Grosvenor carries his argument beyond the Festival. He links the children who saw Wembley in 1924, and their own children at the Festival, to Enoch Powell's 1968 speech on immigrant children (Lawn 2009p. 124). He finds in Powell a definition of belonging already present in the Weekly Empire Bulletin (Lawn 2009p. 124). Hughes says that after the First World War anthropological displays were no longer part of the edifying content of imperial shows (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62). He gives two reasons: uncritical enthusiasm for the Empire fell away after the war (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63), and from 1919 the Foreign Office faced independence movements in India and Egypt (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). They fit the Festival's omission of a live colonial display, though Hughes does not apply them to 1951.

Cinema and national image

John Grierson commissioned the documentary Family Portrait (1950) as a companion to the Festival, and it served as a worldwide advertisement for attracting audiences to it (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364). Because of cuts to the Festival budget, the film was also promoted as representative of British documentary around the world, according to Logan (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364). It was sent to twenty-three countries as a sanctioned advertisement for a new national self-image (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364).

The Festival was conceived as a corrective to economic hardship, asking Britons between 1945 and the early 1950s to forgo material plenty for an abstracted common good (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 367).

In Glasgow, the Exhibition of Industrial Power at the Kelvin Hall was held to coincide with the Festival. Limond calls it the most overtly didactic show in Glasgow between 1888 and 1988, built around progress through benevolent technology (Lawn 2009p. 193). Attendance stayed under a third of a million, which Limond partly blames on high ticket prices and on workers' reluctance to spend their leisure admiring the products of their own labour (Lawn 2009p. 195).

Jennings and the editor Stewart McAllister cut rapidly between places and times, so that Family Portrait presents the nation as a family whose inventions run from Drake's battles to radar (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 367). The film's official story, in its own list, is "tolerance, courage, faith, discipline" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 367). Kevin Jackson reads the same film as an optimistic materialist history of technology (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 368). Flanagan and the encyclopedia date it to 1950 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 363; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318), but a caption in Flanagan's chapter dates it to 1951 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 368).

Visitors and reception

About 13 million people visited events across Britain, and over 8 million visited the London exhibitions. More than one in three Britons were estimated to have attended (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). Badger's table gives 18,000,000 visitors for London 1951, which does not match the encyclopedia's figure (Badger 1979p. 131; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The organisers later admitted they had failed to make the Festival a true celebration of the whole country, because the central London events were the most popular (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317).

Legacy and afterlife

The Festival's design was heavily referenced at the Brussels expo of 1958, and a so-called Festival style of atomic-pattern textiles and products was both emulated and vilified (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). Architects and planners treated its exhibitions as a pattern book for national reconstruction, especially for the New Towns (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The suspended Yugoslav pavilion at Brussels 1958 was read against landmarks such as the Skylon, a cable-stayed tower erected for the Festival (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 233).

The appendix table records that the Dome of Discovery and Skylon did not survive, and gives the redevelopment of the South Bank as the residual legacy (Geppert 2010p. 260). Roche argues that the Millennium Dome of 2000 echoes the Festival's central dome, letting New Labour reconnect with the Labour Party's role in post-war reconstruction (Roche 2000). Allwood and colleagues judge the 2000 Greenwich Dome to have achieved the worst, not the best, of the 1851 and 1951 models (Allwood et al. 2001p. 190).

The Kelvin Hall show

Basil Spence planned the halls of the Exhibition of Industrial Power with the writer Alastair Borthwick. The themes were power, coal, steel, electricity, railways and shipbuilding, irrigation, and a Hall of the Future (Lawn 2009p. 194). Visitors saw a Davy safety lamp, a suit of armour from the Tower of London and a 28-ton diesel shunting locomotive (Lawn 2009p. 194). The Guide-Catalogue begins the Hall of Coal in a coal swamp 220 million years old, and its model pits show hauling and trapping done by female and child labour (Lawn 2009p. 195). A Tesla coil, Limond notes, was there to be seen throwing sparks that the catalogue imagined arcing into the future (Lawn 2009p. 194). The catalogue also warned that nuclear energy, used unwisely, could bring "complete extinction" (Lawn 2009p. 195). Limond places 1951 at the height of a techno-progressive narrative (Lawn 2009p. 196). He sees an upward curve in educational aspiration from the 1880s to the 1950s, followed by decline (Lawn 2009p. 196).

Publicity and films

Four London buses were shipped to mainland Europe early in 1951 for an advance marketing tour (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The organisers spent $100,000 on the first four-page full-colour advertisement in Life magazine, inviting Americans to come to Britain in 1951 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). The BBC archives hold around 2,700 Festival broadcasts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). Films made for the Festival include Festival in London (Crown Film Unit, 1951) and The Magic Box (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). Hugh Casson and Patrick O'Donovan are credited with Brief City, a short valedictory film (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 318). Geppert quotes Casson in it saying "this exhibition to be part of London" (Geppert 2010p. 224), but his footnote names the directors as Maurice Harvey and Jacques B. Brunius, which conflicts with the encyclopedia's credit (Geppert 2010p. 324).

How historians have interpreted it

Findling and Pelle's encyclopedia gives the Festival an entry by Atkinson, which argues that the Festival kept the international exhibition form but subverted that tradition to suit post-war Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317). Atkinson's entry calls it a new type of exhibition: "The Festival marked the emergence of a new type of exhibition that turned the world’s fair model on its head, by putting a nation state on show to the world." (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316)

Allwood and colleagues read the Festival as a national morale project that nonetheless set the scene for the first post-war international exhibition, Brussels in 1958 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). Geppert reads it as a turn in London's exhibition geography. After decades of drift to the suburbs, planners saw the redevelopment value of such an event, and in Geppert's words, "They decided to bring the spectacle back into the city center" (Geppert 2010p. 224). Geppert also places the Festival among the European exhibitions that offered visitors explicit journeys into the future (Geppert 2010p. 245).

Flanagan reads Family Portrait as official propaganda, a voice that declares a newly insular celebration despite Britain's declining power (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 368). Flanagan also reads the film as an argument for compromise: "Family Portrait is a film which takes the Festival of Britain as an occasion to argue in favor of compromise (or synthesis) as a way of life." (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 368) Grosvenor and Limond both read the Festival's displays as part of a larger story about nation and progress, and Limond concludes that the Glasgow show offered science as salvation (Lawn 2009p. 124p. 195).

The readings agree that the Festival broke with the world's fair model and made a nation rather than the world its subject (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Allwood et al. 2001p. 125). They differ over its purpose. Atkinson and Allwood stress recovery and morale, Flanagan stresses propaganda and compromise, and Geppert stresses urban planning (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317; Allwood et al. 2001p. 190; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 368; Geppert 2010p. 224).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say almost nothing about an East, South or Southeast Asian presence at the Festival of Britain. Its exhibits came from Britain alone and were presented without reference to the British colonies or Commonwealth (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316). Grosvenor likewise finds representations of the Empire or Commonwealth almost absent from the Festival (Lawn 2009p. 124). The texts name no Asian pavilion, commissioner, performer or visitor, and they give no account of Asian reactions to the Festival (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Lawn 2009p. 124).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Nation on show — The Festival presented Britain alone as one national story, reversing the world's fair model. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • Exclusion of empire — Exhibits left out the colonies and Commonwealth, and the People of Britain section imagined an all-white nation. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Lawn 2009p. 124; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62)
  • Austerity and morale — The Festival was a corrective to post-war austerity and a civic gift meant to lift the nation. (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 367; Allwood et al. 2001p. 190)
  • Site and layout — The 29-acre South Bank site shaped an informal, picturesque plan and a townscape. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317; Allwood et al. 2001p. 125)
  • Attendance and reception — The encyclopedia gives about 13 million visits across Britain and over 8 million in London, while Badger's table gives 18,000,000. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317; Badger 1979p. 131)
  • Film as advertisement — Family Portrait promoted the Festival and a new national image abroad. (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364)
  • Legacy — The Festival's design was referenced at Brussels 1958, and the Millennium Dome was later said to echo its dome. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 317; Roche 2000)

Questions for study

  1. Did leaving out the empire make the Festival a new kind of world's fair, or did its absence display the empire in a different way? Compare the encyclopedia's account with Grosvenor's and Hughes's. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316; Lawn 2009p. 124; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62)
  2. How far can a film made to advertise the Festival abroad be read as a statement of national self-image? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364)
  3. Why did the Millennium Dome of 2000 borrow the image of the 1951 dome, and what did that borrowing claim? (Roche 2000)
  4. Did the Festival's return to London's centre change how the city used its exhibition sites afterwards? (Geppert 2010p. 223p. 260)
  5. How do the Glasgow show and the London Festival compare in what they claimed about progress and the nation? (Lawn 2009p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 316)
  6. Badger's figures imply a large gap between cost and receipts, and Logan describes cuts to the Festival budget that turned Family Portrait into publicity (Badger 1979p. 131; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 364). How far did money shortfalls shape what the Festival showed, and where it spent its publicity?
  7. The Royal Festival Hall survived the Festival, while the Dome and Skylon did not (Geppert 2010p. 260). Does a surviving hall count as legacy when the Festival's aim was national display (Allwood et al. 2001p. 124)?
  8. Hughes gives war and independence as reasons for dropping live colonial displays (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62p. 63). Did the Festival's omission of them follow the same logic, or did it reflect a choice made in 1951?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
11The Resumption of a Great Traditionp. 128

Allwood's final chapter covers the post-war revival of international exhibitions, starting with the Festival of Britain of 1951. Its South Bank site, the narrative display theme compiled by Ian Cox, the Dome of Discovery and the routing of crowds that gave rise to 'Townscape' are described. The chapter then turns to Brussels 1958, the first international exhibition after the war, centred on the Atomium and the Water Ladder, with the Philips pavilion by Le Corbusier and the American and Soviet pavilions. Italia 61 in Turin is discussed as an attempt to organize exhibitions around the theme of 'Man at Work', which the author judges a failure, though Nervi's Palazzo del Lavoro is valued. The Seattle Century 21 fair of 1962, with its Space Needle, Monorail and United States Science Pavilion, is the last case, and the chapter concludes that it left the city a ready civic centre and a profit. The argument is that exhibitions moved from subject-based halls towards thematic display and from national showcases towards science and civic legacy.

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
6.3Humphrey Jennings and the Festival Audience: Spare Time, Family Portrait, and the Rhetoric of National IdentityKevin M. Flanaganp. 363

Flanagan revisits two films that Humphrey Jennings made for world's fairs and festivals, Spare Time (1939) for the British Pavilion at the New York World's Fair and Family Portrait (1951) for the Festival of Britain. Flanagan argues that Anderson's influential auteurist reading, which prizes the wartime films, overlooks how fair and festival audiences shaped these works. The chapter sets out the audiences the films had to address. Spare Time shows workers in Sheffield, Lancashire and Wales at leisure, with each section built around a local band's performance. Its American references, such as a Western pulp magazine and a basketball game, were pitched at an American audience. Family Portrait, by contrast, speaks to a British public about austerity, presenting the nation as a family whose history of invention, from Drake to radar, supports the present. The film was sent to twenty-three countries as a sanctioned advertisement for the new national self-image. Together the films show cinema serving as national self-presentation at fairs, and Flanagan concludes that Family Portrait argues for compromise as a way of life, preparing for a welfare state.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Teaching the Empire: the weekly Bulletin of Empire Study and the British Empire Exhibition, London, 1924Ian Grosvenorp. 107

Grosvenor looks at the Weekly Bulletin of Empire Study, the classroom series issued in 1924 to prepare schoolchildren for the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley, and asks what ideas of empire it carried into schools and how far the Exhibition reflected them. The Bulletin's 24 weekly parts, which circulated at least 125,000 copies a week, began with lessons on imperial history, then turned to individual territories, and carried two recurring themes, 'race character' and a 'hierarchy of Empire'. Grosvenor argues that this racial hierarchy, which called Africans 'child peoples', was matched at Wembley, where people from Nigeria, the Gold Coast and Sierra Leone were shown in reconstructed villages beside the British Government Pavilion, the most imposing building of the Exhibition. The chapter follows the preparation, the school visits and a route through the site, and concludes that Wembley worked as a 'training school' for imperial virtues that were located in 'race', and links the children who visited to the 1951 Festival of Britain and to Enoch Powell's 1968 speech.

Allwood, Allan and Reid's history of exhibitions treats the Festival in a section of its own, as a country-wide festival rather than an international fair. The book argues that government authority and money, a narrative display on the South Bank and a layout that controlled visitors' circulation gave its designers unusual control, and it presents the Festival as having set the scene for post-war international exhibitions. Its later comparisons set the Festival's aim of boosting morale against the 2000 Greenwich Dome, which the book judges a failure.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Geppert's history of London's exhibitions argues that fairs were pushed from central London out to the suburbs, and that the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley (1924-25) marked the final turn away from the centre. Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Wikipedia

The Festival of Britain was a national exhibition and fair held throughout the United Kingdom in the summer of 1951. Funded chiefly by the government at a cost of £12 million, it was intended to give the British public a sense of recovery and progress after the devastation of the Second World War, and to promote British science, technology, industrial design, architecture and the arts. It proved immensely popular, with more than ten million paid admissions to its six main exhibitions, and helped reshape British arts, crafts and design for a generation. The Festival grew out of a proposal to celebrate the centenary of the Great Exhibition of 1851. Labour Party cabinet member Herbert Morrison took charge in 1947 and shaped it into a uniquely British event: unlike a World Fair, it excluded international themes and the British Commonwealth, focusing entirely on Britain and its achievements.
