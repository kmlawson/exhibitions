The Nagoya Industrial Exhibition, or Nagoya Kyōshinkai, was a Japanese industrial exhibition held at Nagoya in 1910 (Parkp. 7). Only one text in the collection names it, and only in a footnote, where Park lists it among the Japanese kyōshinkai that Korean newspapers covered (Parkp. 7). The texts give no dates, venue, organisers or exhibits. The footnote implies that the exhibition took place, but no text describes it.

What the texts record

Park mentions the Nagoya exhibition once, in a footnote to his article on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Parkp. 7). The note groups it with the Fukuoka Kyōshinkai of 1909 and cites two issues of Hwangsŏng shinmun, dated 23 November 1909 and 9 February 1910 (Parkp. 7). Park does not say which issue reports Nagoya (Parkp. 7). He gives no venue, organisers, size or exhibits for it (Parkp. 7).

The kyōshinkai and its spread to Korea

Park traces the kyōshinkai to a Japanese agricultural product exhibition held at Yokohama in 1879 (Parkp. 7). The kyōshinkai began as a small competition for one farm product and later became a large exposition with spectacular entertainments (Parkp. 7). Matsukata Masayoshi, head of the Agricultural Promotion Bureau in the Interior Ministry, organised the first kyōshinkai after French agricultural fairs (Parkp. 7). After these exhibitions were introduced into Korea, local governments began to plan kongjinhoe, the Korean name for them (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 7). Park calls the Korean local exhibitions important precursors of the 1915 exhibition (Parkp. 6). The texts do not show that the Nagoya exhibition served as a model for them (Parkp. 7).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Japanese kyōshinkai — Park traces the term from a Yokohama show of 1879 to the first kyōshinkai organised by Matsukata, and to its growth into large expositions (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7)
  • Korean press coverage — Korean newspapers reported Japanese local exhibitions, Nagoya among them, and the footnote names two issues as its sources (Parkp. 7)
  • Local exhibitions in Korea — the kongjinhoe series that followed in Korea, which Park treats as precursors of the 1915 exhibition (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 7)

Questions for study

  1. Why did Korean newspapers report a Japanese local exhibition such as Nagoya's, and what did their readers make of it?
  2. How far did Japanese kyōshinkai shape the Korean kongjinhoe that Park describes, and what evidence would show a direct model?
  3. Where can the dates, venue and organisers of the 1910 Nagoya Kyōshinkai be found, given that Park gives none of them?
  4. Why does a study of the 1915 Seoul exhibition mention a Japanese exhibition only in a footnote, and what does that suggest about the links between the two systems?

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
Introduction. Assimilation and Space: Toward an Ethnography of Japanese Rulep. 1Asia

Henry opens in 1925 with the Korea Shrine on Namsan, built by the colonial government in Keijo, and with an official's report of Koreans who looked around but did not worship there. This failure of assimilation frames the book, which treats the city's public spaces as a crucible for Japanese rule. Henry reads assimilation as a set of contested projects in spiritual, material and civic life, and argues against top-down accounts by looking at how shrines, expositions and neighborhoods were used by settlers, Korean elites and poor residents. The introduction sets out three periods, 1910–25, 1925–37 and 1937–45, and the five chapters that follow. It draws on Mary Louise Pratt's idea of contact zones and explains that the sources are limited by the colonial archive, so the book reads Japanese-language newspapers and Korean papers against the grain.

1Constructing Keijō: The Uneven Spaces of a Colonial Capitalp. 22Asia

Henry traces how the Government-General remade Hanyang, the royal capital of the Chosŏn dynasty, into the colonial capital Keijō between 1910 and 1936. Colonial planning reshaped the city's arteries and monuments far more than its everyday neighbourhoods: palaces became parks, museums and offices, and roads such as Taihei Boulevard were widened to display Japanese modernity, while the Korean-populated northern village kept much of its precolonial street pattern and district names. Henry follows this unevenness through urban reforms (1910–25), city planning as cultural rule (1925–34) and the years after the Town Planning Act of 1934, each of which met resistance from Korean residents and from Japanese settlers guarding their own interests. The evidence comes from official plans and maps, the Keijō nippō and Maeil sinbo newspapers, and planning journals. The chapter concludes that colonial governance reached the main arteries but faltered at the capillaries of everyday life, and it leads on to the public spaces of the city's microsites that the following chapters examine.

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hotta-Lister introduces the 1910 exhibition at the White City in London, which ran from May to October and drew over eight million visitors, and places it within the history of nineteenth-century international exhibitions. The study's argument is that the Japanese government saw the event as a national undertaking meant to educate the British public about Japan and to pursue political and economic aims. Quotations from ministers and ambassadors call the two countries 'Island Empires', and the author reads this language as a claim to equal footing. The introduction then surveys the Great Exhibition of 1851, the spread of thematic and imperial displays, and the Franco-British exhibition of 1908 that used the same site. It sets out the book's plan: background on Anglo-Japanese relations, preparations, the opening period, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath. It also notes that the book is not a report on the exhibition itself, and that the final chapter compares it with the 1991 Japan Festival.

1Background of the Time (1905-10)p. 9Asia

Hotta-Lister sets out the diplomatic and economic setting for the exhibition in the years 1905 to 1910. Japan had become a great power after the Russo-Japanese War, and its legations in London, Washington and other capitals were upgraded to embassies between 1906 and 1908. The chapter then traces strains in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which British newspapers and officials increasingly questioned over Manchuria, the 'open door' pledge and Japanese immigration. It goes on to tariff revision, which Komura pursued against British objections, and to Japan's trade deficit and reliance on foreign loans. Its last sections cover the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the rivalry between Katsura and Saionji. It also surveys the Japanese and English-language newspapers on which much of the study relies, including The Times, the Japan Weekly Mail and the Chuo Shimbun. The chapter concludes that these pressures explain why the government was so committed to the exhibition.

2Preparationsp. 38Asia

Hotta-Lister shows how deeply the Japanese government committed itself to the London exhibition and how far the two sides differed in approach. The idea came from the entrepreneur Imre Kiralfy, who had staged imperial spectacles and organised the White City site; Komura took it up in Tokyo, while Kato, Mutsu Hirokichi and the commissioners Wada and Beppu handled it in London. The chapter reconstructs the earlier rejections of Kiralfy's plan and the rivalry with possible American and Russian exhibitions. It then covers the 1.8 million yen budget, the exhibitors' association, the selection of 19,943 articles from 1,286 exhibitors, and the gardens, models and entertainers shipped from Japan. British responses were lukewarm: the Board of Trade and Grey gave little official support, while a General Committee under the Duke of Norfolk formed and the Japan Society was given its own space. It ends with the opening on 14 May 1910, two days late after the death of Edward VII.

3Exhibition Objectivesp. 74Asia

This chapter asks why the Japanese government sponsored the Japan-British Exhibition and what it hoped to gain. Hotta-Lister divide the aims into political and diplomatic, economic and educational groups. The political case is tied to three issues then pending with Britain: revision of Japan's commercial tariffs, renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and the annexation of Korea, which took place in August 1910 while the Exhibition was open. Working through speeches, newspapers and diplomatic correspondence, the authors argue that Komura and Katsura saw the Exhibition as a platform for Japan's imperial role, and they set the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial exhibits of the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908. The economic section covers City loans, exports to Britain and the trade imbalance. The educational section shows Japanese leaders trying to correct British views of Japan as warlike or backward, through the book Japan Today and The Times Japanese Edition. The chapter ends with these publications.

4From the Opening Day Until the End of the Exhibitionp. 100Asia

This chapter narrates the Exhibition from its opening on 14 May 1910 to the receipt of the official reports in 1912. Hotta-Lister estimate attendance at over eight million and describe the evening illuminations, fireworks and package tours that drew crowds. Most of the chapter is a dated record of visits and events: Prince Fushimi's attendance at King Edward's funeral, banquets for British and Japanese dignitaries, Japan Day, and visits by Kitchener, Marie Stopes, the Swedish royal couple and the Brazilian president-elect. It also records a private viewing of retrospective Japanese art for about 350 European experts. The authors then trace the afterlife of the exhibits, which were shipped home, given to British and European institutions, or sent to exhibitions in Budapest, Dresden and Turin. Little is argued here; the chapter is a record of ceremony and diplomacy that frames the reactions discussed next.

5Reactions to the Exhibitionp. 110Asia

This chapter weighs the reception of the Exhibition in Britain and Japan. Hotta-Lister conclude that it did not change British attitudes toward Japan in diplomacy or trade, though publications about Japan rose sharply in 1910 and 1911. The British press praised the Japanese fine arts, gardens and wooden buildings, including the Chokushimon gate later re-erected at Kew, while some critics judged them by European standards as barbaric or childlike. Japanese opinion was largely hostile. Elite residents in London were embarrassed by the Japanese Village, the Ainu and Formosan displays and the sumo wrestlers, and their complaints reached the Diet, where Kurahara and Nishimura attacked Komura and the promoter Kiralfy. The authors read the colonial displays as part of a common imperial genre. The chapter ends with British manufacturers doubting that exhibitions paid, and with a 1910 proposal for a British-only exhibition in Japan.

In the contents of this work

6Aftermath of the Exhibitionp. 153Asia

Hotta-Lister asks whether the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London was, as Japanese opinion held, a failure, and tests its effects in economic, financial, diplomatic and racial terms. On trade, the author sets Japanese and British statistics beside the exhibitors' sales figures to show that Japanese exports to Britain fell overall in 1910 and 1911, while matting, raw silk, shell buttons and toys rose, and treats the Exhibition as one plausible contributor. Loans get the same caution: the government's conversion loans and the municipal and South Manchurian Railway issues were raised largely through the usual banks, so the Exhibition at most reinforced existing confidence. The chapter then follows the tariff dispute, the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in July 1911, drawing heavily on British press reactions. Its racial discussion centres on the Official Guide's claims of similarity between Japanese and British people and on Japanese complaints about Chinese and Ainu displays. It concludes that the Exhibition had only a limited effect, though it was not the failure that Japanese critics claimed.

In the contents of this work

7Some Concluding Reflectionsp. 186Asia

Hotta-Lister gives an overall verdict on the 1910 Exhibition, concluding that it was on balance a success rather than the failure of Japanese opinion. The argument rests on the partnership between Imre Kiralfy, the commercial entrepreneur, and the Japanese government under Komura, which the author sees as the seed of later controversy in the Diet. Komura's aims were mainly diplomatic, to ease anti-Japanese feeling and support the Alliance, with trade and loans as secondary goals, and the proposed Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1917 is shown to have been a further reason for the venture. The Japanese negative reviews are traced to Kiralfy's popular attractions and to the elite's embarrassment at Ainu and village displays, which the author compares with exhibitions in the United States. The closing pages set the 1910 event beside the Great Japan Exhibition of 1981/82 and the Japan Festival of 1991 in London, which shared a wish to correct misunderstanding of Japan but differed in scale, funding and educational follow-up. The author concludes that the Exhibition helped turn the Alliance into a link between peoples, though only to a limited extent.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
AbstractAsia

Hur's abstract summarises a dissertation on the Qing government's attempt to stage modern statehood at world exhibitions and how that attempt was undermined. The Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan and Chinese reformers each shaped China's image, and criticism of the Qing grew after Osaka in 1903 and St Louis in 1904. It ends with the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which Chinese intellectuals came to support once the government promised constitutional reform.

In the contents of this work

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

FourThe Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, the Last Show of the Qing Dynastyp. 124Asia

Hur turns to the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, held in Nanjing from June to November 1910, the first national exposition hosted by China and the last of the Qing dynasty. The chapter traces the project to Duanfang, the Manchu governor-general who proposed it in 1908 and modeled its regulations on Japan's domestic exhibitions, and to his successor Zhang Renjun after Duanfang's transfer in 1909. Hur describes the preparations, including a site of about 165 acres, pavilions for twenty-two provinces, foreign samples buildings, and the security measures taken amid fears of unrest in Nanjing. The main argument concerns the Chinese press, which, unlike its coverage of Osaka and St. Louis, was mostly descriptive or positive, a shift Hur ties to the constitutional movement and to newspapers and organizers who backed it. The chapter also covers the large Japanese visiting groups and the overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia. It concludes that the exposition was a momentary display of an integrated, constitutional China that faded with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
