Seoul · 1915

Home Exhibition (Keijo)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther reading

The Home Exhibition (Keijo) was a domestic-education exhibition held in Seoul from 11 to 26 September, concurrently with the Industrial Exhibition of 1915 (Henry 2014p. 110; Henry 2014p. 97). It was housed at the offices of the Keijo nippo and Maeil sinbo newspapers (Henry 2014p. 110). Its organisers aimed to change women's household work through domestic education, sanitary reform and savings campaigns (Henry 2014p. 110). The texts give it a single paragraph, so the account is thin (Henry 2014p. 110).

In depth: what our sources say

Background and aims

The Industrial Exhibition of 1915 took over the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace, and Henry says it was held to commemorate the first five years of colonial rule (Henry 2014p. 97). The Home Exhibition ran at the same time, in a different place (Henry 2014p. 110). Henry links the event to official efforts to promote a classed role for Korean women as efficient housewives (Henry 2014p. 110).

Organisers and reforms

Cho Chung-ŭng, the Korean vice-director of the Keijo Cooperative Committee (KCC), urged Korean women to stop turning away male visitors when their husbands were absent, and to shop during the day (Henry 2014p. 110; Henry 2014p. 293). Henry describes this advice as a break with precolonial conventions that kept elite women indoors (Henry 2014p. 110). For an overview of the Home Exhibition, Henry refers readers to Yi's study, pages 162 to 167 (Henry 2014p. 244).

Displays and visitors

The texts say little about what the event showed or who attended. Henry cites an article by Kim Myŏng-sŏn on the display of middle-class Japanese homes at the event, but the passage does not describe those displays (Henry 2014p. 244). It gives no attendance figure for the Home Exhibition (Henry 2014p. 110).

Gender and colonial order

Henry reads the appeal to women as part of a colonial scheme. Korean women were encouraged to take part in colonial industry as "good wives and wise mothers", a role limited by a patriarchy that colonialism strengthened (Henry 2014p. 110).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Domestic education and housework — the event aimed to change how women ran households, through education, sanitary reform and savings campaigns, and Henry ties this to a classed image of efficient housewives (Henry 2014p. 110)
  • Gender and colonial modernity — Henry reads the invitation to women as a limited role under patriarchy that colonialism strengthened (Henry 2014p. 110)
  • Concurrent events — the Home Exhibition ran alongside the 1915 Industrial Exhibition but had its own venue at the newspaper offices (Henry 2014p. 110)
  • Displays of Japanese homes — the text cites a study of middle-class Japanese homes shown at the event, without describing the displays itself (Henry 2014p. 244)

Questions for study

  1. Why did organisers present housework, sanitary habits and saving as ways for women to serve colonial industry, and how did a newspaper office suit such a programme?
  2. What would the displays of middle-class Japanese homes have shown to Korean women, and how far can Kim's study answer that question?
  3. How did "good wives and wise mothers" as a role for women at the Home Exhibition compare with the family visits and mothers' areas at the Industrial Exhibition?

Chapters that treat it at length 5 in 2 works

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Introductionp. 1Asia

Park's introduction presents the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition in Seoul as a spectacle that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects. It argues against the literature that reads the event mainly as colonial propaganda, and treats it as a cultural machine that disciplined and seduced visitors, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. The introduction then sets out the article's plan: how the exposition idea reached Korea, how the exhibition represented 'modern Korea', and how visitors experienced it.

In the contents of this work

Representations of “Modern Korea” in the 1915 Exhibitionp. 7Asia

Park examines the architecture, layout and display of the 1915 exhibition as a way of representing 'modern Korea'. The chapter draws on the official report, the exhibition guide and visitor routes printed in Maeil shinbo and Keijō nippō, and on posters, photographs and postcards. It shows that the organisers built Western-style pavilions on the east of the Kyŏngbok Palace site, destroying a large part of its structures, and kept older palace halls on the west as small exhibition rooms. Visitors followed a numbered route from the 'modern' pavilions to the 'premodern' or 'old' space. Displays of penal and hygiene systems, provincial development and products were arranged to show Korean backwardness against Japanese progress. Park situates the architecture in the Tokyo Taisho exhibition of 1914 and in Renaissance and Secession styles, and concludes that the buildings served the colonial claim to have made a 'new modern' Korea.

In the contents of this work

Experiencing Modernity and “New Modern Subjects”p. 15Asia

Park turns from the organisers to the visitors, asking how ordinary Koreans experienced and responded to the exhibition. The main sources are the Maeil shinbo's reports, the American missionaries Edwin Wade Koons and William Arthur Noble, and a survey of visitors' favourite sites. Park argues that the exhibition aimed to produce disciplined, 'civilised' viewers through rules on dress, behaviour, toilets and sanitary inspection, but that many visitors were drawn instead to electric illumination, rides, the entertainment area and shopping. Souvenirs and the Mitsukoshi kimono shop show the exhibition as a 'universe of commodities'. Reformist commentators such as Chang Chi-yŏn and Yun Pong-kyun welcomed it as a means of industrial growth and self-strengthening. The chapter concludes that the Korean subject shaped by the exhibition was divided between the disciplined citizen, the consumer and the colonised subject.

In the contents of this work

Conclusionp. 21Asia

The brief conclusion treats 1915 as the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea, with some thirty large and small expositions held between 1915 and 1940. Park argues that the exhibition was a cultural machinery producing a conflicted Korean subject, shaped by seduction and spectacle, education and discipline, and coercive colonial control. It ends by linking this to a Korean nationalism understood as an ambivalent response to uneven development.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
