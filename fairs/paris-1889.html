Paris Universal Exposition of 1889

Exposition universelle internationale de 1889
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle of 1889 ran in Paris from 6 May to 6 November, with its main buildings on the Champ de Mars and further pavilions across the Seine on the Trocadéro and along the Esplanade des Invalides (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 100; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 106). The Third Republic timed it for the centenary of the Revolution, and most European monarchies declined to take part officially (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101). It drew about 32 million visitors and made a profit, which few great expositions did (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 111; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115). Historians study it because the Eiffel Tower and the Galerie des Machines turned engineering into spectacle, and because its colonial villages set the pattern for displaying colonised peoples (Allwood et al. 2001p. 58; Benedict 1983p. 48).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and the centennial

A decree of 8 November 1884 announced a universal exposition for 1889 and mentioned the Revolution's anniversary almost as an aside (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 108). Hall argues that few world fairs were launched in less favourable conditions: "Few other world fairs have come to fruition under less auspicious circumstances than the Paris Exposition universelle internationale of 1889." (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 108). A purely national fair, or a postponement to 1900, were both considered first (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 108).

Decrees of July 1886 tried to separate the fair from the centenary, but the attempt did not work (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 109). Mandell notes that the fair's eleven-year cycle brought it to the hundredth anniversary of 1789 (Mandell 1967p. 17). Swift says the government tied the exposition to republican achievement to support the Third Republic (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101).

Boulanger's popularity made the fair politically charged. The government used the exposition to counter him, and Boulanger fled to Belgium in April (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 110). Hall counts public preoccupation with the fair as a major factor in Boulanger's defeat (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 110).

Finance and organisation

Estimates of the outlay differ. Greenhalgh gives a basic outlay of 41,500,000 francs: the state paid 17 million, the city 8 million, and banks guaranteed the rest, to be repaid from bons, admission booklets that doubled as lottery tickets (Greenhalgh 1988p. 34). Mandell's figures run from an estimate of about 43 million francs to a final cost of 46,500,000 (Mandell 1967p. 17; Mandell 1967p. 17).

Hall describes a March 1889 adjustment in which the Credit Foncier put up 21.5 million francs instead of 18 million, and made a profit of 3 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 109). Private pavilions paid rent, and the colonies were asked to contribute, Tunisia and Algeria most of all (Greenhalgh 1988p. 34).

The tower was financed differently. The state paid one-fifth of its cost and gave Eiffel the revenue from visits for twenty years (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101). Allwood says Eiffel put up the remaining four-fifths, mortgaging the company to do so (Allwood et al. 2001p. 57), while Mandell says the tower kept nineteen years of receipts as profit (Mandell 1967p. 23). Hall gives the final cost as 7,799,401.31 francs, about 6 per cent under budget (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 111).

The profit figures diverge. Swift gives 8 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 106), Allwood about £320,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207), and Hall an estimate of $600,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115). Mandell calls the profit modest (Mandell 1967p. 22). Attendance also differs: Hall gives over 32 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 111), Maloney more than 30 million (Maloney 2012p. 55), and Mandell almost 40 million, elsewhere 39 million (Mandell 1967p. 22; Mandell 1967p. 38).

Site, tower and machinery

The Proust commission weighed several sites before the main buildings went to the Champ de Mars (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 100). Lockroy's May 1886 competition asked for a 300-metre iron tower, and more than a hundred designs came in, one of them a gigantic guillotine (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 109). The tower reached 300.51 metres, with 15,000 wrought-iron sections and over 2.5 million rivets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 111; Allwood et al. 2001p. 57).

Opposition was strong. Forty-seven French artists and writers signed a protest against the tower (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101), and Geppert notes a contract clause forbidding its demolition after the fair, which a petition of almost fifty signatories opposed (Geppert 2010p. 224). Dutert's Galerie des Machines had hinged supports, drawing on Eiffel's Douro bridge (Allwood et al. 2001p. 58). Greenhalgh calls the Galerie the last great engineering experiment at an exhibition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 155).

Allwood says electricity was used throughout an international exhibition for the first time (Allwood et al. 2001p. 59). Swift describes a display of 20,000 electric bulbs, although electric lighting was not new (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 103). Hall says the bulb let the gates open at night for the first time (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 114). Visitors could see the displays from the tower and from a moving platform over the Galerie (Giberti 2002).

Garnier's History of Habitation, a series of dwellings showing the human past, stood at the foot of the tower. Çelik reads it as Garnier's protest against Eiffel's iron architecture (Çelik 1992p. 71–72). Hall places forty-nine such constructions along the Quai d'Orsay (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113). The Trocadéro gardens had fountains and, Hall says, 4,500 roses in bloom (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113).

Colonial villages and peopled attractions

Qureshi notes that the fair is traditionally identified as the start of importing foreign peoples to Paris solely to be shown in indigenous villages (Qureshi 2011p. 248). Hale says colonial governments and private entrepreneurs sponsored such villages for the first time, staffed by dozens of employees from Indochina, West Africa, Equatorial Africa and the South Pacific (Hale 2008p. 18). Benedict counts over four hundred Indochinese, Senegalese and Tahitians shown in pavilions and villages (Benedict 1983p. 48). A native village was a reconstructed settlement where colonised people lived and worked for visitors (Peterson 2020p. 15).

Burris describes family-based groups that gave an impression of social harmony, although the Senegalese families came from several regions and had no common language (Burris 2001p. 84). Robles argues that the Senegalese village staged the Empire at a time of parliamentary opposition to colonial expansion (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 54). The sources place the villages differently: on the Esplanade des Invalides (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 54), at the foot of the Eiffel Tower (Qureshi 2011p. 248), or on the Chaillot Hill (Greenhalgh 1988p. 87). Robles also records a hierarchy, with North African and Asian colonies along the main alley and sub-Saharan Africans, New Caledonians and Tonkinese in ethnographic villages (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 55).

The colonial administration exceeded its budget for indigenous workers by 120,000 francs (Hale 2008p. 19), and the colonial section finished 280,000 francs in debt (Hale 2008p. 63). Administrators expected colonised participants to return home and praise French civilisation (Hale 2008p. 19).

The Rue du Caire, a reconstructed Cairo street, is among the most-cited attractions. Swift says it had first appeared at the 1878 Paris exposition, and that in 1889 it added bazaars, cafés and belly dancing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 104). Hall calls it second only to the Eiffel Tower as an attraction (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 114). Wilson Smith says it was first shown at Paris in 1889 (Filipová 2015p. 207). Çelik says its designer reused fragments of demolished Cairo buildings and claimed it was more authentic than any street in Cairo (Çelik 1992p. 75–76), and describes an Egyptian visitor disgusted by a mosque used as a coffeehouse with dancing (Çelik 1992p. 79–80).

Nations, visitors and the boycott

Most European monarchies stayed away. Boone lists Austria-Hungary, Belgium, England, Holland, Italy, Portugal, Russia and Sweden as refusing government delegations (Boone 2019p. 69). Greenhalgh says Queen Victoria recalled Lord Lytton as ambassador, and that Belgium alone sent representation to the opening (Greenhalgh 1988p. 36). Geppert says Austria-Hungary and Italy cited cost, while Britain, Russia and Germany objected to the Revolution link (Geppert 2010p. 68). Hall says the refusals were official only (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 110).

Some 35 nations accepted, including the United States, Argentina, Japan and Morocco (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101). Kaiser says the United States had about 1,500 exhibits and Argentina 1,473 (Kaiserp. 51). Badger says many Americans thought their country poorly represented: its exhibitors won more than their share of awards, but the national display was judged a discredit (Badger 1979p. 46). Dussel says Argentina's pavilion omitted gauchos and indigenous peoples and showed modern progress, so that the country could claim equivalence with Paris (Lawn 2009p. 140).

Hall gives the lowest day as 10 May, with 36,922 visitors, and the peak as 3 October, with 387,877 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 111; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115). Geppert gives 1,953,122 tower ascents, which counts tower traffic rather than fair attendance (Geppert 2010p. 225). Mandell says the Republic was solidly affirmed in the September 1889 elections (Mandell 1967p. 24). A contemporary writer, quoted by Mandell, wrote: "France, diseased with politics, went to the exposition as to a cure." (Mandell 1967p. 24).

Hall says the Marxist congress held alongside the fair founded the Second International and designated May Day an international labour holiday (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113). The government's Social Peace display answered labour unrest but won little support from organised labour (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113).

Aftermath and legacy

Benedict says the tower contained no exhibits and had no practical function, yet it symbolised the fair so well that it was preserved and came to symbolise Paris itself (Benedict 1983p. 13). Geppert says attendance at the tower fell after the fair, and the British Builder called it ugly and proposed removing or truncating it (Geppert 2010p. 76). Watkin's Folly, a London imitation, came out of a public competition held just after the fair (Geppert 2010p. 138).

Chicago's planners took Paris as their benchmark. Rydell says Paris 1889 was the model that Chicago followed and tried to surpass, and that Chicago's ethnological ventures began with the anthropology congress held alongside the fair (Rydell 1984). The Chicago Tribune ran a design competition for a tower to outdo the Eiffel Tower, but the Ferris wheel was chosen (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Later fairs copied its features. Geppert says Berlin's 1896 Kairo was modelled directly on the Rue du Caire (Geppert 2010p. 49). Greenhalgh says the 1889 imperial displays set the model for later imperial exhibitions (Greenhalgh 1988p. 66). Paris 1900 cost more than twice the 1889 outlay, although Geppert records an official deficit of two million francs (Geppert 2010p. 97).

How historians have interpreted it

The main readings agree that the fair was a political showcase. Swift reads it as a success won against a hostile setting, in which industry was turned into spectacle and the Republic was celebrated (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101). Hall reads it as a celebration of liberalism and capitalism presented as light entertainment (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115). Mandell calls it a political triumph and the benchmark for judging the less successful 1900 fair (Mandell 1967p. 24; Mandell 1967p. 24). Greenhalgh argues that the French state linked republican legitimacy to empire and technology (Greenhalgh 1988p. 87). Roche places the fair within a Third Republic policy that tied the Revolution's heritage to state display and citizens' pleasure (Roche 2000p. 36).

The historians differ over origins and over the peopled displays. Burris reads the fair within social evolutionism, arguing that its displays were commercial entertainment and that by 1889 expositions presented the nation as an empire (Burris 2001p. 84). Burris also names the London Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 as the first international exposition to display actual peoples in a significant way (Burris 2001p. 83). Qureshi calls the traditional view that 1889 began the practice in need of revision, because the 1851 and 1886 exhibitions had already used displayed peoples (Qureshi 2011p. 248). Hinsley cites Mason's praise of the fair as the most thoroughly anthropological of its time (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Roche reads the fair as the moment Coubertin turned to reviving the Olympic Games, and argues that the fair's exposition tradition misled Coubertin into linking the 1900 Olympics to the 1900 fair (Roche 2000p. 92; Roche 2000p. 93). Harvey reads the tower's viewing platform through panopticism, the ideal of a public that watches itself and is constantly surveyed (Harvey 1996p. 135). Çelik reads the Islamic quarters as the colonisers' fixed image of Islamic culture, which only colonisation could save (Çelik 1992p. 73–74). Peterson sees 1889 as a stage in the shift from displaying objects to displaying people (Peterson 2020p. 15). Geppert reads the financial losses of Vienna 1873 and Paris 1889 as reasons Berlin never held a universal exposition (Geppert 2010p. 36). Hall and Swift both record a profit, so the interpretations rest on contested figures.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan accepted the French invitation and was one of the nations that took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101). A Japanese garden stood among the horticultural displays across the Seine (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113; Maloney 2012p. 55). Raizman and Robey's volume describes Japanese dissatisfaction with the 1889 fair: the French had placed fanciful Japanese facades and gateways, designed by local architects, inside the main buildings, and a Japanese House in the History of Habitation placed Japan among underdeveloped countries (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 95). Some fairgoers likened Japan to part of China, which the volume judged beyond deplorable (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 95). Greenhalgh places Japanese art among the exhibits that had a constant presence at Paris expositions, including 1889 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 148).

Garnier's History of Habitation grouped Chinese, Japanese and Indian dwellings among contemporary versions of primitive civilizations that had not influenced human progress (Çelik 1992p. 73–74). Tsuboi Shōgorō, the Japanese anthropologist, visited the 1889 fair, which included a Negro Village display, before drafting the prospectus for the anthropological hall at Osaka (Hurp. 57). Kwon says the Japanese human displays of the early twentieth century resembled the native-village model first seen at Paris in 1889 (Kwonp. 20). Kaneko Kentaro had witnessed the 1889 fair before the mission to the St Louis fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 214), and Maeda Masamori was involved with the Paris expositions of 1878, 1889 and 1897 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 218).

Indochina, China and the Dutch East Indies

Indochina was the best-represented Asian region. Hale says pavilions were built for Cochinchina, Annam and Tonkin, and that twenty Vietnamese and Chinese artisans settled in Paris for several months to decorate the Annamite pavilion (Hale 2008p. 17). Burris and Qureshi list Cochin-Chinese and Kampong-Javanese groups among the peoples in the settlements (Burris 2001p. 84; Qureshi 2011p. 248). Robles places Tonkinese among the ethnographic villages (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 55). Mason praised the Tonkinese temple, where Buddhist priests performed rites (Karp & Lavine 1991). Swift says the palaces included a facsimile of the Angkor Wat pagoda and that visitors rode in rickshaws pulled by Indochinese men (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 104; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 105). Hale says the prince of Annam toured the grounds in summer 1889 with the Guinean king, and that observers still saw such visitors as foreign and exotic (Hale 2008p. 20).

Javanese performers were a steady attraction. Swift says Javanese dancers performed in the Dutch colonial section (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 103), and Hall places them in the Dutch pavilion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 114). Hale names a female Javanese dance group among the famous attractions (Hale 2008p. 19). Gauguin watched the Javanese dancers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115). Coté says the Dutch used native exhibits at Paris 1889, and that the village javanaise there became the model that later exhibitions followed (Cotép. 2; Cotép. 12).

British India and Asian visitors

The Indian tea trade had its own palace. The Indian tea association opened an Indian Palace with twelve Native Attendants who spoke French and served tea, and afterwards set up an Indian restaurant in Paris (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). Mathur says Liberty's, the London store selling Eastern goods, had a bazaar at the fair (Mathur 2007p. 34). Hoffenberg reproduces a picture of Indian workmen moving crates under the Eiffel Tower (Hoffenberg 2001p. 186).

Mathur says Gandhi, then a barrister student in London, attended the fair, climbed the tower two or three times, and was affected by the contrast between the modern industrial West and the ancient East (Mathur 2007p. 48). Peterson's study of Asian self-representation mentions 1889 as the stage for gazing at colonised peoples, and places Asian nations' own narrative in later pavilions (Peterson 2020p. 15; Peterson 2020p. 21). Apart from Japan, the texts say little about an independent Asian state's own display at this fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Monarchist boycott and the centennial — The texts agree that most European monarchies refused to take part, but differ on details: Boone lists Belgium as a refusal, while Greenhalgh says it alone sent representation to the opening (Boone 2019p. 69; Greenhalgh 1988p. 36). The centennial made the fair a republican occasion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 108; Mandell 1967p. 24).
  • Engineering and the Eiffel Tower — The tower and the Galerie des Machines are the fair's technical centrepieces, and the texts argue that they made engineering a spectacle (Allwood et al. 2001p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 155; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 100).
  • Colonial villages and peopled display — The texts treat the fair as the point where colonised peoples became paid attractions, with the Senegalese village and the Rue du Caire as the main cases, though the start date is disputed (Burris 2001p. 83; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 54; Qureshi 2011p. 248).
  • Finance and profit — Outlay, bons and the Eiffel contract are set out in several texts, but the profit and attendance figures differ, so the reference works are the safest basis (Mandell 1967p. 17; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 115; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 106).
  • Boulanger and the Republic — The fair is read as a republican response to the Boulanger crisis, and some writers credit it with the republicans' success in the September 1889 elections (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 101; Mandell 1967p. 24; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 110).
  • Labour and the socialist congresses — The socialist congresses and the government's Social Peace display show the fair as a site of class politics, with the Second International founded there (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 113).
  • Benchmark for later fairs — Chicago 1893 and Paris 1900 are repeatedly measured against 1889, in planning, layout and the imitation of the Rue du Caire (Rydell 1984; Geppert 2010p. 49; Mandell 1967p. 24).

Questions for study

  1. The texts disagree on the fair's profit and attendance. Which source should a student trust, and what evidence would settle the question?
  2. Why did the organisers present colonised families as showing social harmony, and what did that presentation hide?
  3. How far did the Rue du Caire and the native villages create an image of the East that later fairs repeated, and how far did they reflect what was already in Paris?
  4. Was the Eiffel Tower an engineering achievement, a political statement or a commercial attraction, and can the texts separate these meanings?
  5. Why did Japan object to the 1889 displays, and what does that objection show about who controlled national images at fairs?
  6. Did the boycott by European monarchies weaken the fair's claim to be international, or did private exhibitors keep it international in practice?

Substantial discussion 10

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
chapter 'Eiffel's Tower and Chicago's White City, 1889-1897', Paris section, pp. c.56-61

Allwood and colleagues present the Paris exposition of 1889 as the high point of Victorian engineering: Eiffel's tower and Dutert's Galerie des Machines embody the ingenuity and showmanship of the engineers, whom the chapter sets against the academic architects of the Chicago fair that followed. The authors stress that the fair commemorated the French Revolution, that foreign governments kept their distance, and that its profit surprised the organisers and the French. They also single out electricity, used throughout an international exhibition for the first time, as one of the fair's defining moments. Their larger point is that the 1889 fair set the pattern for the engineering-led exhibitions that Chicago followed in 1893.

Wikipedia

The Exposition Universelle de 1889 (French pronunciation: [ɛkspozisjɔ̃ ynivɛʁsɛl]), better known in English as the 1889 Paris Exposition, was a world's fair held in Paris, France, from 6 May to 31 October 1889. It was the fifth of ten major expositions held in the city between 1855 and 1937. It attracted more than thirty-two million visitors. The most famous structure created for the exposition, and still remaining, is the Eiffel Tower.
