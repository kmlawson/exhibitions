North East Coast Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The North East Coast Exhibition opened at Newcastle upon Tyne in May 1929 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65). Its entertainment pavilion included an 'African Village' of performers whom Hughes identifies as Fullah and Senegalese (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). A privately owned company arranged the show (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). Ladipo Solanke, writing for the West African Students' Union, asked the British government to intervene, and its answer turned on whether the performers were British subjects (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 61; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The organisers sought to replicate the experience of the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley, held in 1924 and 1925 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 61; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). Hughes says their purpose was to promote imperial unity and national industry (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 61). Taking their cues from the Wembley catalogue, they tried to balance education with entertainment (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 61). Hughes presents the 'African Village' as the point where that balance was contested (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 61).

Organisers and attractions

The show was arranged by a privately owned and operated company with more than twenty-five years' experience of supplying entertainments to exhibitions in Europe and America (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). Hughes concludes that showmen, not professional anthropologists, delivered the 'African Village' (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). The Newcastle Chronicle reported on 16 February 1929 that the attractions included a water chute and a West African setting presented as true to life (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64). Hughes's note gives the paper's full title as the North Mail and Newcastle Chronicle (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 68). H. B. Singer, managing director of Exhibition Attractions, Ltd, was to provide the attractions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64). The texts give no figures for the show's finances.

Who took part

Singer, as the paper reported him, promised to bring representatives of the Fullah 'tribe' to Newcastle and called them "practically savages" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64). Solanke's letter opposed a plan to bring about 100 West African people to England against their wish (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64). Hughes gives no count of the performers who came. She says the Newcastle participants were members of one of the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes that toured Europe and the United States (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62), and later names the people as Fullah and Senegalese (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). Greenhalgh places Senegalese villages at Newcastle in 1929 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). He says the Senegalese and Dahomeyan groups were leased to entrepreneurs yet still appeared as part of French imperial sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94; Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). He also notes that it is not known whether the same group toured Europe and America (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). The two texts therefore differ on the performers' name, and neither identifies the individuals.

Empire and colony

Greenhalgh counts Newcastle (1929) among the most impressive of the substantial national imperial exhibitions held in cities in the British Isles after 1900 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). He links these shows to a loss of confidence: after the Boer War, exhibitions became more vocal about empire, because its permanence was for the first time at issue (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). Hughes describes British imperial exhibitions as organised to popularise imperialism (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62). Of seven imperial exhibitions held in Great Britain between 1851 and 1951, all but the 1951 event included a live display of colonial people (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 62).

The colonial question at Newcastle was the performers' origin. The Home Office authorised Singer to bring people of African origin into Britain and accepted his word that he could provide French documentation for them (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). The Colonial Office replied that it "was unable to take any steps", because admission to Britain did not rest with it and it had no power over persons resident in French, Spanish or other African territory (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). Whether the performers were British subjects was decisive. The Prime Minister asked where the Africans would be recruited and whether they were British subjects (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). Hughes says they were not British citizens, and that this left little legal ground for intervention (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65). Neither text names a colony or dominion among the exhibitors at Newcastle, or any colonial product shown there.

Protest and government response

Solanke's letter argued that the display would promote racial prejudice through the exploitation of people, and that it ran against the international and inter-racial spirit of the time (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64). The League Against Imperialism told William Ormsby-Gore at the Colonial Office that the show would offend African peoples and Black people in the West Indies and America (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65). The Anti-Slavery and Aborigines Protection Society also corresponded with Leo Amery at the Dominions Office about the show's apparent inhumanity (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65). The Colonial Office invited Solanke to help investigate (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65).

The Home Office had authorised Singer, and obliged him to guarantee the performers' maintenance and repatriation and to have them medically inspected on arrival (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). Hughes finds no evidence that anyone at the Home Office objected on grounds of exploitation, and no further investigation followed (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). A footnote reports that British authorities investigated a complaint of mistreatment and that Amery later saw no evidence of it (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 69). Bridgeman told Ormsby-Gore that League members did not care where the people came from (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). The show went ahead and proved a popular distraction (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67). Hughes says the matter was not closed and that later calls for intervention followed (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67).

Anthropology and anti-colonial protest

Hughes argues that the protests rested on changes in anthropology and in politics after the First World War. Uncritical enthusiasm for empire fell out of favour after the war (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). By the 1930s academic anthropologists had distanced themselves from popular displays, and dismissed them as holdovers from a less enlightened age (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). Once their scientific justification had gone, the shows were exposed as entertainment (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63). Hughes concludes that the 'African Village' became vulnerable to protest against scientific racism and its impact on human rights (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67). She sees the Newcastle controversy as evidence that by 1929 the government took complaints about colonial persons very seriously (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67). Her conclusion is that, despite the show's aim of promoting imperial unity, it became a site for contesting subject inequality (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67). Hughes's chapter is the only interpretation of the show in our texts.

Visitors and reception

Hughes says questions about reception are hard to answer without audience commentary (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67). She asks whether visitors dismissed the village as a sideshow or took it as evidence of racial prejudice, and leaves the question to later research (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67).

Its place in the series

Greenhalgh's list places Newcastle (1929) with the imperial exhibitions at Edinburgh (1886), Bradford (1904) and Wolverhampton (1907) (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). Hughes links the show to Wembley, where the Colonial and Home Offices had affirmed in principle that the government opposed misrepresentation and exploitation of British subjects at such events (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66). The texts connect the show with no later exhibitions.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Showmen and popular entertainment — Hughes treats the 'African Village' as a commercial attraction run by showmen once academic anthropologists had abandoned popular displays (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63).
  • Subject status and citizenship — the government's decision turned on whether the performers were British subjects, which Hughes says they were not (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65).
  • Wembley precedent — Hughes uses the 1924 controversy to explain why the Home and Colonial Offices felt bound to respond (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66).
  • Anti-colonial protest — Hughes shows the WASU and League writing to the government, and argues that the show became a site for contesting subject inequality (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67).
  • Performing troupes — Greenhalgh and Hughes both place Senegalese performers at Newcastle, but they name the groups differently (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66).

Questions for study

  1. If the Home Office accepted a showman's word on French documentation, what did the refusal to intervene assume about the status of colonial subjects (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66)?
  2. Hughes argues the show became vulnerable once anthropology had lost its scientific claim. What other grounds could a government have used to stop such a show (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66)?
  3. Greenhalgh and Hughes name different performing groups for Newcastle. How would you test which account is right (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66)?
  4. Hughes leaves visitors' responses open. What evidence would show whether visitors took the village as a sideshow or as racial evidence (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 67)?

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
1.5Debating the African VillageDeborah L. Hughesp. 61

Hughes examines the protests of 1929 against the 'African Village' at the North East Coast Exhibition in Newcastle, where West African performers from a touring troupe were shown in a commercial entertainment pavilion. The chapter follows the letters from the West African Students' Union, the Anti-Slavery and Aborigines Protection Society and the League Against Imperialism, which asked the Home and Colonial Offices to halt the display on grounds of exploitation and racial prejudice. The government's response turned on whether the performers were British subjects, and the Home Office, which had licensed the show, found no grounds for objection. Hughes argues that by the 1920s academic anthropologists had distanced themselves from popular ethnological display, leaving such shows to showmen, and that once their scientific justification had gone they became open to anti-colonial criticism. The 1924 Wembley exhibition set the precedent for these complaints. The chapter concludes that the controversy made Newcastle another site where the rights of colonial subjects were contested, even though the show went ahead.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Wikipedia

The North East Coast Exhibition was a world's fair held in Newcastle, Tyne and Wear and ran from May to October 1929. Held five years after the British Empire Exhibition in Wembley Park, London, and at the start of the Great Depression the event was held to encourage local heavy industry.
