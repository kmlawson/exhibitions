Dublin International Exhibition of 1865

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Dublin International Exhibition of 1865, which the texts also call the International Exhibition of Arts and Manufactures, was held in Dublin from 9 May 1865 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33; Allwood et al. 2001p. 30). The Prince of Wales opened it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Allwood et al. 2001p. 30). A private company, the Dublin Exhibition Palace and Winter Garden Company, organised it on the Cobourg Gardens site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). The texts say it was held. They differ on the number of foreign countries that exhibited and on the visitor figure in one table, and the only closing date in the passages read is 8 November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31; Allwood et al. 2001p. 206).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Two entries name the promoters as the Duke of Leinster, Lord Talbot de Malahide and the brewer Benjamin Lee Guinness (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). According to Daly, they wanted to extend Dublin's cultural and educational facilities. She describes Dublin as the second largest city of the British empire, with no institution that offered such resources to all social classes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova says the organisers were eager to prove to Ireland and the world that Dublin was the British Empire's "second city" in size, and in cultural and economic weight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33).

Ivanova dates the company to 1862 and gives its capital as £50,000, in 10,000 shares of £5 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). Daly says the company decided to hold an international exhibition to launch its new venture, and that the London exhibition of 1862 and the value of the Crystal Palace facilities influenced it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Allwood, Allan and Reid name Sir Benjamin Lee Guinness as the man who financed the exhibition, and say he had only just finished paying for the refurbishment of St Patrick's Cathedral (Allwood et al. 2001p. 30).

Site and buildings

Guinness had a 15-acre plot in the city, known to locals as the Cobourg Gardens (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). The company leased adjoining land from the Hutchinson estate and used the Kildare Street facilities of the Royal Dublin Society, which gave it 17 acres of exhibition grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). Daly gives the same 17 acres, leased on nominal terms by Guinness, in the south-east of the city near railway lines (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova gives the boundaries as Harcourt Street and Earlsfort Terrace to the west and east, and St Stephen's Green and Hatch Street to the north and south (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33).

Ivanova describes the final palace as five acres in three structures: a brick-and-stone main building, a winter garden building of iron and glass influenced by London's Crystal Palace, and a permanent stone annex (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). The June 1862 call for designs produced no winner, because no project met the £35,000 ceiling. The committee adopted plans by the Dublin architect Alfred G. Jones, to be revised under the company's advising architect, F. Darley (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). Daly calls Jones a London architect (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova dates the foundation ceremony to 12 June 1863, and names the engineers Ordish and Lefevre, the Dublin contractor Beardwood and Sons, and the Liverpool ironworks firm Rankin and Co. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe two buildings in Coburg Gardens, and say the iron-and-glass winter garden building was later dismantled and re-erected in London (Allwood et al. 2001p. 30).

Exhibits and attractions

Ivanova says the committee divided the exhibits into raw materials, machinery, textile fabrics, manufactures and fine arts. This followed the London exhibitions of 1851 and 1862, and drew on the Royal Dublin Society's experience with art exhibits since 1853 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Daly describes five industrial categories plus fine arts, shown in national groups and organised much as the London exhibition of 1862 was (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). The winter garden offered fountains, mazes and cascades, and a column of coal from Nova Scotia (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova describes a machinery annex that showed machines both at work and at rest, and pleasure grounds with fountains, gardens, a conservatory, a cascade and an archery field (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34).

Who took part

Ivanova gives the combined participation of 25 foreign countries and cities and 23 British colonies as 2,368 cases, as of 1 June 1865 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Daly gives 21 foreign countries and 22 British colonies, supplying more than 2,300 exhibits. She says the committee deliberately restricted British and Irish exhibits in favour of foreign ones, so that more than two-thirds of the exhibits were foreign (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Neither entry explains the difference. Ivanova gives Great Britain, including Ireland, 2,413 cases and 775 exhibitors, against 1,467 British exhibitors in 1853 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34).

Victoria took part directly. Its governor appointed an official board to organise its displays for the fair (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9). Hoffenberg cites a catalogue of the colony's products for the fair, and says that over one hundred cases were shipped to Dublin, including a cabinet collection of types of the colony's principal industries (Hoffenberg 2001p. 292). John G. Knight, the Victorian secretary, organised the colony's contributions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 283).

Visitors and reception

Ivanova says the organisers marched 600 soldiers of the 78th Highlanders through the unfinished galleries on 31 March 1865, as a test of the building's strength (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). The exhibition opened on 9 May and was formally closed on 8 November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Hoffenberg reports that Palmerston recommended a royal visit to the exhibition, and that the prime minister suggested the Prince of Wales open it, as a show of strength and attachment to the Crown, during Fenian challenges to English authority. The prince declared it open before over ten thousand spectators (Hoffenberg 2001p. 258).

Attendance was 956,295 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova says visitors "decreased by a fifth" from 1853, despite lower admission prices and cheap excursion tickets (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Daly says attendance was 200,000 below 1853, possibly because of an economic depression (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Receipts totalled £45,000, nearly a quarter of it from season tickets (£11,586) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Daly says receipts covered operating costs and a £10,000 rent for the building (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Hoffenberg quotes the London Leisure Hour, whose editors, writing at the time of the 1865 Dublin fair, said that at national displays employers, commissioners and officials took the credit for workers' productions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 183).

Empire and colony

The texts place the fair within the British imperial world, though none of the passages read describes how Ireland was governed. Daly calls Dublin the second largest city of the British empire (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Colonies took part in numbers that the two entries give differently: 23 British colonies in Ivanova's count and 22 in Daly's (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Ivanova places the British display, which includes its colonies, beside the machinery annex in the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Victoria's government prepared exhibits for the fair (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9).

Hoffenberg reads such royal openings as a ritualistic political and social centre of imperial culture, in which the Crown was presented as the centre of the empire (Hoffenberg 2001p. 258). The organisers' aims, as the passages give them, were cultural, educational and Irish. None of the passages read says that the organisers meant the fair to serve empire, trade or settlement (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33). Nor do they describe "native villages" or colonised people on display at this fair.

Aftermath and legacy

The company went on to hold smaller national exhibitions, and in the 1880s it failed to establish a permanent national industrial exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 32). Ivanova says the Royal University acquired the buildings in the 1880s, and that Earlsfort Terrace is now the site of Ireland's National Concert Hall (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34). Daly says the company folded and the buildings became Royal University property (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 32). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the winter garden building was re-erected opposite Battersea Park in London in 1885 as the Albert Palace of Science and Art, and was demolished nine years later (Allwood et al. 2001p. 30). Daly reports that it was felt Ireland needed a longer interval between international exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31).

Its place in the series

Lawn's chronology lists the Dublin fair between London 1862 and Paris 1867 (Lawn 2009p. 13). Ivanova's bibliography notes that Parkinson and Simmonds' 1866 catalogue drew comparisons between the 1853 and 1865 Dublin fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 17). Both entries measure 1865 against 1853 on attendance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31). Hoffenberg places Victoria's Dublin board three years after the colony's 1862 London displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Empire and colonial display — colonies and Victoria's board took part, and Hoffenberg reads the royal opening as imperial ritual (Hoffenberg 2001p. 9; Hoffenberg 2001p. 258; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34)
  • Private finance — a company capitalised at £50,000, a leased site and receipts of £45,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34)
  • Irish cultural purpose — the aim to prove Dublin's standing and to extend cultural and educational facilities (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 33; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31)
  • Foreign participation — the foreign majority of exhibits, and counts that differ between entries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 31; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34)
  • Labour behind the displays — a contemporary critique that Hoffenberg uses to show workers hidden at exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 183)
  • Afterlife of the buildings — Royal University, National Concert Hall, and the re-erected Albert Palace (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 34; Allwood et al. 2001p. 30)

Questions for study

  1. The entries give 25 foreign countries (Ivanova) and 21 (Daly). What evidence would settle the difference, and what does it show about using encyclopedia figures?
  2. Hoffenberg reads royal openings as imperial ritual. Which details in the Dublin passages support that reading, and what would a historian need before applying it to 1865?
  3. The entries present the fair as an attempt to prove Dublin's standing. How far do the visitor and receipt figures support that aim?
  4. Hoffenberg quotes a contemporary labour critique of exhibitions. What makes such a source useful for one fair, and what limits it?

Ivanova's encyclopedia entry sets out the origins, company, site, building, exhibits and attendance of the Dublin fair, and ends with a short bibliographical note. Its main line of argument concerns Dublin's standing: the promoters are said to have been eager to prove to Ireland and the world that Dublin was the British Empire's second city in size and in cultural and economic weight. The entry presents the fair as an effort to show what an Irish city could do on the scale of London's 1851 and 1862 exhibitions. It gives little interpretation beyond that, and its figures are reported without comment on how they were counted.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Daly's dictionary entry describes the fair as the project of a few wealthy Dublin backers who wanted to give the city cultural and educational facilities it lacked. Her account stresses the private company, its capital and its attempt to use the fair to launch a permanent venture. Her argument is that the venture failed: the company's later national exhibitions and its attempt at a permanent national industrial exhibition in the 1880s did not succeed, and the buildings passed to the Royal University. She compares the display and attendance with London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853, and she presents the fair mainly as a story of Irish ambition rather than of empire.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Brief discussion 2

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
Chapter 3, 'The Copyists... Paris Exposition Universelle and London Again', around printed p. 30 (l. 1648-1658); international exhibitions table (l. 10034)

Allwood, Allan and Reid's chapter on the copies of the 1851 exhibition gives Dublin 1865 only a short factual passage. It records that the fair opened on 9 May 1865 under the Prince of Wales, that a brewer, Sir Benjamin Lee Guinness, financed it, and that its iron-and-glass winter garden building was later re-erected in London as the Albert Palace. The authors' general comment on Victorian philanthropy in financing exhibitions is made about Dublin in 1853 and London in 1862, not about the 1865 fair. The table of fairs gives the 1865 Dublin profit, which the authors do not discuss.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
Chapter 1, printed p. 9 (note 43, l. 1203-1209); Chapter 6 'Machines-in-Motion', printed p. 183 (l. 9388-9394); Chapter 8, printed p. 258 (l. 13108-13120); Appendix B, Barry and Knight entries, printed pp. 281-283 (l. 14035, 14157); notes p. 292 (l. 14569-14572); bibliography p. 385 (l. 19372-19374)

Hoffenberg's book argues that exhibitions were a means of building imperial and colonial identities. For the Dublin fair the book makes only scattered points. It notes that Victoria's government organised exhibits for it, and reads the Prince of Wales's opening as part of an imperial ritual in which the Crown was presented as the centre of the empire. It also uses a contemporary labour critique, written at the time of the Dublin fair, to argue that workers were hidden behind the machines and displays of exhibitions. Most other Dublin references concern the 1853 fair or lists of cities, so the book does not treat this exhibition at length.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Lawn's introduction includes a chronology of international exhibitions from London 1851 to New York 1939, in which the Dublin fair appears only as a listed entry. The text says nothing more about it.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.
