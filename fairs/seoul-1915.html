Seoul · 1915

Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition

Shijŏng onyŏn kinyŏm Chosŏn mulsan kongjinhoe 始政五年記念朝鮮物產共進會
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Shijŏng onyŏn kinyŏm Chosŏn mulsan kongjinhoe) was held at Kyŏngbok Palace in Seoul from 11 September to 31 October 1915, a run of 51 days (Parkp. 1; Henry 2014p. 103). The Government-General staged it to mark five years of Japanese rule, setting new Western-style pavilions against the old palace halls (Parkp. 1; Parkp. 8). Park gives attendance as more than one million, while Henry gives 790,000 total admissions (Parkp. 3; Henry 2014p. 106). Both authors argue that the event was designed to teach Koreans to see colonial rule as progress (Parkp. 21; Henry 2014p. 98).

Where the dates and figures come from, and where the texts disagree

Duration: 'fifty days' (Park) against '51-day event' (Henry 2014). Dates 11 Sept-31 Oct 1915 are from Park; Henry 2014 gives no opening or closing dates. Visitors: 'more than 1,000,000' (Park, twice) against 790,000 total admissions with about 80,000 tour-group visitors (Henry 2014). An 1913 plan for a Seoul exposition (Taegyŏngsŏng pangnamhoe) was retitled a kongjinhoe; it has no separate entry.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Young-sin Park treats the exhibition as a cultural machinery, not only as political propaganda, and asks how it addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects (Parkp. 2). Todd A. Henry places the event in a series of palace-grounds expositions and reads them as a means of material assimilation, by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule (Henry 2014p. 92).

Park traces the exposition idea into Korea from the 1880s. Pak Chŏng-yang, after visiting an exhibition in Tokyo in 1881, wrote about the term pangnamhoe (Parkp. 4). A Korean mission to the United States, a year after the 1882 treaty, saw Western expositions at first hand (Parkp. 4). Yu Kil-chun's Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895 spread the word more widely (Parkp. 4). A Seoul exposition planned after the mission failed for lack of capital and after the coup of December 1884 (Parkp. 5).

Korea sent exhibits to Chicago in 1893 and to Paris in 1900. Park says the Chicago pavilion attracted little attention and that the Paris pavilion was shaped by the image of a Hermit Kingdom (Parkp. 5; Parkp. 5). The first event officially called a pangnamhoe in Korea was the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906. The Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 followed, which Park says drew about 200,000 visitors (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 3). Henry's account of the 1907 Keijo exhibition stresses Japanese control. Twenty-three Korean vendors showed goods at ten displays, against more than 160 exhibits by Japanese merchants (Henry 2014p. 94). A larger Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion, budgeted at 500,000 yen, was planned in 1908 but postponed after the Korean government failed to raise the funds (Henry 2014p. 96; Henry 2014p. 97).

In 1913 Governor-General Terauchi Masatake approved a plan for a great commemorational exposition in Seoul. He changed its title from pangnamhoe to kongjinhoe, insisting that it should not be called an exposition (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7). Henry records that in the autumn of 1913 the Government-General held a birthday ceremony for the Meiji Emperor on the palace grounds, with garden parties and kisaeng performances (Henry 2014p. 97).

Site and buildings

Kyŏngbok Palace was a prime symbol of the Chosŏn dynasty and the largest of its five grand palaces (Parkp. 7). Park reports that about 791.8 p'yŏng of floor space, including fifteen palace buildings and nine gates, was destroyed to make room for the new pavilions on the east side. Older halls such as Kŭnjŏng Hall were kept on the west as small exhibition rooms (Parkp. 8; Parkp. 9).

Henry describes the clearance in harsher terms. Planners called the palace structures ruins and proposed to sweep away 123 palace buildings. As many as two-thirds of such structures were transferred to Japanese owners, eateries and Buddhist temples (Henry 2014p. 98; Henry 2014p. 98). The two figures measure different things. Henry's 123 is the planners' proposal, while Park counts the structures that were actually destroyed (Henry 2014p. 98; Parkp. 8).

The new halls were temporary wooden structures of pine and cedar, decorated in Renaissance and Secession styles. Park notes that technically the style was closer to the American Beaux-Arts (Parkp. 10; Parkp. 10; Parkp. 10). Henry describes Building One, the first hall visitors reached, with two central towers of 25.5 metres. The Machine Hall and the Special Forestry Hall blocked the view of Kŭnjŏng Hall (Henry 2014p. 99; Henry 2014p. 99). Park reports a numbered route of twenty stops that led visitors from the modern pavilions to the old palace space (Parkp. 9). The staged contrast between modern and premodern was, for Park, the most important design feature of the exhibition (Parkp. 6).

Exhibits and attractions

The halls used three display methods, which Henry calls museological, dichotomising and decontextualising. The museological method classified goods by standard categories, and the dichotomising method set a backward Korean past against a progressive colonial present (Henry 2014p. 104; Henry 2014p. 104). The third method, decontextualising, treated the shaman as a superstitious figure under police and hygienic control (Henry 2014p. 104; Henry 2014p. 104). Park adds that the Second Main Exhibition Hall showed penal reform and hygiene, with bacteria specimens and medical appliances (Parkp. 14).

Amusements were central. Henry says the Keijo Cooperative Committee doubled its allocation for entertainment to 100,000 yen, and amusements occupied about a third of the palace grounds (Henry 2014p. 101; Henry 2014p. 101). Admission-based attractions included a performance hall, a circus, a zoo and a georama (Henry 2014p. 101). Officials, through the former Agricultural and Commercial Affairs Minister Matsuoka Ko-ki, warned that without the ideological aim the event could easily become a festival (Henry 2014p. 102; Henry 2014p. 103). Park describes the electric illumination, which Korean visitors called "the exhibition of fire" (Parkp. 17). He also records that the shop area resembled a modern department store, and, drawing on Walter Benjamin, he describes the commodity display as "the universe of commodities" (Parkp. 18; Parkp. 18). The Mitsukoshi kimono shop sold fabric for Western-style suits and opened a branch office in Kyŏngsŏng (Parkp. 19; Parkp. 19).

Who took part and visitors

Park's visitor section begins with the American missionary Edwin Wade Koons, who described a crowd that behaved well: after almost ten days, no one had got drunk or argued (Parkp. 15; Parkp. 16). Park also cites William Arthur Noble, who described people from the most distant mountain villages who had never seen the capital (Parkp. 17). Park says that the contemporary print media offered propagandistic appraisal managed or censored by the Government-General, which is why he turns to Koons's report (Parkp. 15; Parkp. 15).

Henry gives 790,000 total admissions over 51 days. In the first 48 days almost 70 per cent of them came from outside the capital (Henry 2014p. 106; Henry 2014p. 106). Henry also puts group visitors at under 7 per cent of all exhibition-goers (Henry 2014p. 106). Later he says that 80,000 people attended in 1915 as members of tour groups (Henry 2014p. 121). That is about a tenth of 790,000, so the two figures do not reconcile. On Ladies' and Children's Day, 17 September, nearly 14,000 Koreans and 14,620 Japanese attended. The daily averages were just over 9,000 Koreans and 6,500 Japanese (Henry 2014p. 110; Henry 2014p. 110).

Visitors read the exhibits in their own ways. A Kangwŏn guide, Mr Chang, took a water pump for a waterfall, and a pupil from the Sakurai Common School wrote that the water never seemed to go down (Henry 2014p. 108; Henry 2014p. 109). Park's list of the most popular sites, from a Hyesan survey, puts a parade and a fountain at the head, followed by a stuffed whale and charcoal from P'yŏngyang (Parkp. 18). In the last three days free admission drew nearly 290,000 Koreans and 84,000 Japanese, and Monkey Day on 30 October drew over 90,000 Koreans (Henry 2014p. 111; Henry 2014p. 111).

Discipline, health and control

Park calls the exhibition an efficient training machine for a civilised modern subject (Parkp. 16). He notes that the Special Department of Hygiene was set up in June 1915 under an ordinance on communicable diseases, and that sanitary police carried out household inspections (Parkp. 16; Parkp. 16). Park's Maeil shinbo note says that only three police officers took on the cleaning and food-hygiene inspections at the site (Parkp. 16).

Henry's figures show the limits of control. About one hundred patrol officers were deployed, and police cited over 64,000 improprieties, most of them outside the display halls (Henry 2014p. 105; Henry 2014p. 105; Henry 2014p. 105). More than half the recorded arrests were of ethnic Japanese, although Korean visitors outnumbered them about two to one (Henry 2014p. 105). Guides were largely idle, and the Keijō nippō mocked them in a cartoon (Henry 2014p. 105; Henry 2014p. 105). Authorities urged visitors to take notes, but left Koreans to make the links between exhibits and progress by themselves (Henry 2014p. 106).

Politics and propaganda

Park reads the exhibition as propaganda that helped establish the legitimacy of annexation while concealing brutal colonial control (Parkp. 19). He also calls it a colonial exposition conceived by the Japanese government as a more coercive and oppressive machine (Parkp. 19).

Korean reformists welcomed the exhibition as a means of industrial growth and self-strengthening. Yun Pong-kyun recorded in his diary the significance of seeing expositions (Parkp. 20). The reformist journalist Chang Chi-yŏn urged visitors to learn how to develop their own industries (Parkp. 20; Parkp. 20). Kim Yun-sik, by contrast, complained that prices had soared and that county governments had collected group tour costs from local people (Parkp. 20). Park draws on Tom Nairn's argument that nationalism grows out of uneven development (Parkp. 20).

Its place in the series

Park counts some thirty large and small expositions and industrial exhibitions held in Kyŏngsŏng and local cities between 1915 and 1940 (Parkp. 21). He calls 1915 the first government-sponsored national event of the colonial period, and links it to the Chosŏn Exposition of 1929 and the Great Chosŏn Exposition of 1940 (Parkp. 21; Parkp. 21).

Henry's chapter ends with the Korea Exposition of 1929, which commemorated twenty years of colonial progress (Henry 2014p. 113). An administrative complex completed in 1925 stood on the site of the 1915 exhibition (Henry 2014p. 113), and the 1929 exposition was held behind it (Henry 2014p. 113). Its organisers drew on the 1915 techniques (Henry 2014p. 114), but they dropped the 1915 approach of Western architectural styles (Henry 2014p. 116). The halls were built in what the press called "pure Korean style" (Henry 2014p. 117). Henry notes that the 1929 display halls were criticised by a reporter who called one of them the "hall for the promotion of assimilation" (Henry 2014p. 128). Henry's comparison is explicit: the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity were already visible in 1907 and 1915 (Henry 2014p. 129).

How historians have interpreted it

Two authors treat the exhibition at length. Young-sin Park's article appeared in the Journal of Asian Humanities at Kyushu University in 2021 (Park). Park reads the exhibition as an apparatus that produced a new modern Korean subject, divided between disciplined citizen, consumer and colonised subject (Parkp. 2; Parkp. 21). Todd A. Henry's book Assimilating Seoul (2014) reads the palace-grounds expositions as a device for material assimilation, and argues that visitors' own pursuits quietly subverted the intended message (Henry 2014; Henry 2014p. 92; Henry 2014p. 93).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Japanese visitors and firms

Japanese visitors are counted separately from Koreans in Henry's figures, and they made up a substantial share of the crowd (Henry 2014p. 110; Henry 2014p. 110). Japanese companies also took part. Mitsukoshi opened a branch office in Kyŏngsŏng after selling at the exhibition (Parkp. 19), and Japanese firms used kisaeng images in advertisements (Henry 2014p. 101).

Korea in Japan

The Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914 provided the model for the Seoul architecture. Its Western-style pavilions stood for modernising Japan, while the Chosen (Korean) Pavilion was built in a traditional native style (Parkp. 9). Neither text reports sections for Chinese, Taiwanese, Manchurian or Southeast Asian participants at the 1915 exhibition.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Modern and premodern on display — New Western-style halls faced the old palace, and the displays set a backward Korean past against a progressive colonial present (Parkp. 6; Henry 2014p. 99).
  • Discipline and surveillance — Park reads the exhibition as a Panopticon-like apparatus that disciplined visitors through dress, behaviour and hygiene rules (Parkp. 1; Parkp. 16). Henry shows the limits of police control (Henry 2014p. 105).
  • Spectacle, amusement and consumption — Lights, rides, shops and souvenirs drew many visitors away from the displays; both authors treat this as a failure of the lesson (Parkp. 17; Parkp. 18; Henry 2014p. 101; Henry 2014p. 112).
  • Visitors and tour groups — Rural notables brought in by group tours formed the basis of the press accounts, and visitors responded in their own ways (Henry 2014p. 106; Henry 2014p. 106; Parkp. 18).
  • Public health — Park presents hygiene displays and sanitary regulations as civilising measures (Parkp. 14; Parkp. 16).
  • Nationalism and development — Park links reformist support for the exhibition to self-strengthening and to Nairn's account of uneven development (Parkp. 20; Parkp. 20).

Questions for study

  1. Park says the print media gave propagandistic appraisal managed or censored by the Government-General (Parkp. 15; Parkp. 15), and he turns to a missionary's report instead. How far can such sources reveal what ordinary visitors understood?
  2. Henry's count of 790,000 admissions and Park's figure of more than one million cannot both be right. Which of the official reports that both authors draw on would settle the question? (Parkp. 3; Henry 2014p. 106)
  3. Park records Korean criticism of the 1915 exhibition in Kim Yun-sik's diary (Parkp. 20), while Henry's critics come mainly from 1929 (Henry 2014p. 121). Why does the 1915 criticism receive less attention?
  4. The palace was cleared, and the old halls reused, to present the palace as a historic relic rather than as a national symbol (Henry 2014p. 98). What did that require of the buildings, and how did visitors respond?

In the contents of this work

The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

In the contents of this work

Representations of “Modern Korea” in the 1915 Exhibitionp. 7Asia

Park examines the architecture, layout and display of the 1915 exhibition as a way of representing 'modern Korea'. The chapter draws on the official report, the exhibition guide and visitor routes printed in Maeil shinbo and Keijō nippō, and on posters, photographs and postcards. It shows that the organisers built Western-style pavilions on the east of the Kyŏngbok Palace site, destroying a large part of its structures, and kept older palace halls on the west as small exhibition rooms. Visitors followed a numbered route from the 'modern' pavilions to the 'premodern' or 'old' space. Displays of penal and hygiene systems, provincial development and products were arranged to show Korean backwardness against Japanese progress. Park situates the architecture in the Tokyo Taisho exhibition of 1914 and in Renaissance and Secession styles, and concludes that the buildings served the colonial claim to have made a 'new modern' Korea.

In the contents of this work

Experiencing Modernity and “New Modern Subjects”p. 15Asia

Park turns from the organisers to the visitors, asking how ordinary Koreans experienced and responded to the exhibition. The main sources are the Maeil shinbo's reports, the American missionaries Edwin Wade Koons and William Arthur Noble, and a survey of visitors' favourite sites. Park argues that the exhibition aimed to produce disciplined, 'civilised' viewers through rules on dress, behaviour, toilets and sanitary inspection, but that many visitors were drawn instead to electric illumination, rides, the entertainment area and shopping. Souvenirs and the Mitsukoshi kimono shop show the exhibition as a 'universe of commodities'. Reformist commentators such as Chang Chi-yŏn and Yun Pong-kyun welcomed it as a means of industrial growth and self-strengthening. The chapter concludes that the Korean subject shaped by the exhibition was divided between the disciplined citizen, the consumer and the colonised subject.

In the contents of this work

Conclusionp. 21Asia

The brief conclusion treats 1915 as the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea, with some thirty large and small expositions held between 1915 and 1940. Park argues that the exhibition was a cultural machinery producing a conflicted Korean subject, shaped by seduction and spectacle, education and discipline, and coercive colonial control. It ends by linking this to a Korean nationalism understood as an ambivalent response to uneven development.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.

Wikipedia

The Chōsen Industrial Exhibition (朝鮮物産共進会, Chōsen bussan kyōshinkai) was a colonial fair held in 1915 to mark the 5th anniversary of the establishment of Japanese Korea, and was the first official event of the new government. It was held in Keijō (Seoul) at Gyeongbokgung.
