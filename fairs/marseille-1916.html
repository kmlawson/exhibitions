Colonial Exposition, Marseille (planned 1916)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyOn the web

The colonial exposition planned for Marseilles in 1916 was to follow the city's 1906 colonial exposition and recur every ten years after it (Hale 2008p. 87). Jules Charles-Roux was again named commissioner general, a groundbreaking was held in 1913, and the war put the plans on hold in August 1914 (Hale 2008p. 86p. 87). After the armistice the organisers moved the starting date to April 1922, and the 1922 colonial exposition took its place (Hale 2008p. 87p. 88). Rydell treats the plan briefly, as one episode in a dispute with Paris (Rydell 1993p. 63), and Hale gives it about a page (Hale 2008p. 87).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Planning before the war

The 1916 plan grew out of the 1906 Exposition coloniale. Hale says its success in attendance and presentation led the city to "plan another colonial show in 1916 and every ten years thereafter" (Hale 2008p. 87). Charles-Roux had acted as commissioner general for the 1906 show (Hale 2008p. 87). For the new show the ministry again named him to the post, and Hale stresses that Marseilles now had the full support of the state (Hale 2008p. 87).

The president of France went to Marseilles to open the groundbreaking in 1913. Charles-Roux's staff and government representatives in the colonies went on with the organisation until the war intervened (Hale 2008p. 87). Hale says the war interrupted construction of the pavilions, but the texts do not say what the pavilions were to contain, which colonies were to build them, or how far construction went (Hale 2008p. 87).

Empire and colony

The texts link the 1916 plan to empire through its sponsors and its model. The Ministère des Affaires étrangères et des Colonies named the commissioner, and the state provided support (Hale 2008p. 87). Rydell describes the planned event as a sponsored exposition of French colonies in Marseilles in 1916 (Rydell 1993p. 63). Hale's account of the 1906 show, whose success prompted the new plan, has colonised people from each colonial region making craftwork or performing, and some of them living on the site in traditional dwellings (Hale 2008p. 87). Neither text says which colonies or dominions would have taken part, which goods or peoples would have been shown, or whether there would have been a native village section for 1916 (Hale 2008p. 87; Rydell 1993p. 63). The texts state no aims for the 1916 show beyond its predecessor's success (Hale 2008p. 87).

Rivalry with Paris

Rydell and Hale both describe a dispute over where France's colonial fair should be held, but they date it differently. Rydell places an acrimonious debate between Marseilles and Paris politicians over the site before the government split the difference, sponsoring a Marseilles fair in 1916 and an international colonial exposition in Paris in 1920 (Rydell 1993p. 63). Hale places the dispute after the armistice. The Paris Municipal Council had approved a colonial exposition by France and its allies for 1921 or 1922, and Ernest Outrey, who represented Cochinchina in parliament, had introduced a petition for a similar Paris event in the early 1920s (Hale 2008p. 88).

Hale describes the Provençal press as seeing the Paris move as central government usurping a regional initiative. One newspaper argued that a second Marseilles exposition would increase business for colonists and merchants (Hale 2008p. 88). Its author suggested that the exotic features of a Paris exposition would draw idle sightseers and day-trippers rather than encourage trade (Hale 2008p. 88).

Hale gives the outcome as a compromise in spring 1919: Marseilles would hold its exposition in 1922, and Paris would sponsor a colonial exposition with its allies in 1925 (Hale 2008p. 88). The two texts therefore give different dates for the Paris event: Rydell 1920 (Rydell 1993p. 63), Hale 1925 (Hale 2008p. 88). Hale says Maréchal Hubert Lyautey, a friend of Charles-Roux, apparently persuaded the government to let Marseilles proceed in memory of the dead commissioner (Hale 2008p. 88).

After the war

Neither text says the 1916 fair was cancelled. Hale says the war put the plans on hold in August 1914 (Hale 2008p. 87). Days after the armistice, the mayor of Marseilles and the head committee resumed preparations and moved the starting date to April 1922 (Hale 2008p. 87). Charles-Roux died in September 1918, and Adrien Artaud, president of the Chamber of Commerce and adjunct commissioner, was named commissioner general (Hale 2008p. 88). Rydell reports that Marseilles leaders clamoured for permission to proceed within two days of the armistice (Rydell 1993p. 63).

Its place in the series

Hale describes the 1922 Marseilles exposition as following the 1906 pattern, and as stressing the native soldier and the indispensability of overseas troops to France (Hale 2008p. 88). Rydell says the 1922 Marseilles Colonial Exposition and the 1924-25 British Empire Exhibition at Wembley were both conceived before the First World War began (Rydell 1993p. 63). Hale notes that the Paris exposition of 1931 followed years of administrative problems and several postponements (Hale 2008p. 88).

Themes in the scholarship

  • State sponsorship — Hale stresses that the ministry named the commissioner and that the state gave full support to the 1916 plan (Hale 2008p. 87). Rydell calls the decision a government compromise between two cities (Rydell 1993p. 63).
  • Marseilles against Paris — Rydell and Hale both record a dispute over the site, but date it differently. Hale gives the Paris proposal for 1921 or 1922 and a press argument for Marseilles (Hale 2008p. 88).
  • War and postponement — Hale says the war put the plans on hold and that the start date then moved to April 1922 (Hale 2008p. 87).
  • The 1906 model — Hale presents the 1916 plan as a repeat of the 1906 success, and describes colonised people taking part in that show (Hale 2008p. 87).

Questions for study

  1. The two texts date the Paris fair differently, 1920 and 1925. What might explain the difference, and how would a student check it?
  2. Hale describes the 1906 show with colonised people working and living on the site. What, according to the texts, was planned to be shown in 1916, and what is missing from the record?
  3. Charles-Roux was named commissioner general for the 1916 show. What changed when Adrien Artaud took over after his death in 1918?
  4. Why did Marseilles and Paris both want the next colonial fair, and what did each city say it would gain?

Moderate discussion 1

Hale, Dana S. Races on Display: French Representations of Colonized Peoples, 1886–1940. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2008.
Introduction to Part II, section 'Background and Organization of the Interwar Colonial Expositions', printed pp. 87-88

Hale's introduction to Part II argues that the First World War changed the French view of the empire, because colonial labourers, soldiers and supplies sustained France. It then traces the state-backed colonial expositions of the interwar years, and the planned Marseilles show of 1916 appears as the first stage of that story: a repeat of the 1906 success, a commissioner general named again by the ministry, a groundbreaking in 1913, and a war that put the plans on hold. Hale gives the dispute with Paris over the next colonial fair real space, and presents the 1919 compromise as the origin of the 1922 Marseilles event. The introduction states that later chapters examine the communication of French racial and cultural superiority through expositions and trademark images, so the 1916 plan serves as background to that larger case.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hale asks how the Third Republic represented its colonial subjects and, through them, its own identity. Using trademark labels registered in Paris and Marseilles from 1886 to 1940 and the colonial sections of the fairs in those cities, the book compares sub-Saharan Africans, North Africans and Indochinese. Part I (1886–1913) covers the empire, racial theory and the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900; Part II (1914–1940) covers the Marseilles and Paris expositions of 1922, 1931 and 1937. A final chapter treats France's self-image as parent and brother to its colonial children. Hale argues that the images stressed human difference and French superiority, and that this framework still shapes France's ties with its former colonies.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are central to the book, but almost entirely through Indochina, one of the three colonial groups the study is built around. Chapters 4 ('Indochinese: Gentle Subjects') and 7 ('Indochinese: Fils Doué') are devoted to it, covering trademarks and the 1900, 1922, 1931 and 1937 fairs. Chapter 1 covers Indochina's conquest and its pavilions at the 1889 Paris fair, and chapter 8 treats the Indochinese as part of France's self-presentation. Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos appear as conquered territories and as exhibits: the Angkor Wat reconstructions of 1922 and 1931, the Cambodian royal ballet, the Théâtre Annamite, the dragon procession and the Rue Annamite, with artisans, soldiers and performers housed on the fairgrounds. Asian visitors include the prince of Annam in 1889. East Asia gets only passing attention. Chinese appear as neighbours seen as troublesome in the Tonkin affair that opens chapter 4, and as workers in pay and hours disputes (chapter 4), and as rickshaw pullers whose planned employment in Paris in 1931 was dropped after the Chinese consul protested (chapter 7). Japan and Korea get no discussion, though kimono-clad women recur on trademarks. In Southeast Asia, Thailand appears only as a threat to Cambodia, and the Javanese dancers of 1889 and the Malays of the 1931 ethnographic directives are passing mentions; the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma are absent.

Brief discussion 1

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
Chapter 3, 'Coloniale Moderne', printed p. 63

Rydell's chapter on the 'Coloniale Moderne' argues that interwar colonial expositions made empire look like part of modernity, and that the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, with its official American representation, set in motion an American version of the imperial revival. The planned Marseilles fair of 1916 appears only in the chapter's opening history, as the outcome of a quarrel between Marseilles and Paris politicians over the site of a colonial fair. Rydell uses that history to introduce the 1922 Marseilles fair and the 1924-25 Wembley exhibition as the start of the interwar cycle, not to study the 1916 plan itself.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.
