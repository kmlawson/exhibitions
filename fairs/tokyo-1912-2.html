Tokyo · 1912 · Cancelled

Grand Japanese Exhibition (proposed world's fair)

Nippon Dai-Hakurankai
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Grand Japanese Exhibition (Nippon Dai Hakuran Kai) was a planned international exhibition for Tokyo. It was decided in April 1907 to run from 1 April to 31 October 1912 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). Count Kaneko Kentaro chaired its government committee (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41p. 42). It was postponed to 1917 in early 1908 for financial reasons and again, indefinitely, in 1912 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Its committee was dissolved after the war began in 1914 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Findling and Pelle say World War I cancelled the 1917 successor (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404). Hotta-Lister argues that the 1910 London exhibition was meant to advertise the 1917 fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The plan originated within the Japanese government. It was meant to be an exhibition of international character, with foreign governments and peoples taking part (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). Hotta-Lister calls it the Nippon Dai Hakuran Kai (The Grand Japanese Exhibition) (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The authorities made it international in part because of the recent victory over Russia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Its dates were chosen for their symbolism: 1 April for the cherry blossom and 31 October for the chrysanthemum (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). In October 1907 Kaneko, as committee chairman, argued that an international exhibition was an essential ingredient for a country passing through a transition to industrial development, and that its effects would be several times its cost (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 184).

Organisers and finance

In June 1907 Komura Jutaro, the ambassador in London, wrote to Grey inviting British exhibits, especially in science, the arts, education, machinery and electricity (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41p. 187). The Board of Trade advised in August 1907 that the government should delay its decision (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). Kaneko was elected chairman of the Grand Japanese Exhibition Committee in August 1907 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42), and an official committee was formed under him with other leaders in charge of sections (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The budget was 20 million yen and the site was 250 acres in Tokyo (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Invitations went out in June 1907 to various countries. The United States voted several million US dollars and sent three officials to Japan for a feasibility study (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Money then decided the timetable. In early 1908 the government postponed the exhibition to 1917 because of financial difficulties after the war with Russia, and because its cost had been underestimated (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Hotta-Lister also records MacDonald's report that the government postponed it because of the country's financial situation (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42).

Postponement and the 1917 plan

In early 1908 Komura asked Tokyo to consider Kiralfy's proposal for a joint exhibition in London, citing the Franco-British Exhibition as an ideal example and the chance to advertise the Grand Exhibition of 1912 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42p. 187). Tokyo did not commit itself, and as late as 11 July 1908 it held back (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). The cabinet decided on 16 October 1908 to take part in the London exhibition, to be held in 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 45). The author thinks a provisional decision by the previous cabinet may have followed the decision to postpone the Grand Exhibition of Japan (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 45). Wada and Beppu, who ran the London exhibition, had also been appointed to the postponed Grand Exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 50).

Komura's 1908 proposal to the cabinet named advertising the 1917 fair among the London exhibition's purposes (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). Dr Furuichi was sent to London to study the 1910 exhibition in preparing a feasibility study for the 1917 exhibition, and Wada and Beppu were appointed to similar work (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). In December 1910 the Chuo Shimbun reported that 410,000 yen was proposed for the 1911 budget of the 1917 exhibition. Its new commission chairman, Hirayama Seishin, said it would be three times the size of the Osaka exhibition of 1903 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189). Hotta-Lister estimates the 1917 budget at 20 million yen, ten times that of the London exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189). She calls the 1917 fair a "never-to-be-realized mirage" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189). In 1912 the plan was postponed again, indefinitely, but the author says it was not yet abandoned (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189).

Why it was not held

The Committee Office was officially dissolved after the Great War began in 1914 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Findling and Pelle give other accounts. Their Osaka 1970 entry says a fair scheduled for 1912 was cancelled after the death of Emperor Meiji (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). Their 1917 entry says the plan was "cancelled because of World War I" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404). The texts give different dates and causes for the end of the plan.

Hotta-Lister reports Maeda Masamori's view. Maeda had been involved in Paris exhibitions. He thought conditions in Japan were not adequate for many Western visitors, citing roads and transport. He was disappointed by the postponement but thought it the better course (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 218). Hotta-Lister herself writes that the postponement and cancellation, though much regretted, could have been a wise decision (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 218).

Its place in the series

Hotta-Lister places the plan after the Osaka exhibition of 1903, which had an international atmosphere with eighteen countries taking part (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). An earlier scheme, proposed in 1889 for a celebration of 2,550 years, was dropped because the time was not yet ripe (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). She says the Osaka 1970 exposition fulfilled at last the aspirations of the planned 1917 exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 186). Findling and Pelle record that a fair was hoped for in 1922, in conjunction with the Tokyo Peace Memorial Exposition, but preparations could not be made in time (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405). They also record a 1940 Grand International Exposition, sited in Tokyo and Yokohama, which war caused to be postponed indefinitely (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405). Tickets sold for the 1940 fair were honoured at Osaka 1970 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Finance — The texts link the postponements to the government's money: the cost had been underestimated, and the plan's budget was 20 million yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217p. 189).
  • London as a trial — Hotta-Lister argues that the London exhibition was used to advertise and prepare the 1917 fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188).
  • Readiness of Japan — Maeda's view that conditions in Japan were not adequate for many Western visitors is the only account of readiness in the texts (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 218).
  • War — The 1914 war ended the committee's work in Hotta-Lister's account (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217); Findling and Pelle give World War I as the cause for the 1917 fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404).
  • Successor fairs — Hotta-Lister ties the 1917 plan to Osaka 1970 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 186); Findling and Pelle tie the 1912 and 1917 plans to a 1922 hope and the 1940 Tokyo fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405).

Questions for study

  1. Findling and Pelle give the Emperor's death (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339) and World War I (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404) as causes, while Hotta-Lister gives finance (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Which account does the evidence support best, and why do the dates differ?
  2. Hotta-Lister treats the London exhibition as a trial for the 1917 fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). What could a trial of this kind show, and what could it not show?
  3. Maeda put the problem in roads and transport (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 218). How far do the texts support the view that Japan's infrastructure, rather than finance, decided the matter?
  4. The Tokyo plan and the London exhibition shared commissioners (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 50). What did this shared staff mean for the Tokyo plan's chances?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
2Preparationsp. 38Asia

Hotta-Lister shows how deeply the Japanese government committed itself to the London exhibition and how far the two sides differed in approach. The idea came from the entrepreneur Imre Kiralfy, who had staged imperial spectacles and organised the White City site; Komura took it up in Tokyo, while Kato, Mutsu Hirokichi and the commissioners Wada and Beppu handled it in London. The chapter reconstructs the earlier rejections of Kiralfy's plan and the rivalry with possible American and Russian exhibitions. It then covers the 1.8 million yen budget, the exhibitors' association, the selection of 19,943 articles from 1,286 exhibitors, and the gardens, models and entertainers shipped from Japan. British responses were lukewarm: the Board of Trade and Grey gave little official support, while a General Committee under the Duke of Norfolk formed and the Japan Society was given its own space. It ends with the opening on 14 May 1910, two days late after the death of Edward VII.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 4

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
Untitled opening section (no heading in text)p. 1Asia

Coté opens by placing the Semarang International Colonial Exhibition of 1914 within the history of world's fairs, which he presents as stages for a discourse of progress and a pedagogy of mass education. Colonial exhibitions, he argues, carried the same didactic aims as European ones but faced a particular problem: could native audiences read them correctly? Drawing on Prakash's account of British India and on Dutch participation in world exhibitions from London in 1851 to Paris in 1931, he shows how the Netherlands used such displays to present itself abroad. The Semarang exhibition, planned in 1912, postponed from 1913 and staged by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, is described through its scale, its architecture, its electric lighting and the foreign pavilions of Japan, China and two Australian states. Coté concludes that the exhibition, opening as war began in Europe, was a settler-led showcase whose meanings could not be fixed, and that it marked the beginning of the end of the imperial future it celebrated.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
4From the Opening Day Until the End of the Exhibitionp. 100Asia

This chapter narrates the Exhibition from its opening on 14 May 1910 to the receipt of the official reports in 1912. Hotta-Lister estimate attendance at over eight million and describe the evening illuminations, fireworks and package tours that drew crowds. Most of the chapter is a dated record of visits and events: Prince Fushimi's attendance at King Edward's funeral, banquets for British and Japanese dignitaries, Japan Day, and visits by Kitchener, Marie Stopes, the Swedish royal couple and the Brazilian president-elect. It also records a private viewing of retrospective Japanese art for about 350 European experts. The authors then trace the afterlife of the exhibits, which were shipped home, given to British and European institutions, or sent to exhibitions in Budapest, Dresden and Turin. Little is argued here; the chapter is a record of ceremony and diplomacy that frames the reactions discussed next.

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Panama-Pacific International Exposition, San Francisco, 1915: East Meets West in the Gardenp. 133Asia

This chapter examines the landscape of the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco, which it presents as a fair that joined Eastern and Western garden traditions on land made from the bay. It explains how the Harbor View site was created by dredging, and how Edward Bennett's block plan of courtyards was undermined when he resigned in 1912 after accusing the city's Department of Works of pernicious influence. Most of the chapter concerns John McLaren, the local landscape designer whose plantings are described in detail: the ice plant hedge that imitated an aged wall, the palm and cypress avenues, and the courts that the chapter treats as early small-space gardens. Its other principal cases are the Palace of Fine Arts and its lagoon, the Japanese garden, and the Horticulture Palace, whose exhibits moved from single fruit specimens to commercial processes. The chapter also covers the quarantine inspections that imported plants required. It ends by tracing the fair's legacy through the trees planted in Golden Gate Park and the de Sabla garden at El Cerrito.

In the contents of this work

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
2The Master of the Formp. 39Asia

Peterson argues that by 1915 Japan had become the master of the international exhibition format, and that its self-representation at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco was built on staged encounters rather than on objects alone. He starts with diplomacy: the 1912 groundbreaking, the gold 'Loving Cup' and the public performances of Japanese and American officials that presented the two countries as peaceful partners on the Pacific. The first and longest case is the Japanese government site, with its temple-like pavilion and garden, where Peterson reads photographs of kimono-clad women as evidence of how fairgoers performed an emotional attachment to the place. He then turns to the commercial 'Japan Beautiful' concession in the Joy Zone, with its miniature Asakusa-style village, geisha and Mount Fuji tea house, then to the trade halls selling ceramics, lacquer and silk, and to the fine arts galleries, where San Francisco critics praised traditional work and dismissed Western-style painting. The chapter ends by arguing that Japan knew what America wanted to see, a pre-modern and refined nation, and supplied it.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Findling and Pelle list the Tokyo fair planned for 1912 as one of the world's fairs that were planned but never held. Their entry is a short paragraph: the fair was put back to 1917 as a larger 'Grand Exhibition of Japan', and it was cancelled because of the First World War. Elsewhere the dictionary gives a different reason for the 1912 fair's collapse, the death of the Emperor Meiji, and links the failed plans to the later Tokyo 1940 project. The treatment is a summary of the reference-work kind, not an argument about the fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
