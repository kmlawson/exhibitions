Melbourne · 1888–1889

Centennial International Exhibition (Melbourne)

Australian Centennial Exhibition
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Centennial International Exhibition opened in Melbourne, capital of the colony of Victoria, on 1 August 1888 and closed in early 1889 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). It marked the colonial centenary, and about two million people came (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Historians treat it as a display of colonial nationhood at the height of Melbourne's boom, and as the last great fling of an era of extravagance, before the financial collapse of 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Victoria's prime minister, Duncan Gillies, pushed the plans forward, while officials in Sydney, which had waited too long to prepare, were only moderately enthusiastic (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Sydney, the older colony, did not host the fair. Dunstan's entry explains that the New South Wales leader, Sir Patrick Jennings, was trying to curb public debt, and that Sydney's Garden Palace had been lost to fire in 1882 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97). Dunstan presents the fair as a celebration of the colonial centenary and of the colonists' sense of nationhood (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97).

The fair was meant to mark more than Victoria. Hoffenberg says it set out to commemorate the development of Australasia since the first convict settlement, and to mark Australia's entry into the international community. For that purpose, "the commissioners invited exhibitors from Great Britain, India, Canada, South Africa, Ceylon, North America, Europe, and East Asia" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12).

Site, organisation and finance

The fair used the Exhibition Hall in Carlton Gardens, built for the Melbourne International Exhibition of 1880–81. Its quadrangle was roofed again and extended to more than 35 acres of exhibition space (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Dunstan gives a different measure: the planners first expected 31 acres, and the fair finally covered 38 acres, the largest public event Australians had yet attempted (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97). The public entrance moved to Nicholson Street, and the interior was repainted in a more lavish style by the decorative artist John Clay Beeler (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97).

Chief Justice George Higinbotham was appointed president of the exhibition commission, and an English committee led by Sir Graham Berry was formed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97). The commissioners set the opening and closing dates in January 1887, resolved to open in the evenings, and offered motive power to exhibitors free of charge (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97). They also chose to light the buildings by electricity, a costly decision that needed generating machinery and more than a thousand arc lamps and two thousand incandescent lamps (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97).

The fair lost a great deal of money. Powell gives a deficit of £238,000, and says it led to Higinbotham's resignation, with Sir James McBain succeeding him (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Dunstan gives a cost of nearly £238,000 against an original estimate of £25,000, and records that the organisers defended the loss by pointing to money paid to Victorian workers, extra railway and customs revenue, and new trade (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The reference table in another source gives a building-only loss of about £237,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Hoffenberg lists British government direct expenditure at £6,557 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 286).

Attendance is also given differently. Powell gives almost 2.2 million visitors and a closing date of 31 January 1889 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Dunstan gives more than two million, nearly double the population of Victoria, and dates the close to February 1889 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The table in a third source gives 2,003,593 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Before 1888 the Exhibition Hall had been built and landscaped for the 1880–81 show at a cost of more than £150,000. Between 1880 and 1888 parts of it served as a concert hall, a government printing shop and an aquarium (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Jules Joubert advised Australian officials planning the Centennial to follow the South Kensington shows, which opened at night and had shops, lit gardens and concerts. He wanted a display on a scale fitting a centenary (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).

Australian commissioners had let exhibitors price their goods at most colonial shows, the Melbourne Intercolonial among them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 77). A generation later, Melbourne shopkeepers called the sale of priced exhibits inside the Centennial halls unfair competition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 77). Badger, writing of fairs in general, says promoters used visitors' spending, new business and rising land values to win heavy financial support from city governments (Badger 1979p. 11). The Melbourne texts give no comparable figures for those gains.

Exhibits and entertainment

Victoria had twice the space of any other nation, and Dunstan records a weapons court with Aboriginal and "native" weapons, including examples from Borneo and arms from China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell says Victoria's exhibits covered nearly eight acres, and that Australian exhibitors took about half the floor space (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).

Machinery and power were a theme. Hoffenberg reports that local officials boasted their machinery courts demonstrated how hand labour had been almost entirely superseded, taking dress shirts and collars as the example (Hoffenberg 2001p. 176). The organisers provided a 1500-horsepower steam engine and two Brush dynamos for their galleries (Hoffenberg 2001p. 178).

The cultural programme set the fair apart. Dunstan says Frederic Cowen was paid £5000 for six months, and that major works by Beethoven, Brahms and Wagner were performed in Australia for the first time (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell gives more than 260 concerts over six months for about half a million people, and more than 2,000 paintings on loan from Britain, France and other countries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). Powell says the most popular attractions were sideshows such as the switchback railway and the concerts (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106), and Dunstan says extra sideshows were added when attendance fell in November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98).

Nations and colonies on show

The United States frontage was given over to sewing machines, with the Singer Company occupying two large stands where women demonstrated the machines (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell also records a model of the Anheuser-Busch brewery in St. Louis in the American court (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).

Germany's court was the second largest, with pianos from eighty-five exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell says an architect sent from Berlin decorated it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). Hoffenberg reports that a former president of the Victorian Chamber of Manufactures toured its art and economic displays, and then visited Germany to study its art and industrial schools (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). Hoffenberg also reports English unease about German and other European exhibitors at the Australian fairs of the 1880s (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). Geppert records that Germany had its own sections at Melbourne (Geppert 2010p. 16), and names Adolf Wermuth as German general commissioner for the fair (Geppert 2010p. 278).

Dunstan says the French contribution disappointed, because the French were preoccupied with their own fair in Paris in 1889, though a model of the Eiffel Tower drew attention (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell says the largest and most interesting French exhibit was that of the ministry of public education (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). The British display ranged from machinery to Wedgewood (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).

New Zealand artists also took part. Some of Eleanor Mair's portraits of Maori were shown at the Melbourne fair in New Zealand's fine art display, and the British pictures on show at Melbourne were requested for the Dunedin exhibition of 1889–90 (Filipová 2015p. 244; Filipová 2015p. 241).

Dunstan gives more than ninety countries exhibiting (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Powell gives ninety-three participating states, with the United States, Britain, France, Germany and Austria-Hungary the chief nations, and notes Austria's Bohemian glass and furniture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).

Geppert places human displays in a wider network. Displays of native peoples had become a standard feature of European exhibitions by the 1880s (Geppert 2010p. 48). A German journalist in 1896 listed the Melbourne fair of 1888–89 among world's fairs where such shows had been staged (Geppert 2010p. 48). Geppert does not say what Melbourne showed, and the other texts here are silent on it.

Ceremonies and the labour procession

At the 1880 opening, union men with banners lined the way for the governors, and they had to buy tickets for the ceremony (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264). For the Centennial, the order of march invited representatives of forty other labour societies from the Trades' Hall, among them seamen, tailors and gas stokers. Some of them sat on the executive committee (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264). Hoffenberg reads this as a sign that organised labour had been admitted to Melbourne's public culture (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264). Rank was harder to settle. The colony had no herald's office to fix precedence. The chief justice, who objected that the speaker had usurped his place, stayed away from the opening altogether (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264).

Visitors and the city

Visitors found Melbourne "flat and dusty", but it had more large public buildings than any other British city outside London (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Most were fascinated by the "fast and silent cable trams", and the new electric light drew attention (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). The viewing platform round the great dome put the city's own citizens on display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Mrs G. F. Cross, an Anglican vicar's wife, came many times with her children and recalled the free education on offer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The novelist Ada Cambridge noted that lovers monopolised some of the exhibition's spaces (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98).

Goods, gifts and the Crown

A Renaissance-style chateau housed the furniture of Wallach Brothers, which the Argus said expressed "the luxury of modern life" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The temperance commissioner James Munro opposed displays of brewing and winemaking, without success (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Cellars held wines and dairy produce, and bakers and confectioners gave away samples of tea and cocoa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Dunstan lists paintings by Turner, Leighton and Landseer in the British loan collection (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The royal family supplied electrotypes of the Tower regalia and gave paintings by Turner and Landseer to the Adelaide and Melbourne centennial shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 262).

How historians have interpreted it

Dunstan argues that the fair expressed intercolonial cooperation and national sentiment, but that historians have not seen it as seminal to federation, the union of the colonies in 1901. Dunstan calls it "the last great fling in an era of extravagance," and says its memory discouraged any comparable international event for a long time (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99). Powell agrees that the fair was an expression of pride. Powell says the fair, like the pageant poems produced for it, celebrated Melbourne's progress, "its vigorous business and social life, its widening repute" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105), and calls it a symbol of Australia's patriotism and achievement (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). The two differ on the verdict. Powell says most people judged the fair a great success despite press criticism, and places it at the twilight of Melbourne's golden age of prosperity (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106). Dunstan stresses what came after: a collapse of real estate values, unemployment and bank failures in 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99).

Hoffenberg reads the Melbourne fair within a wider argument. Colonial international exhibitions, Hoffenberg says, were efforts to increase trade and status, celebrate imperial ties and negotiate local or national identities (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Hoffenberg's reading gives more weight to commerce and imperial anxieties about Germany than Dunstan or Powell do (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). The introductory essay in the volume edited by Filipová reads the Australian colonial fairs as displays that promoted settlement and investment, and that often served British dominance and imperial authority (Filipová 2015p. 14). Roche, citing MacKenzie, reads colonial exhibitions as a celebration of white settlers' transplanted societies (Roche 2000p. 58). The imperial readings of Hoffenberg, Filipová and Roche stress the fair as a display of power; the national readings of Dunstan and Powell stress it as a celebration of Australians.

Hoffenberg says the colonial processions defined and represented the national and civic communities and their places in the wider empire (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264). Peterson, writing on Brisbane's Expo 88, calls the Melbourne fair the first official exposition on Australian soil (Peterson 2020p. 143). Sydney's international exhibition of 1879 came earlier (Hoffenberg 2001p. 263; Lawn 2009p. 13), so that claim does not hold. Dunstan's entry recommends Ada Cambridge's memoir Thirty Years in Australia (1903) and points to Davison, McCarty and McLeary's Australians 1888 (1987) for wider context (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Invitations and India

The commissioners invited exhibitors from India, Ceylon and East Asia to mark Australia's entry into the international community (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12). Our texts do not say which East Asian states, or which Indian or Ceylonese firms, took up the invitation. Hoffenberg records one commercial link with India: P. G. King, of a New South Wales mining company, corresponded with the Indian Department of Revenue and Agriculture about Australian horses for the Indian Army, and passed on information about colonial horses from the fair's official catalogue (Hoffenberg 2001p. 120). Hoffenberg treats this as an existing trade connection that the exhibitions promoted, and tried to expand (Hoffenberg 2001p. 120). Belcher, the Danish vice consul at the fair, conversed with an unnamed Indian commissioner and with Australasian governors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 255).

Weapons, tobacco and kiosks

Dunstan records a weapons court that held Aboriginal and "native" weapons, with examples from Borneo and arms from China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). The Germans had a "lagerbier kiosk", and a "Manila tobacco-house" had cigars (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98). Our texts do not say who supplied these objects, or who organised or paid for the Borneo, Chinese and Manila displays. Apart from the unnamed Indian commissioner, they describe no Asian visitors, observers or pavilions. The fair's organisers were the Victorian commissioners (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97).

Reading the presence

Hoffenberg reads the invitations within the trade-and-status argument about colonial exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). The other sources in this set do not discuss an Asian presence at the fair. Powell describes the courts without mentioning Asia (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106), and Allwood and colleagues mention the fair only in Joubert's career, in a table and in a comparison with Expo 88 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54; Allwood et al. 2001p. 169).

Indian tea at the Melbourne shows

The Indian tea association, whose members were tea planters, displayed and sold tea at exhibitions in Melbourne, Glasgow, Paris, Chicago and Antwerp in the decade after its 1886 London Tea Court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). The text does not date the Melbourne show, so it may or may not be this fair. James Inglis, a former planter and India's commissioner at the early Australian exhibitions, worked with the association and the Indian Department of Revenue and Agriculture to exhibit and sell Indian teas and other South Asian goods at the Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). The superintendent of the Madras Central Museum had shown his region's teas at the Melbourne exhibition of 1880–81 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118), which was a different show. These passages add Indian commercial exhibits, but no pavilion.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Nationhood and centenary — the texts read the fair as colonial pride in a century of settlement, but differ on how far it was national (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).
  • Victoria's boom and its end — the fair is tied to Melbourne's prosperity and to the losses and bank failures that followed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99).
  • Machinery and electricity — the machinery courts, the steam engine and the electric lighting were shown as evidence of technical progress (Hoffenberg 2001p. 176; Hoffenberg 2001p. 178; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97).
  • Music and the cultural programme — the orchestra and the art loans set the fair apart from its predecessor (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106).
  • German and European exhibitors — German displays aroused English unease about European trade and imperial rivalry (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122; Geppert 2010p. 16).
  • Asian invitations and objects — the invitations to India, Ceylon and East Asia, and the Borneo and Chinese weapons, are the only Asian traces in these texts (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98).

Questions for study

  1. Why did Victoria, rather than Sydney, host the Australian centenary fair, and what do the texts suggest about rivalry between the colonies? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 97; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105)
  2. Dunstan calls the fair a "last great fling," while Powell judges it a great success. What evidence would decide whether it was a moment of national confidence or the peak of a boom? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 99; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106)
  3. The reference works give different attendance figures and closing months. How should a historian choose between them, and what does the disagreement reveal about records of exhibitions? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98; Allwood et al. 2001p. 207)
  4. The commissioners invited exhibitors from India, Ceylon and East Asia, but these texts name few of them. What sources would show who displayed what, and who paid for it? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12)
  5. Hoffenberg links German displays to imperial unease. How far can a court of sewing machines or pianos carry that kind of argument? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 98)
  6. The editor of the Filipová collection and Roche read colonial fairs as displays of British power. Does the cultural programme at Melbourne complicate that reading? (Filipová 2015p. 14; Roche 2000p. 58; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 106)
  7. Did the Indian tea association exhibit at the 1888–89 fair, or only at the 1880–81 show, and who paid for its exhibit and staff? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Hoffenberg 2001p. 118)
  8. Peterson calls the Melbourne fair the first official exposition on Australian soil. Why would a 2020 study date it that way when Sydney had held one in 1879? (Peterson 2020p. 143; Hoffenberg 2001p. 263)
  9. Geppert's 1896 journalist lists Melbourne among fairs with displays of native peoples. Who were the people shown in Melbourne, and where did they come from? (Geppert 2010p. 48)

Chapters that treat it at length 3 in 1 work

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
1Exhibitions and the New Imperialismp. 1Asia

Hoffenberg argues that the popular exhibitions held in London and in colonial cities such as Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta between 1851 and 1914 deserve as much study as the Great Exhibition that overshadows them. The chapter shows how colonial commissioners used these shows to present Australia as a progressive settler society and India as a restored tradition. Its evidence includes the Doulton 'Impasto' panels at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, the Zenana Days in Calcutta, and Aboriginal Australians displayed at Sydney and Adelaide, cases in which visitors and exhibits sometimes returned the colonial gaze. The chapter also traces the afterlife of the shows in museum collections, catalogues and the classifications used to organise them. It argues that the exhibitions promoted an imperial market and a federated commonwealth, while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. It closes by claiming that exhibitions shaped social order through participation rather than coercion, and that their ideas about imperial federation carried into the Commonwealth.

6Machines-in-Motion: Technology, Labor, and the Ironies of Industrialismp. 166Asia

Hoffenberg examines the working machines that drew crowds at English, Australian and Indian exhibitions from 1851 onwards. Machinery courts, reapers and steam-powered demonstrations presented industrialism as something visitors could watch and test, but the chapter argues that the same displays hid the labour that built and ran them. Machinery halls were framed as idealised factories, and visible hand workers, such as Indian artisans and women in the craft courts, were placed beside self-acting machines to suggest a harmonious industrial order. Hoffenberg shows this with Doulton panels at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, and with the building sites of London in 1862 and Sydney in 1879, where accidents and strikes were left out of official albums. Exhibitions did help diffuse new technology across England and the colonies, and machines kept on in museums and technical schools extended that reach. The chapter ends with the armaments displays, where guns and warships were admired as beautiful objects, so that the peaceful pledge of 1851 was undercut by war.

In the contents of this work

8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 4

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
6Genesis and Growth of the Ideap. 43

Badger traces how the idea of a Columbian world's fair in 1892 developed in the decade after the centennial, from scattered proposals in Baltimore, Mexico City and Washington to a contest between Chicago and New York. Chicago's clubs, newspapers and businessmen built a campaign that stressed the city's centrality and its claim to speak for the West, while New York papers portrayed it as a raw outpost boasting of its grain, lumber and meat. The chapter follows the lobbying in Congress, the House vote of February 1890 that gave the fair to Chicago, and the ten million dollar guarantee that the city's committee pledged without consulting its citizens. Paris in 1889 overshadows the story, because American visitors measured their plans against it and judged the national display there a discredit. The chapter ends by noting that Chicago now had to make good its claim, and that the dual structure of the local company and the national commission would complicate the work.

Gosden, Chris, and Chantal Knowles. Collecting Colonialism: Material Culture and Colonial Change. Oxford: Berg, 2001.
2Colonial Culture and History in West New Britain

Chapter Two bridges the general argument and the detailed fieldwork by giving a history of western New Britain. It describes pre-colonial societies organised in small cognatic hamlets, linked by Siassi trade, languages and ceremonies, and shows how colonial rule reshaped them. The core is a division into two periods: German rule from 1884 to 1914, when a recruiting and trading economy gave way to plantations, head taxes, luluai and tultul offices and large coastal villages; and Australian administration from 1914 to 1942, under military rule and then the mandate, with foot patrols, the Expropriation Board and competing Anglican and Catholic missions on the south coast. The authors draw on patrol reports, letters and plantation records, using the 1888 Ritter collapse, the epidemics of the 1890s, the recruiting ban and Arawe Plantation as cases. They close by noting that the collectors worked as temporary members of this colonial society, and turn to the collections.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
3Commissioners and the State: Power and Controversy at the Exhibitionsp. 63Asia

This chapter asks how exhibition commissioners justified their authority as a new cultural elite, and why that authority drew criticism. Hoffenberg argues that commissioners presented themselves as disinterested experts serving the state, yet their work of selecting, pricing and displaying objects exposed them to charges of self-interest and commerce. The main evidence is the Crystal Palace of 1851, where the executive commissioners forbade price tags, and the London International of 1862, which radical papers denounced as a jobbery run by a 'Science and Art clique'. Colonial cases, including the Melbourne Centennial Exhibition, the Adelaide Jubilee show of 1887 and the sales rules at Calcutta in 1883-84, show how on-site selling set commissioners against local merchants. Hoffenberg then compares state funding in Australia and India, which was direct and generous, with England's ad hoc royal commissions, and traces critics from Babbage to the radical press who attacked the commissioners from opposite sides. The chapter ends by treating exhibitions as contests for public opinion, in which the shows revealed the tensions they were meant to conceal.

Qureshi, Sadiah. Peoples on Parade: Exhibitions, Empire, and Anthropology in Nineteenth-Century Britain. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2011.
7The End of an Affairp. 222Asia

Qureshi uses Frank Fillis's Savage South Africa, staged at Earl's Court in 1899, to show how African and Afrikaner performers were displayed in late Victorian London. The argument rests on the show's mock kraal, its reenactments of the Ndebele wars and the press coverage that framed them. Qureshi sets these against earlier British fairs, above all the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, where performers worked as attendants and craftsmen inside the main displays rather than in segregated villages. Paris in 1889 and Chicago in 1893 supply the contrasting model of the native village and the Midway. The chapter then turns to anthropologists such as Galton and Rivers, who used performers and collections at the 1886 exhibition, and argues that British researchers exploited displayed peoples less systematically than their German counterparts. It ends by suggesting that by 1900 the shows had become detached from anthropology and tied to entertainment and imperial spectacle.

This reference-work entry on the Centennial International Exhibition is the fullest account in the set. Dunstan covers the colonial background, the choice of Victoria over Sydney, the site, the electric lighting, the courts and their exhibits, the music programme, the attendance and the finances. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Wikipedia

The Melbourne Centennial Exhibition of 1888–1889 was organised to celebrate a century of European settlement in Australia. The Exhibition Building, constructed in 1880 for the Melbourne International Exhibition, was extended and reused. The Centennial Exhibition focused on Australia itself, and emphasised music and painting that attracted many visitors. However the exhibition was not recognised by the Bureau of International Expositions as a "world's fair". Parer & Higgins Co. paid £1250 to operate the bar and light refreshments, one of only three areas where the sale of alcohol was permitted. Dora Elizabeth Armitage was at the exhibition, where she won a medal for her typing. Her testimonial about her Calligraph typewriter was used by its manufacturers in their advertising.
