Yeosu 2010 exposition bid

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The Yosu 2010 bid was a South Korean plan to hold an international exposition at Yosu City in 2010 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). Allwood, Allan and Reid list it among the 2010 bids under consideration, beside Shanghai, Russia (Moscow) and Mexico (Mexico City) (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211). The authors place the Yosu project in the south of the country, with oceanology as its theme, as part of the Asian use of expositions for national and regional development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The texts name no organisers, budget or outcome, so they do not show whether the bid was pursued, won or lost (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The earliest passage on the bid comes in the authors' account of Kunming Expo 99. There they write that China was preparing a major event for Shanghai in 2010, and that China would compete with Korea, among others (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). They then describe "a project for Yosu City on the Wosu peninsula in the south of the country", with oceanology as its theme (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The passage gives no year for the Yosu project (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The authors set both the Shanghai and the Yosu projects within the Asian use of the exposition as a tool of economic and national development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).

Bidding rules and competition

The 1988 protocol to the 1928 convention allowed one BIE-registered exposition in each five-year cycle (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). The authors say that prospective hosts had to give notice ten years in advance, and that China, Korea and Russia were lining up for 2010 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). Their own rules summary gives a window of five to nine years before the event (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211), so the two statements disagree. The authors argue that the rigid five-year interval encourages competition for every fifth year, with bidding of the kind that has disgraced the Olympics (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). They also write that the 1988 system virtually guarantees four international expositions in each decade (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). In the wake of Hanover 2000, the debate has intensified among nations contemplating the next expositions in 2005 and 2010 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 192).

Outcome and place in the series

The texts do not say whether the Korean bid was pursued, won or lost. The authors note that Japan won the competition for 2005, with a plan more modest than Germany's for Hanover and essentially specialised (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). They write that Korea would doubtless be content, like Japan, with something akin to the specialised type (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). They also say that Korea had already exploited the 1988 protocol, but the passage does not name the event (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). Text 26 lists Yeosu among expositions held since 2010, but gives its date as 2012 and does not connect it with the bid.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Asian expositions as national development — the authors place the Yosu project beside the Shanghai plans as a continuation of the Asian use of expositions for development (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).
  • The 1988 BIE rules and bidding — the five-year cycle and the notice period shaped when hosts bid for 2010, and the authors' two accounts of the notice period do not agree (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196p. 211).
  • Competition for hosts — China (Shanghai), Korea (Yosu City), Russia and Mexico are listed as bids under consideration, with the authors describing the competition as costly (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211p. 197).
  • Oversupply of expositions — the authors argue that the system produces too many expositions for the medium to sustain (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197).

Questions for study

  1. The Yosu project is given an oceanology theme but no year at its first mention. What other evidence would show that it was a bid for 2010 in its own right?
  2. The authors describe the BIE rules as allowing one registered exposition each five-year cycle. How could a host pursue a 2010 bid under those rules, and which notice period applied?
  3. The authors expect Korea to choose a specialised rather than a universal exposition. What arguments in the texts support that expectation, and what would test it?
  4. Text 26 names an exposition at Yeosu in 2012. What would a student need to find to show that this event was or was not the 2010 bid?

Molella, Arthur P., and Scott Gabriel Knowles, eds. World’s Fairs in the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of Progress. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2019.
16Does the World’s Fair Still Matter? Discovering New Worlds after 1989Scott Gabriel Knowlesp. 194Asia

Knowles asks whether the world's fair still matters after 1989, and examines the fairs planned during or after the Cold War, from Plovdiv in 1991 to Shanghai in 2010. The argument is that these fairs were shaped by the political changes of their moment and in turn helped to shape them, as host cities used them for urban renewal, national self-presentation and reform. The chapter moves fair by fair. Plovdiv's young-inventors expositions of 1985 and 1991 are read as a shift from Cold War showcase to a sign of Bulgarian transition, and Seville 1992, Lisbon 1998 and Hannover 2000 as bids for European integration, reunification and a sustainable image. Daejeon 1993 is presented as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China. Knowles relies on press reports, Bureau of International Expositions assessments and the fairs' own films, and records the American difficulties at Seville and Hannover. The chapter ends by pointing to Dubai 2020 and a possible American return, presenting the fairs as theatres for national visions.

In the contents of this work

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
10Conclusionp. 279Asia

Peterson's conclusion looks beyond Milan to the future of national representation at world's fairs. He argues that the 2015 Milan Expo, with its cost overruns, unfinished pavilions and protests, suggests that the grand European exposition is unlikely to be revived. Asia has taken the lead as host, and Peterson weighs Shanghai 2010, with its 73 million visitors and forced displacement of residents, against Dubai 2020, whose site relied on migrant labor. The Chinese pavilion at Milan, with its animated family film and live Hani dancers, is read as a more effective affective encounter than the Korean and Thai pavilions. Drawing on Volcic and Andrejvic, Peterson proposes that states now 'nationalize the sell', so that future expositions are more likely to exploit national identity than to say anything meaningful about it. He ends by arguing that the analytical frameworks for studying these displays must change, though they will remain performative, visual and increasingly virtual.

Allwood, Allan and Reid's reference work mentions the Korean project only briefly, as one of several bids for 2010. It sets a Yosu City project on the theme of oceanology beside the Shanghai 2010 event that China was preparing, and calls both part of the Asian use of expositions as a tool of national and regional development. In its final chapter the authors argue that the 1988 bidding rules encouraged crowded competition for each five-year slot, so that China, Korea and Russia were lining up for 2010. The text gives no outcome for the Korean bid.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.
