Glasgow International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Glasgow International Exhibition of 1901 ran from 2 May to 9 November in Kelvingrove Park, on the ground used for the city's 1888 fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). It commemorated the fiftieth anniversary of the Great Exhibition of 1851 and opened Glasgow's new Art Gallery and Museum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Carbone calls it "the largest of its kind in Great Britain to date" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Historians read it as a display of industry, empire and civic pride (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Lawn 2009p. 187).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The 1901 fair built on the 1888 exhibition. Carls treats it as a grander successor, held to celebrate the new museum and to commemorate the 1851 jubilee, the fiftieth anniversary of the Great Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carbone gives the same two purposes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). The museum was paid for partly from the 1888 profits, together with an Art Gallery Building Fund appeal (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81; Lawn 2009p. 187).

Carbone notes that the fair opened in mourning for Queen Victoria, who had died that January, and during the Boer War, the South African conflict then in progress (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Carls says the fair extolled Britain's world supremacy, a claim the entry ties to technological virtuosity and colonial domination, and that it was staged during an economic boom (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Greenhalgh names the Glasgow fairs of 1901, 1911 and 1938 among the larger shows that carried the imperial theme to a grand scale (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58).

Organisation, site and finance

The fair used 75 acres of Kelvingrove Park, virtually the same ground as in 1888 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). The Duchess of Fife opened it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164); Carls names the duke and duchess (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). The officials were Lord Blythswood as president, Henry Anthony Hedley as general manager and James Hunter Dickson as treasurer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310).

The texts disagree on the profit. Carbone and Carls report a surplus of more than £35,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 173). Allwood, Allan and Reid give a profit of £30,571 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Badger's appendix lists receipts of £404,105 and a cost of £350,600 (Badger 1979p. 131).

Attendance is less contested. Carbone gives 11.5 million visitors, double the 1851 figure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Carls and Allwood, Allan and Reid also put it at twice the 1851 total (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Badger's appendix prints 11,559,649 (Badger 1979p. 131).

Site and architecture

James Miller designed the buildings, which Carls describes as prefabricated white wood-and-plaster pavilions in a Spanish Renaissance style (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carbone describes Miller breaking up the white facade of the 200,000 square foot Industrial Hall into pseudo-Spanish, Turkish and Venetian massings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164), and links the main building's style to Chicago 1893 and Buffalo 1901 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). The Industrial Hall, lit with electric lights under a golden dome, held most of the space. Machinery Hall lay behind the museum and was reached by the Grand Avenue (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165).

The museum was the centrepiece. Carls credits Simpson and Allen of London with its design, and Carbone calls it a red sandstone Spanish-Jacobethan bulwark (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164).

Mackintosh's role is disputed. Allwood, Allan and Reid write, "Unfortunately he was not invited to submit a design" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81), and call the main building "a very mundane building which had even less to offer than Captain Fowkes’ 1862 design" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Carbone says Mackintosh designed four pavilions for industrialists and the Glasgow School of Art, though his bold designs for the main halls were rejected (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carls states, "Curiously and conspicuously absent was design representative of Glasgow’s own art-nouveau style" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Art Nouveau was a curving, decorative style of the late nineteenth century. Carbone finds the Glasgow Style in George Walton's portrait studio for T&R Annan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165).

An Angel of Light stood on top of the golden dome of the Industrial Hall, holding an electric torch (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Carbone puts an 18-foot statue of Edward VII directly under that dome (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the dome as carrying biblical quotations and the names of Scottish inventors, and place beneath it a statue of the king at twice life size (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). The two texts disagree on the size of the statue. After the dome, visitors passed rows of giant steam locomotives and exhibits from the colonies and foreign countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Carbone says the Art Galleries and the Industrial Hall, angled towards each other at the top of the park, formed a backdrop to the foreign and commercial pavilions below (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Among those pavilions were James Templeton and Company's Thousand and One Nights, a mosque-like pavilion, and Miss Cranston's Tea Room, placed directly in front of the art galleries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). A massive circular Concert Hall in the park was used for the opening ceremony and held 4,000 people (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172).

Exhibits and attractions

Exhibits were grouped in eight classes, from raw materials and manufactures to machinery, marine engineering and sport (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carls adds women's, education, photographic, Scottish history and fine art sections (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). For Carbone the photography displays were the most novel: photography was recognised as an art at a world's fair for the first time, shown in the art galleries by James Craig Annan and by Alfred Stieglitz's Pictorialist exhibition, a style of photography aiming at painterly effects (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). A loan of 160 works by William Burrell would become the basis of his gift to the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165).

The entertainments were varied. Carbone lists motor-car trials, football matches, a water chute and a switchback railway (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Both Carbone and Carls mention an Indian theatre of jugglers and snake charmers (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 173). Carls also lists a model farm with a working dairy, electric launches on the Kelvin, band concerts and organ recitals (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 173).

William Burrell's loan of 160 works included James McNeill Whistler's La Princesse du pays de la porcelaine (1863-64), which Carbone says is now in the Freer Gallery in Washington (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carbone also lists a plaster version of Rodin's Burghers of Calais (1888) and Burne-Jones's Danae or The Tower of Brass (1887-88) among the most modern works, set amid an encyclopedic display of modern English art. Both were later given to the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). The Saracen Fountain in front of the two main buildings was the work of Walter MacFarlane, a Glasgow ironfounder (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Near the Lever Brothers Sunlight workers' cottage, which Carbone says may have caught the eye of Glasgow's industrialists, the fair also offered soap sculpture of Queen Victoria, marzipan polar bears and an Eddystone lighthouse made of sewing spools (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165).

Nations and colonies on show

Carls names Canada, Ireland, Japan and Russia as the countries with national pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carbone describes a Canada pavilion with displays for would-be emigrants, Irish cottages, and a Russian village of four wooden pavilions on which the czar spent some £30,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carbone reads the Russian display as a sign of strong trade between Russia and Scotland (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Allwood, Allan and Reid mention a Russian village and exhibits from the colonies and foreign countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Walker worked with James Miller on a Canadian pavilion for the 1901 fair (Filipová 2015p. 145).

The imperial frame runs through the sources. Carls links the fair to colonial domination (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172), and Roche lists the Glasgow fairs of 1888, 1901 and 1911 among British imperial expositions and festivals (Roche 2000p. 42). The Asian displays are treated separately below.

Visitors and reception

The texts say little about how visitors responded. Carbone calls the Pictorialist photography show much acclaimed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carls says the site was transformed into something like a turn-of-the-century amusement park (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Some contemporaries called the fair the New Country Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172).

Carbone says that the king's subjects, somewhat disrespectfully, arranged to meet "under King Edward’s nose", that is, under the statue (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Visitors could watch Singer sewing machine demonstrations or have a walking stick made to measure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). After dark the buildings and grounds were lit with coloured electric bulbs, and a searchlight first shown at Chicago was turned on the crowds to announce the daily closing time (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164).

Aftermath and legacy

The Art Gallery and Museum outlasted the fair. Limond calls it an important icon of Glasgow's civic pride, then and now (Lawn 2009p. 187). Carbone notes that the Saracen Fountain, one of the few remainders of the fair, was moved to Alexandra Park in 1914 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). The Glasgow Room of the Mitchell Library holds the largest collection of the fair's primary sources (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 166).

Women and workers at the fair

Carbone says that women's contributions were spread through the exhibition grounds under a Women's Committee rather than kept in one place (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Photography was also shown in the Trades section as a worthwhile employment for women (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carls adds a Women's Section that highlighted the products of women's labour (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carbone does not name the members of the committee, and the texts give no figures for women exhibitors or workers.

Influence on other towns

Filipová says that the Wolverhampton Arts and Industrial Exhibition of 1902 was in many ways inspired by the Glasgow Exhibition of the year before (Filipová 2015p. 144). It was started by Thomas Graham, owner of the Express and Star, with support from other industrialists and the town council. Its aim was to be cosmopolitan, with national and international exhibitors (Filipová 2015p. 144). The texts do not say which features of the Glasgow fair Wolverhampton took as its model.

How historians have interpreted it

The main readings differ in focus. Carbone reads the fair through art and design, calling it surely demonstrative of Glasgow's economic flowering at the close of the Victorian era (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 166). Carls reads it as a claim to imperial supremacy built on technological virtuosity and colonial domination (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Allwood, Allan and Reid judge the main building harshly and set Glasgow against the Turin exposition of 1902, whose Art Nouveau architecture they describe as adventurous (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Greenhalgh places the Glasgow fairs in a wider argument that British commitment to empire at exhibitions never slackened from 1851 to 1940 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Roche gives France and later the United States the leadership of the expo genre after 1862 (Roche 2000p. 42). Limond calls the 1901 and 1911 fairs significant but sets them aside, and reads the museum as civic pride (Lawn 2009p. 184; Lawn 2009p. 187). Smith describes the Scottish fairs as a rivalry Glasgow won convincingly, with Glasgow exhibitions bigger and more successful than Edinburgh's (Filipová 2015p. 211).

The sources agree that the fair was tied to the 1851 jubilee and the museum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Allwood et al. 2001p. 81; Lawn 2009p. 187). Carls, Greenhalgh and Roche all place it in a British imperial frame (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172; Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Roche 2000p. 42). They differ on the Glasgow Style: Carbone finds it in the pavilions and the photographic studio, while Carls finds it absent (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). They also differ on profit: Allwood, Allan and Reid give £30,571, while Carbone and Carls give more than £35,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 173). Limond sets the fair aside, while Carbone calls it the largest of its kind in Britain (Lawn 2009p. 184; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 164). Badger, Geppert and Hotta-Lister give figures or a footnote rather than argument (Badger 1979p. 131; Geppert 2010p. 323; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222).

Limond's discussion of the rivalry concerns the 1888 exhibition. He says the claim that Glasgow's first venture was a response to Edinburgh's 1886 show is routinely made and greatly exaggerated, and that Glasgow's elite looked to London, Paris and New York rather than to Edinburgh (Lawn 2009p. 184). Smith, by contrast, allows that the alternating series of Scottish exhibitions may express a rivalry, and if so, "a competition Glasgow won convincingly" (Filipová 2015p. 211). He says Glasgow's shows were bigger and more successful in attendance and receipts, and that Glasgow became Britain's premier exhibition city outside London (Filipová 2015p. 211). For comparison, Smith gives the Edinburgh show of 1886 about 2,750,000 attendances and a surplus of around £5,500 (Filipová 2015p. 211).

The Greenhalgh passage on Glasgow used here concerns the 1938 show. There he says it was "less overtly imperialistic in aim and far more progressive" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 188). He adds that Glasgow's exhibitions generally kept a more enlightened spirit than London's (Greenhalgh 1988p. 188). This qualifies Carls's reading of the 1901 fair as a claim to imperial supremacy (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172), although Greenhalgh does not make the point about 1901 alone.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say little about Asian displays at this fair. Japan is the clearest case. Carls lists Japan among the four countries that built national pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). Carbone places Japan's pavilion in the half-demolished Kelvingrove Mansion, painted white with a large black sun over the statue of Britannia, inside a traditional Japanese garden (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carbone also notes that Glasgow had exchanged exhibits with Japan since 1878 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Hotta-Lister's appendix of Japan's international exhibitions records Japan's expenditure on the Glasgow International Exhibition of 1901 as 57,718 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222). The texts do not say who organised the Japanese pavilion or who paid for it beyond that expenditure figure.

Indian and Cinghalese performers and staff

The outside attractions included an Indian theatre with jugglers, athletes and snake charmers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 173). Carbone lists the same theatre among less highbrow entertainments (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). Carbone also notes that the Cinghalese waiters at Prince's Restaurant evidently surprised local people with their ability to speak perfect English (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). The texts do not say who organised the theatre, who employed the waiters, or where the performers and waiters came from.

The King of Siam and other visitors

Carbone's list of the fair's curiosities includes the appearances made by the King of Siam, placed beside those of the incognito Empress Eugenie (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165). The text gives no date, place or capacity for these appearances, and none of the other texts for this fair mentions him. No text interprets the Asian displays. The only frame offered is the wider colonial one Carls applies to the whole fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 172). The texts say nothing else about Asian commissioners, observers or visitors, or about how audiences received the Asian displays.

Japan after London 1910

Hotta-Lister's appendix prints the Japanese expenditure column in yen, which confirms that the 57,718 figure for Glasgow is yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221p. 222). Hotta-Lister says that after the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London, several countries asked Japan to send exhibits, naming Manchester, Glasgow and Edinburgh among British cities (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 184). That request came after 1910, not 1901, and Hotta-Lister does not link it to the 1901 pavilion. Carbone is the only text that describes the Japanese pavilion of 1901 in any detail (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 165).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Jubilee and museum — The fair commemorated the 1851 jubilee and opened the Art Gallery and Museum, which the 1888 profits helped pay for. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Lawn 2009)
  • Imperial and colonial display — Carls ties the fair to colonial domination, and Greenhalgh and Roche place it among British imperial shows. (Findling & Pelle 1990; Greenhalgh 1988; Roche 2000)
  • Architecture and the Glasgow Style — Miller and Simpson and Allen designed the buildings, and the texts disagree on whether Glasgow's own style was on show. (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • Photography as art — Carbone says photography was recognised as an art at a world's fair for the first time. (Findling & Pelle 2008)
  • National pavilions — Japan, Canada, Ireland and Russia built pavilions, and Walker worked with Miller on a Canadian pavilion. (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Filipová 2015)
  • Finance and attendance — The profit figures differ (£30,571 against more than £35,000), while attendance is put at 11.5 million and twice the 1851 total. (Allwood et al. 2001; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Badger 1979)
  • Glasgow against Edinburgh — Smith reads the Scottish fairs as a rivalry Glasgow won. (Filipová 2015)

Questions for study

  1. Carbone and Carls disagree about whether the Glasgow Style appeared at the fair. Which sources would settle that question, and how would each reading change our picture of the fair's architecture?
  2. The profit figures in the texts do not match. How would you decide which record to trust, and what does the gap suggest about how exhibition accounts were kept?
  3. Greenhalgh reads the fair through empire, while Limond reads the museum through civic pride. Can both readings hold for a fair whose most lasting building was a civic museum?
  4. The Asian displays appear only in passing, and the texts do not say who organised them. What evidence would a fuller history of Japanese or Indian presence at Glasgow need, and which of these sources could supply it?
  5. Smith reads Glasgow's exhibitions as a contest with Edinburgh. How far does that rivalry explain the scale of the 1901 fair, and how far does it explain the fair's imperial character?
  6. Carbone gives an 18-foot statue of Edward VII under the Industrial Hall dome, and Allwood, Allan and Reid give twice life size. Which sources, such as photographs or the official catalogue, would settle the size, and what would the answer change about how the dome was read?
  7. Limond calls the Glasgow-Edinburgh rivalry largely a media construct, while Smith reads it as a contest Glasgow won. What evidence about the 1901 fair, such as press coverage or committee papers, would show which reading fits?
  8. Filipová says Wolverhampton's 1902 show was in many ways inspired by Glasgow. What would a comparison of the two catalogues show about how one exhibition's model passed to another town?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
7An Architectural Fantasiap. 69Asia

Allwood treats Paris 1900 as the high point of Art Nouveau in exhibitions, and as the point where spectacle began to outrun financial sense. The Grand Palais, the Porte Monumentale, the Rue des Nations and the Palace of Electricity are described, along with the architects behind them and the contrasting Finnish, Swedish and British pavilions. The chapter then turns to new forms of display: Grimoin-Sanson's Cinéorama, the Trottoir Roulant moving walkway and the Olympic Games. It argues that the exhibition lost money because high concession rents were passed on to visitors. Buffalo 1901 is treated through its Electric Tower, its Midway and the assassination of President McKinley. Glasgow 1901 and Turin 1902 are briefly compared for their architecture and their Art Nouveau displays, and the chapter ends with the Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03.

In the contents of this work

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
6The Forefront of English Commercial Centres: Wolverhampton’s Exhibitions of 1869 and 1902Marta Filipováp. 137

Filipová asks what two exhibitions held in Wolverhampton, a town in the Black Country with a poor reputation, were meant to achieve. The South Staffordshire Exhibition of 1869 at Molineux House drew about 220,000 visitors and displayed local goods such as locks, iron, engines and coal, with an art section that mixed fine art and industrial objects. The Wolverhampton Arts and Industrial Exhibition of 1902, inspired by Glasgow's exhibition of 1901, had purpose-built halls, a Canadian pavilion and a separate fine art section in the new Art Gallery, but it lost about 30,000 pounds and drew few visitors from outside the region. Filipová reads both against the region's industrial identity, its rivalry with Birmingham and the decline of local industry by 1902. A smaller show in 1884 is discussed as the event that gave the Art Gallery its collection. She concludes that the events aimed at local education and civic pride, and that their limited reach reflected economic change and the exhibition fatigue of the period.

Substantial discussion 1

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
Glasgow 1901 entry (contributor Cristina Carbone), pp. 164-66; the fair-statistics appendix row is garbled in our extraction and not used

Carbone's entry presents the 1901 Glasgow International Exhibition as the second of the city's fairs, held on the 1888 site and timed to commemorate the 1851 Great Exhibition while opening the Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum. It stresses scale and modernity, calling the fair the largest of its kind in Britain to that date and linking its Spanish-Baroque main buildings to Chicago 1893 and Buffalo 1901. Its interpretive emphasis falls on the art and design side: it says photography was recognized as an art form at a world's fair for the first time, and that the Glasgow Style appeared in the fair's displays. Its closing judgement is that the fair was 'surely demonstrative' of Glasgow's economic flowering at the close of the Victorian era.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Allwood, Allan and Reid give Glasgow's 1901 fair one paragraph within a chapter on architectural fantasy, set beside the Turin exposition of 1902. They argue that Glasgow, which had one of the leading Art Nouveau designers in the city, produced a very mundane main building, and they speculate about what Mackintosh would have made of it. They say the profits of the 1888 fair, with an appeal, paid for the Art Gallery and Museum that opened in time for 1901. Their figures are a profit of 30,571 pounds and twice the attendance of 1851.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. The Glasgow International Exhibition was the second of 4 international exhibitions held in Glasgow, Scotland during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The exhibition took place during a period of half-mourning requested by Edward VII but was still popular and made more than £35000 profit. The exhibition was opened by the King's daughter, the Princess Louise, Duchess of Fife.. It was presided over by a "Citizen Council". Its members included Grace Paterson, Sir William Arrol, Sir Charles Tennant and Sir David Richmond
