Wolverhampton · 1869

South Staffordshire Industrial and Fine Arts Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The South Staffordshire Industrial and Fine Arts Exhibition was a regional exhibition held at Molineux House, Wolverhampton, from 11 May to 29 October 1869 (Filipová 2015p. 142; Filipová 2015p. 143). A committee of local representatives under the Earl of Lichfield organised it (Filipová 2015p. 142). Its official aim was to promote the welfare of a local art school (Filipová 2015p. 146). It drew about 220,000 visitors (Filipová 2015p. 144). Filipová reads it as an attempt to change the image of the Black Country, and its colonial display was small (Filipová 2015p. 137; Filipová 2015p. 142).

Background and aims

By 1850 Wolverhampton already had a poor image, and Filipová links the 1869 exhibition to an effort to correct it and to present the town as a centre of social and political activity (Filipová 2015p. 137; Filipová 2015p. 137). The name Black Country refers to coal mining and ironworks, the industries that gave the region its image (Filipová 2015p. 138). The official financial aim was "promoting the welfare of the Wolverhampton School of Practical Art and of the South Staffordshire Educational Association" (Filipová 2015p. 146). The school had opened on 1 August 1854 and closed temporarily in the early 1860s (Filipová 2015p. 146; Filipová 2015p. 146). The organisers also hoped that local artisans would gain taste and ideas from the displays (Filipová 2015p. 146). In his opening speech, Earl Granville said it was a good idea to show the world what the manufactures of Staffordshire and its immediate neighbourhood were (Filipová 2015p. 147).

Organisers and site

Molineux House, built in 1720, was used as the entrance to the site. It was owned by Oliver McGregor, a local tobacco entrepreneur, and stood on grounds north-east of the city centre (Filipová 2015p. 142; Filipová 2015p. 142). A committee of local representatives from the Town Hall and from business, under the presidency of the Earl of Lichfield, organised the event after long preparations (Filipová 2015p. 142). Nathaniel Neal Solly was the Vice-Chairman (Filipová 2015p. 149). A temporary iron and glass building stood in the garden, and the site held about 130 industrial and over 700 artistic exhibits (Filipová 2015p. 142). The chapter gives no budget, cost or profit for the exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 146).

Exhibits and attractions

The industrial displays were mainly local iron and steel goods, such as nails, bolts, wrought iron frames and chains, and also jewellery, crystal glass and the locks and keys of the Chubb company (Filipová 2015p. 142). Science displays included a nine-metre diagram of the solar system and demonstrations of electricity in communication (Filipová 2015p. 142). The larger objects were a steam engine by the Cornwall works of Smethwick, a model mine from the Wigan Coal and Iron Company and agricultural machinery by Henry Denton of Wolverhampton (Filipová 2015p. 142). The art section was not kept apart from manufactures. It held about 700 oil paintings, watercolours, photographs, engravings, sculptures and art objects, lent by local owners, the South Kensington Museum and the India Collection in London (Filipová 2015p. 148; Filipová 2015p. 149). Its paintings were mostly by local artists, and French art was almost absent (Filipová 2015p. 149).

Empire and colony

The 1869 exhibition had little imperial content. A few colonial objects represented recent British exploits in Abyssinia, including the 1868 armed expedition to free missionaries held in the Ethiopian Empire (Filipová 2015p. 142). The display was a glass case of trophies: Ethiopian ceremonial robes, seals and weapons from the ownership of Queen Victoria and the Lords of the Admiralty, which served as "a reminder of British overseas powers" (Filipová 2015p. 142). Indian silverware, ivory and wooden carvings, silks and swords came from the India Museum in London (Filipová 2015p. 142). Filipová calls the colonial display rather limited, especially beside the Great Exhibition and the 1862 International Exhibition in London, and says that "the provinciality of the event was undeniable" (Filipová 2015p. 142). The chapter names no colony or dominion among the exhibitors, and no colonised people shown or taking part. It presents the organisers' aims as local education and civic image, not empire, trade or settlement (Filipová 2015p. 146; Filipová 2015p. 147).

Visitors and reception

The exhibition was open for five months and attracted visitors from across Staffordshire and further afield. The final attendance was about 220,000, including more than 10,000 children (Filipová 2015p. 143). The Mayor, Moses Ironmonger, linked the event to the Great Exhibition, and shops closed for the procession to the grounds (Filipová 2015p. 143). The opening was attended by Lord Granville, the Earl of Lichfield and John Chubb, among others (Filipová 2015p. 143). The Art Journal praised the organisers and called Wolverhampton a prosperous town (Filipová 2015p. 147). Filipová concludes that the exhibition excited wide local interest but did not permanently change the Black Country's image outside the region (Filipová 2015p. 147).

Its place in the series

Earlier small exhibitions in Wolverhampton were held in 1839 and 1840 and were associated with George Wallis (Filipová 2015p. 141). The 1884 Wolverhampton and Staffordshire Fine Art and Industrial Exhibition was much smaller, and some items shown there later became a permanent collection of the Art Gallery (Filipová 2015p. 149; Filipová 2015p. 151). The larger Wolverhampton Arts and Industrial Exhibition of 1902 is treated as a separate event. Filipová links the 1869 event to the region's industrial heritage and the 1902 event to a time when many industries had moved out or were in decline (Filipová 2015p. 153). The town's municipal institutions grew after 1869, and the first library opened in the year of the exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 147; Filipová 2015p. 147). The encyclopedia lists this exhibition only in its appendix of fairs that fell short of the qualifications for the main body of the book (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423).

India in the 1869 exhibition

Indian objects were present but few. Silverware, ivory and wooden carvings, silks and swords were borrowed from the India Museum in London (Filipová 2015p. 142), and art loans included items from the India Collection in London (Filipová 2015p. 149). The chapter names no visitors, performers or exhibits from China, Japan, Korea, Burma, Siam, Indochina, Ceylon or the Dutch East Indies for 1869 (Filipová 2015p. 142; Filipová 2015p. 149).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Regional identity — Filipová reads the event as an attempt to correct the Black Country's image and to build a regional identity, with an effect that stayed largely local (Filipová 2015p. 137; Filipová 2015p. 147).
  • Colonial display — empire appears as a small case of Abyssinian trophies and borrowed Indian objects, which the author calls limited (Filipová 2015p. 142; Filipová 2015p. 142).
  • Fine art and industry — art was shown beside manufactures, where many other large exhibitions kept them apart (Filipová 2015p. 148).
  • Education and civic pride — the official aim was a local art school, and the organisers hoped artisans would gain taste (Filipová 2015p. 146; Filipová 2015p. 146).
  • Provincial margins — Wolverhampton is described as a peripheral location whose exhibitions drew few outside visitors (Filipová 2015p. 138; Filipová 2015p. 148).

Questions for study

  1. Filipová calls the colonial display small and provincial. What does a handful of borrowed trophies say about the imperial ambitions of a regional exhibition? (Filipová 2015p. 142)
  2. The 1869 event mixed fine art with manufactures. What did that mixing do for a town that wanted a cultured image? (Filipová 2015p. 148)
  3. The official aim was a local art school, yet the organisers also wanted to show the world Staffordshire's manufactures. How far do these aims conflict? (Filipová 2015p. 146; Filipová 2015p. 147)
  4. Why did the larger 1902 exhibition also fail to reach outside audiences? (Filipová 2015p. 148; Filipová 2015p. 153)
  5. The encyclopedia excludes this exhibition from its main body. What makes a regional exhibition count as a world's fair? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422)

Introduction: The Margins of Exhibitions and Exhibitions StudiesMarta Filipováp. 1Asia

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

In the contents of this work

6The Forefront of English Commercial Centres: Wolverhampton’s Exhibitions of 1869 and 1902Marta Filipováp. 137

Filipová asks what two exhibitions held in Wolverhampton, a town in the Black Country with a poor reputation, were meant to achieve. The South Staffordshire Exhibition of 1869 at Molineux House drew about 220,000 visitors and displayed local goods such as locks, iron, engines and coal, with an art section that mixed fine art and industrial objects. The Wolverhampton Arts and Industrial Exhibition of 1902, inspired by Glasgow's exhibition of 1901, had purpose-built halls, a Canadian pavilion and a separate fine art section in the new Art Gallery, but it lost about 30,000 pounds and drew few visitors from outside the region. Filipová reads both against the region's industrial identity, its rivalry with Birmingham and the decline of local industry by 1902. A smaller show in 1884 is discussed as the event that gave the Art Gallery its collection. She concludes that the events aimed at local education and civic pride, and that their limited reach reflected economic change and the exhibition fatigue of the period.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.
