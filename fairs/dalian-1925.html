Dalian Industrial Exposition

Dairen kangyō hakurankai
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther reading

The Dalian Industrial Exposition (Dairen kangyō hakurankai) was held in 1925 in what the study's English bibliographic title calls modern northeast China, or former Manchuria (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545p. 563). The texts say nothing about its organisers, scale or visitors. Kari Shepherdson-Scott cites it in a footnote as an earlier case of presenting Manchuria as a site of Japanese economic cooperation, and so as precedent for the Manchuria pavilion at Chicago in 1933 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

A precedent in a footnote

Shepherdson-Scott treats the Dalian exposition in a footnote to her account of the Manchuria exhibit at Chicago (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545). The main text says that Japanese planners made Mantetsu "the screen through which the international spectators would view Manchuria" (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545). The footnote adds that "There was precedent, too, in emphasizing the fruitful economic relationship Japan had cultivated in Manchuria", and gives the 1925 Dalian exposition as the example (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545).

What the texts record

The footnote's sentence continues after a page break. There the exposition "highlighted how Manchuria functioned as an economic facilitator of Sino-Japanese friendship and trade", and the note cites Takemura's 2008 article (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The body text names the exposition in Japanese as Dairen kangyō hakurankai (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545). The bibliography gives Takemura's title in English as a study of the Dalian Industrial Exhibition held in modern northeast China, or former Manchuria, in 1925 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 563).

What the texts do not say

The texts give the year but no further date (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545p. 563). They give no organisers, scale, exhibits or visitor figures for the exposition (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545p. 546). Dalian appears elsewhere in the Chicago chapter, where two paintings show the city of Dalian "of Yesterday" and "of Today" (Shepherdson-Scottp. 553). Those paintings concern the city, and the passage does not connect them to the 1925 exposition (Shepherdson-Scottp. 553).

Its place in the series

The Chicago fair, A Century of Progress, opened on 27 May 1933 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539). Shepherdson-Scott places the Dalian exposition earlier than that fair, as a precedent for stressing Japan's economic ties with Manchuria (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Precedent for the Chicago framing — the footnote presents the 1925 exposition as an earlier instance of the economic emphasis that the Chicago planners adopted through Mantetsu (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545).
  • Sino-Japanese friendship and trade — the footnote describes the exposition as showing Manchuria as "an economic facilitator" of Sino-Japanese relations, citing Takemura (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546).
  • Limits of the evidence — one footnote and one bibliography entry are the only treatment; the texts give no detail on the exposition itself (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545p. 563).

Questions for study

  1. What would a student need to find in Takemura (2008) to establish the exposition's dates, organisers, exhibits and visitors, which Shepherdson-Scott does not give?
  2. Does the phrase "an economic facilitator of Sino-Japanese friendship and trade" describe the 1925 exposition itself, or Shepherdson-Scott's reading of it?
  3. Why did a footnote precedent matter to Japanese planners who were trying to shift attention away from Manchukuo's politics at Chicago (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545)?

The footnote gives no dates, scale, organisers or visitor figures, and the bibliography cites Takemura Tamio's 2008 study of the exposition.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Shepherdson-Scott examines the Manchuria pavilion that the South Manchuria Railway Company and the Manchukuo government sponsored at Chicago's A Century of Progress fair in 1933 and 1934, months after Manchukuo was founded and Japan left the League of Nations. The argument is that this small building, which drew about two percent of fairgoers, was a diplomatic instrument aimed at American opinion, and that Mantetsu let the exhibit present Manchuria as an economic project rather than a political one. The paper also treats the American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol, whose site and authenticity contested the pavilion's maps, which placed the recently occupied province of Jehol inside Manchukuo. It concludes that the fair never sold a single idea of Manchuria.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Manchuria is the subject of the whole article, so East Asia is central, but the coverage is confined to a few states. Japan is the organiser: the government, the Kwantung Army, Mantetsu and the exhibition committees shape the pavilion, which chapter 2 reads against Japan's own Kyoto Manchuria pavilion of 1915 and the Qing-inspired architecture it dropped. Manchukuo is treated as a new state whose sovereignty the exhibit had to perform. China enters as the party whose planned display prompted Japan to participate, and chapter 3 is concentrated on the Golden Temple of Jehol, a replica built in Beijing by Chinese craftsmen and sited beside the China and Japan exhibits. Mongolia appears through Jehol and the Sino-Mongolian culture Bendix claimed to conserve. Korea appears only as a Japanese colony shown on Mantetsu's map, its border with Manchukuo blurred. Southeast Asia is absent. None of its countries, colonies or peoples is named in the text, and it discusses no exhibition held in Southeast Asia. Its Asian comparisons are confined to Japan, China, Korea and Manchuria, and the wider 'Asia' language is used for the Japanese imperial project in Northeast Asia.
