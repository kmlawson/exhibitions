Melbourne · 1880–1881

Melbourne International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Melbourne International Exhibition ran from 1 October 1880 to 30 April 1881 in the Exhibition Hall in Carlton Gardens, Melbourne (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). The texts call it Melbourne's first international exhibition, a display of Victorian manufactures and colonial progress that drew between about 1.3 and 1.5 million visitors, depending on the source (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Badger 1979p. 131). Historians use it to study how a colony displayed itself to the world, promoted trade and built a national identity before federation in 1901 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Powell opens with the claim that "Melbourne welcomed the world as its first international exhibition opened on October 1, 1880" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Victorian manufacturers had already shown their goods at local and intercolonial exhibitions in 1854, 1861, 1866, 1872 and 1875 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). The proposal for an international fair was politically contested. Debate in the Victorian legislature from 1877 to 1880 coincided with conflicts between small property owners and manufacturers on one side and mercantile and landed interests on the other (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Premier Graham Berry hoped the fair would revive a sagging economy, and his free-trade opponents accused him of promoting selected Victorian interests (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Dunstan says that after Berry's 1877 election his manufacturer supporters sought a permanent hall, and that approving legislation passed only after a successful showing at Paris (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67). The commercial aim was explicit: leaders hoped to encourage world trade by displaying products against thirty-seven nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74).

Organisation and finance

William Clarke, whom Dunstan calls Victoria's richest man, headed a five-man executive committee stacked with Berry's supporters; its forty commissioners had industrial or professional associations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67).

The texts give different money figures. Dunstan gives the cost as £277,292, less than half the sum feared, and says most temporary structures were then dismantled and sold (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Powell gives the same sum as the deficit, which he says was largely caused by the building; detractors called the building a dead loss (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Badger's table gives receipts of £331,901 and a cost of £330,330 (Badger 1979p. 131). These figures do not match each other, and the texts do not reconcile them.

Site and architecture

Joseph Reed designed the Exhibition Hall in an ecclesiastical style. Its dome rose 217 feet, the second highest in the world (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Dunstan describes a cruciform plan with naves, transepts and round arches, and says its forms recalled early Norman churches, the Italian Renaissance and Paxton's Crystal Palace (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67). The texts disagree on size: Powell gives a 21-acre site (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74), Badger's table gives 20 acres (Badger 1979p. 131), and Allwood gives about 22 acres of floor space for the building and its annexes (Allwood et al. 2001p. 52). Reed's garden plan, elaborated by William Sangster, had a central promenade, lawns and flower beds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 68).

The hall had several later uses. It served as an exhibition hall, printing office, seat of the state parliament and aquarium (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74), and the 1888 Centennial Exhibition was again centred on it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 105). Allwood notes that it still stands and hosted the opening of the first Federal Parliament (Allwood et al. 2001p. 52). UNESCO's world heritage listing in 2004 rested on its survival largely intact (Filipová 2015p. 256).

Exhibits and courts

The hall held more than 13,000 exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69), although Badger's table gives 12,792 (Badger 1979p. 131). Great Britain obtained 559 awards, Victoria 373, France 338, Germany 259, Italy 138 and the United States 115 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Dunstan says nearly every exhibit in the Victorian court was set in a colossal temple or trophy of local handiwork, meant to show the colony's rapid development (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). New South Wales commissioners organised a special court of wines, tin, gold, copper, coal, diamonds and telegraph apparatus (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2). Dunstan records that the German Emperor's special prize for the most meritorious Australasian exhibit went to the wine industry (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). About 800 works from the British collection hung in the galleries, and Lefebvre's Chloe stayed in Melbourne as a national symbol (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69).

Trade and manufacturers

Powell says the fair was primarily a display of manufactured goods, meant to advertise Australian products and open trade, particularly with France; its effect on trade stayed a small fraction of Australia's total (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Dunstan agrees that the trade and manufacturing effects were slight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Allwood reports a larger effect: direct trade with Germany, America, France and Belgium, steamship services to Marseilles and Bremen, and French bank branches in Melbourne and Sydney (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). Victorian manufacturers, earning £13m a year, found they could hold their own against foreign goods (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). The Melbourne Chamber of Commerce put proposals for direct trade to the Italian commissioner and direct steam communication to the German one; no direct trading scheme resulted at the time (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). A Budapest milling firm, Ganz and Co, sold its roller-milling technology after showing it and then opened a Melbourne branch; the texts credit the show with transforming Victoria's grain milling (Hoffenberg 2001p. 188).

Visitors and reception

Dunstan gives 1,458,896 visitors over seven months, with the closing ball on 1 June 1881 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69); Powell gives the same total (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74). Allwood says visitors were counted on a newly patented registering machine and that attendance, as a share of population, was nearly four times Paris 1878's (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). The opening was a public holiday in dull, windy and chilly weather, with crowds lining the streets (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 68). Trades union members with banners lined the route for the governors, and tradesmen had to buy tickets for the opening ceremony (Hoffenberg 2001p. 264). Visitors' books for the Melbourne volume often compared the fair favourably with Paris (Hoffenberg 2001p. 251). Powell notes that the trustees themselves admitted the paintings were third and fourth rate, and the music banal (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74).

Who ran and opened the fair

George Collins Levey, the Secretary of the Exhibition Commission, spent a year travelling through North America and Europe to persuade nations and individuals to take part (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). At the opening on 1 October 1880 the Governor of Victoria, the Marquis of Normanby, called it "a source of legitimate pride to the people of Victoria" that a colony whose territory had been unknown less than half a century earlier had carried the project through (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53).

Art sales and gifts

Sydney's art exhibits reached Melbourne after a showing at the Queensland Exhibition in Brisbane in July 1880 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). Allwood says that some £14,000 worth of these pictures were sold at the exhibitions, many of them to public art galleries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). Gifts moved alongside the sales. The twenty-nine James Barry etchings sent by the London Society of Arts went to the Melbourne National Gallery, and other pictures went to galleries in other colonies (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54).

Foreign courts and the press

Dunstan gives 1,250 French exhibitors at Melbourne, against 300 at Sydney (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). The German court held five spaces apart from fine art, with pianos, furniture, toys, porcelain, lager beer, wines, armaments, cigars and perfumes, which the text links, perhaps, to imperial interest in the region (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). The United States drew attention with new gadgets, the typewriter among them, despite the loss of the ship Eric the Red (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Le Figaro's correspondent, identified as the musician Henri Kowalski, mocked British puritanism that banished certain (nude) paintings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Powell calls the Argus "Exhibition Supplement" of 2 and 6 October 1880 particularly useful for courts and statistics (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75), and calls the Official Record of 1882 the most thorough printed source, displaying "the Victorians’ passion for statistics" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75).

Popular verse and music

Powell lists a great deal of verse linked to the exhibition's cultural pretensions and marketing, including Edward Glick's extravaganza "The Fairies at the Exhibition", printed in the Great International Advertiser on 18 October 1880 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75). Leon Caron's Victoria Cantata (1880) was first performed at the exhibition and hailed Victoria as the Queen of the South (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75). Dunstan adds that visitors' vernacular verse and observations survive in diaries and scrapbooks held by the State Library of Victoria (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70).

Visitors and their book

Dunstan describes the Visitors Register, 44.5 cm wide and 17 cm deep, which records the names and thoughts of 16,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70). Visitors compared the show with other fairs as they would compare museums, restaurants and hotels, and the colonial show faced stiff competition with the Great Exhibition of 1851 and against one Bombay visitor's own personal "bazaar and harem" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 251). Allwood puts attendance, as a share of population, at about six times that of the preceding shows (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54).

The Fijian court

Fiji's "Resources and Progress" display at Sydney and Melbourne in 1879 and 1880 was mostly European goods: timber samples, cotton seed, cigars, bricks and nut oil (Thomas 1991p. 176). Its Fijian part showed cloth, mats, bowls, pottery and fishing nets (Thomas 1991p. 176). Thomas notes that there were very few weapons at Sydney and none at Melbourne (Thomas 1991p. 177). The contrast with W. C. Gardenhire's "Fiji Cannibal Exhibition", shown on the American West Coast, is sharp, and Thomas notes that only eight years separated the two shows (Thomas 1991p. 165; Thomas 1991p. 177). The cannibal theme survived in staged photographs of Fijian warfare, while the material displays stressed craft and commercial development (Thomas 1991p. 177).

How historians have interpreted it

Hoffenberg places Melbourne in an exchange system. Commissioners from New South Wales, Queensland and Victoria used their colonies' natural history and ethnography to secure exchanges, and Melbourne's museum superintendent offered Victorian gold and native plants for German court exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48). Hoffenberg argues that such exchanges gave colonial and imperial experts public authority (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48; Hoffenberg 2001p. 48). Dunstan reads the fair as a vehicle of colonial display, self-congratulation and exchange that helped lead to federation in 1901 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70). Powell argues its real significance was symbolic: it "was most significant as a patriotic and symbolic identification of Australia with prevailing economic and cultural movements of the European world" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75). Allwood sees the colonies taking a more independent position in trade (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). Roche, quoting MacKenzie, reads colonial expositions as celebrating European-style societies transplanted overseas (Roche 2000p. 58). Filipová's volume describes colonial fairs as organised to promote settlement, investment and development and as displays of British dominance (Filipová 2015p. 14). Bennett places the fair among nineteenth-century ideas of teaching by sight (Bennett 2004p. 160).

The texts agree that the fair was a colonial display of Victorian identity (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75; Filipová 2015p. 14). They differ on its effects. Dunstan calls the trade effect slight but the fair significant, whereas Allwood reports new direct trade (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). Powell judges its cultural impact slight, while Dunstan stresses its political and national consequences (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70). Hoffenberg's imperial reading differs from the Victorian-centred accounts of Dunstan and Powell.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan's presence was modest and mainly commercial. Hotta-Lister gives Japan's budget as about 33,000 yen for Melbourne (October 1880 to April 1881), against about 40,000 yen for Sydney in 1879 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212); the appendix records 33,014 yen for the Melbourne exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). Japanese educational articles and books won the first prize, while Sydney had given prizes to rice, green tea and red tea (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212). Alexander Marks, Japan's honorary consul in Australia and an advocate of trade between the two countries, gave notable help (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212). A Japanese naval cruiser, named in the text as the Rytijo, was sent to Melbourne in 1880, "receiving considerable attention from the Australians" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 77). It carried about two hundred sailors and anchored at Melbourne near the close of the exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). Hotta-Lister counts this among earlier, smaller Japanese naval displays before London 1910 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 77). Afterwards, the two government officials in charge of the exhibition resigned their posts, returned to Melbourne and opened Akita Shokai, the first Japanese trading company in Australia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). Hotta-Lister presents the fair as the point at which Japan began to see the market for its goods in Australia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213). The passages give no Japanese view of the fair itself.

India and Ceylon

Hoffenberg argues that Melbourne showed the Indian government had to take a direct financial and organisational role, because private trade would not do so alone: "Private trade, if left to itself, would do nothing" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 87). The government spent Rs. 37,000 on general exhibits and offered the Indian Tea Syndicate a further Rs. 10,000 for its displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 87). The appendix lists Edward Buck as the Indian government's commissioner at Melbourne (Hoffenberg 2001p. 282). Hoffenberg says the Indian Court was quite new to the colony, in the view of a local guidebook editor (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159). Dunstan notes that people dressed in Indian fashion served exotic teas and told stories at the Indian court (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69). Visitors bought seemingly authentic items from the East as souvenirs, goods that had not been available before (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159).

The Madras superintendent of the Government Central Museum displayed Indian teas at Melbourne, which were praised for greater strength and stronger aroma than Chinese teas (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). Exhibition tea kiosks, pamphlets and sales of samples helped stimulate Indian and Ceylonese tea shipments to Australia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). Allwood lists Ceylon and the Straits Settlements among the colonies that lent aid, and says India made a brilliant display (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). Hoffenberg reads these displays as part of the imperial exchange in which experts gained public standing in Calcutta and Melbourne (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48). The texts name no Chinese, Korean or Dutch East Indies display at Melbourne.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial display and Victorian identity — The texts read the fair as Victoria's showcase of progress and self-image before federation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75).
  • Victorian politics and the hall — The fair was tied to party conflict and to Berry's wish to revive a sagging economy (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 67).
  • Trade and manufacturers — Reading differs: Dunstan calls the trade effect slight, while Allwood reports direct trade links (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Allwood et al. 2001p. 54).
  • Imperial exchange and government finance — Hoffenberg shows colonial commissioners trading goods and the Indian state funding its displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48; Hoffenberg 2001p. 87).
  • Asian courts and markets — Japan's budget and cruiser, and Indian tea and the Indian Court, show trade as their main aim (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212; Hoffenberg 2001p. 118).
  • Building and afterlife — The hall was reused for other purposes and later listed as world heritage (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74; Filipová 2015p. 256).
  • Visitors and popular reception — Attendance figures differ, and visitors' books and the opening crowds show popular engagement (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Badger 1979p. 131; Hoffenberg 2001p. 264).

Questions for study

  1. Why do the texts give visitor totals of 1,458,896 and 1,330,279, and which would you trust for a study of attendance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Badger 1979p. 131)?
  2. Does the fair show a colony building its identity, or the workings of imperial exchange? Compare Dunstan's reading with Hoffenberg's (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70; Hoffenberg 2001p. 48).
  3. Why did the Indian government have to finance its own displays at Melbourne, and what does that suggest about who paid for colonial fairs (Hoffenberg 2001p. 87)?
  4. Did the Japanese cruiser and the export plans at Melbourne mark a new relationship with Australia, or only a passing display (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 77; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 213)?
  5. How should a historian weigh the Victorian court's self-presentation against later judgements of the fair's cultural impact (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 74)?
  6. Why did the Fijian displays at Sydney and Melbourne leave out weapons, and whose interests did the change of emphasis serve (Thomas 1991p. 177)?
  7. Dunstan's figures give 1,250 French exhibitors, and the Visitors Register holds the names and thoughts of 16,000 visitors. Which kind of evidence would show whether the fair opened new markets, as against recording visitors' impressions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70; Allwood et al. 2001p. 54)?
  8. Did the printed verse and cantatas speak for popular opinion, or for the promoters who commissioned them (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 75; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 70)?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
5Paris, Australia and all the Worldp. 46Asia

Allwood follows the exhibition circuit from Paris to the Antipodes in the 1870s and 1880s. The chapter opens with Paris 1878, set against the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian war and the Commune, and describes the Krantz building, the Rue des Nations, the ventilation system devised by the architect Hardy, and landmarks such as Doré's vase. Sala's complaints about exhaustion, dear restaurants and the dominance of large firms serve as evidence that exhibitions were turning from individual craftsmanship towards commerce. Most of the chapter then covers the Sydney exhibition of 1879 and the Melbourne exhibition of 1880, using visitor numbers, the Garden Palace and art sales to show their effects. The central figure is Jules Joubert, whose shows in Adelaide, Perth, Christchurch, Calcutta and Tasmania are traced in detail. The chapter ends with London's Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, which led to the founding of the Imperial Institute.

In the contents of this work

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
2The Exhibition Wallahs: Commissioners and the Production of Imperial Knowledgep. 31Asia

Hoffenberg's chapter examines the commissioners who organized Britain's and its colonies' exhibitions in the Victorian and Edwardian eras, and how their work turned exhibitions into networks of imperial and colonial knowledge. The argument is that these cultural bureaucrats, who were often scientists, art experts, botanists and museum officials, created and circulated the categories by which the empire and its colonies were known, and that their exchanges of objects and information strengthened ties between metropole and periphery. Hoffenberg works through South Kensington first: Henry Cole, Cunliffe-Owen and Purdon Clarke, whose handbooks, purchases and loans linked London to Australian and Indian museums. The bulk of the chapter then compares Australian commissioners such as Redmond Barry, William Macarthur, Edward Combes and Ferdinand von Mueller with Anglo-Indian and South Asian ones, above all Buck, Watt, Lockwood Kipling and Trilokya Nath Mukharji, whose catalogues and overseas travels form the chapter's fullest case study. The conclusion argues that this participation was not simply hegemonic: colonial experts became nationalist intellectuals, so that imperial knowledge could also undermine imperial rule.

In the contents of this work

8The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress: Ceremonies, Tourism, and Epic Theater at the Exhibitionsp. 242Asia

Hoffenberg argues that visitors to the British and colonial exhibitions were active participants rather than passive spectators, and that their ceremonies, tourism and pageants helped create the Victorian public and an imperial civil society. The chapter begins with Lord Curzon's 1903 Delhi Durbar Art Exhibition and draws on Crystal Palace ceremonies, Australian and Indian shows, and the memoirs of colonial visitors such as Alexander Dobbie and Edward Ogilvie, for whom exhibitions were stops on an imperial pilgrimage. Opening ceremonies in London, Dublin, Calcutta, Sydney and Melbourne placed the Crown, viceroys, Indian princes and trade unionists in a visible hierarchy, though Anglo-Indian boycotts in Calcutta during the Ilbert Bill dispute exposed its limits. Pageants, above all the 1911 Festival of Empire at Sydenham, and closing ceremonies and souvenirs then fixed the shows in public memory. The exhibitions, Hoffenberg concludes, were a contested process that constituted the public rather than merely reflecting it, and their rituals drew colonial subjects into an imperial order that also nurtured colonial nationalism.

In the contents of this work

Thomas, Nicholas. Entangled Objects: Exchange, Material Culture, and Colonialism in the Pacific. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1991.
4The European Appropriation of Indigenous Thingsp. 125

Thomas turns from indigenous appropriation to European collecting, from Cook's voyages to the early twentieth century. Sailors and naturalists bought curiosities, and observers called objects curious without judging them, a vagueness Thomas links to the absence of any developed anthropological language of material culture. Later collectors gave objects narrative weight: Keate's Pelew bowl stood for a king's generosity, missionaries displayed burned idols as proof of conversion, and settlers in Fiji treated clubs and cannibal forks as evidence of murder. Anatole Von Hugel's systematic collecting and Governor Gordon's display of clubs and bowls at Government House in Levuka ordered Fijian culture into a totality for the colonial state. Fijian exhibits at Sydney 1879 and Melbourne 1880 showed craft with few weapons. The chapter ends with Frank Hurley's Papuan collecting, which it reads as a denial of the people who made the objects, and concludes that both sides reworked the meanings of things.

Allwood, Allan and Reid's entry places the 1880-81 Melbourne fair among the post-1870s colonial exhibitions, describing its opening, its foreign participation, its visitor numbers and its commercial effects. Their measure of success is popular attendance, which they set against Paris 1878 and earlier colonial shows.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

Dunstan's encyclopedia entry describes the Melbourne International Exhibition of 1880-81 in the Royal Exhibition Building: its origins in Victorian party politics, its opening, its awards and its finances. For Dunstan it ushered in the boom of the 1880s and was a vehicle of colonial display, self-congratulation and exchange that led, ultimately, to federation and nationhood in 1901.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.
