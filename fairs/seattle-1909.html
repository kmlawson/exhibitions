Seattle · 1909

Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition (AYP) was Seattle's first world's fair. It was set to open on 1 June 1909 on the campus of the University of Washington and to close on 16 October (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). Its promoters presented Seattle as the gateway to the Pacific and to Alaska (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). Historians have used it to study how American fairs tied commerce to Asia and displayed non-white peoples as stages of racial progress (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Planning began in Seattle's Alaska Club, an offshoot of the Chamber of Commerce, while the Lewis and Clark fair in Portland was still under way (Rydell 1984). The first idea was a fair on Alaska's resources, but Henry E. Reed, secretary of the Portland exposition corporation, urged the club to include Pacific markets (Rydell 1984). William Howard Taft described the aim as exploiting Alaska's resources and developing trade on the Pacific slope (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 202). The official seal showed three women representing Japan, Alaska and the Pacific Northwest, holding a steamship, a gold nugget and a locomotive (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). At the opening-day address, the railway builder James J. Hill told visitors they would take home ideas about new markets and new trading peoples (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206).

The sources disagree on when planning began. Fair America says that "Shortly after the close of the Lewis and Clark Exposition, planning began" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Historical Dictionary dates the association and executive committee to the spring of 1906 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). Rydell has the Alaska Club planning while the Portland fair was still open (Rydell 1984). The dictionary also calls Ira A. Nadeau a former general agent of the Northern Pacific Railroad (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206), against the Great Northern agency given above (Rydell 1984).

Organisation and finance

Stock subscriptions paid for the fair. Reed's statement to a House committee in 1908 showed that three subscribers held $75,000 of stock, while 2,595 subscribers held $56,400 (Rydell 1984). Railways, including the Great Northern, banks and newspapers gave financial support (Rydell 1984). The director-general, Ira A. Nadeau, had been a Great Northern agent (Rydell 1984). The budget was closely monitored, so the fair lacked the large commemorative fountains of earlier fairs and had few extensive plantings (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The sources disagree on attendance. One reference work gives total paid attendance as 3,740,551 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207). Another gives the total as 3,750,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 205). Fair America gives 2.8 million paid visits and 3.7 million in all (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Fair America puts the modest profit at $63,676 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and a statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives the same profit figure (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Labour and the open shop

Labour opposed the fair. The Seattle Socialist claimed that the directors had placed notices in eastern papers during the 1907 panic, bringing in workers and allowing an open shop, meaning a workplace where unions are not recognised (Rydell 1984). Fair America says labour leaders, mainly socialists, feared the effect on organised labour (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The state's unions placed the fair on their unfair list (Rydell 1984). The directors made Labor Day 1909 Seattle Day at the fair and offered free passes, but between twelve and fourteen thousand people gathered in a union protest at Woodland Park (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

In Portland, unions had subscribed to the Lewis and Clark stock on promises of union conditions, but the management adopted an open shop in April 1903 (Rydell 1984). Carpenters and painters answered with petitions for a referendum on the appropriation (Rydell 1984). The socialist-controlled Trades Council backed them (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Henry Kundert, editor of the Labor Press, wrote of the organisers: "It is needless to say that these men are Socialists." (Rydell 1984) The drive failed when trade unionists who owned property or worked in trades that gained from fairgoers opposed it (Rydell 1984).

In Seattle in 1909, several plasterers, painters and carpenters walked around the fair's triumphal arch rather than under it (Rydell 1984). The Union Record also cast the Igorots as the image of shame for the directors (Rydell 1984).

Site, buildings and the Pay Streak

The 250-acre site beside the University of Washington was laid out by the Olmsted Brothers of Boston (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). John Langley Howard designed the buildings, most of them in a beaux-arts derivation that recalls seventeenth-century French court architecture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). Olmsted's plan to clear the native firs and build in a Russian-inspired style was rejected (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). The Forestry Building used timbers from Snohomish County, but it had few practical features and started no school of Northwest architecture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206).

The amusement strip, the Pay Streak, mixed totem poles with Japanese lanterns (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). It had a ferris wheel (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207) and the Upside Down House, a building with angled mirrors that confused visitors' sense of space (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Sources disagree on the style. Fair America calls the buildings standard neoclassical (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Encyclopedia says the dominant style was French Renaissance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 202), and the Historical Dictionary's beaux-arts description stands against both (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). The main axis of the Olmsted plan opened towards Mount Rainier (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206). Maloney notes that the site keeps elements of the Olmsted landscape (Maloney 2012p. 214). Benedict reproduces the Upside Down House as a souvenir postcard (Benedict 1983p. 57).

Peoples on display

The Igorot village, run by the private showmen Felder and Schneidewind, stood at the head of the South Pay Streak (Rydell 1984). The Encyclopedia says the Igorots were presented as "the dog-eating, head-hunting, primitive and wild people of the Philippines" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). More than sixteen thousand schoolchildren visited while the village was being built (Rydell 1984). Alfred C. Haddon, a Cambridge anthropologist, taught a summer course that used the fair's peoples as a living laboratory. Haddon also defended the village against charges of indecency made by visiting Filipino sailors (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

The Eskimo Village, the largest concession on the Pay Streak, housed Siberian and Alaskan Eskimos (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). Fair America says that "the message that was communicated suggested that racial characteristics were a primary indicator of progress" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Columbia Eneutseak, born at Chicago's 1893 Eskimo village, led a protest during Taft's visit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204).

Arthur W. Lewis, director of concessions, gave the Eskimo village contract to the Arctic trader A. M. Baber, who gathered thirty-four Siberians (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Before the fair Baber housed them in a Seattle cold storage plant, and by opening day the village also held Alaskans whom the directors called partly educated (Rydell 1984). A separate Land of the Midnight Sun concession showed Labrador Inuit in a more civilised light (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203).

Visitors and reception

The opening day included Japanese fireworks and two admirals of the Japanese fleet as dignitaries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). Japan Day, 4 September, drew large crowds, but the texts give different numbers. Rydell and Hotta-Lister say nearly seven thousand Japanese residents turned out (Rydell 1984; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). The Encyclopedia says more than 7,000 citizens of Japanese ancestry attended (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). An endnote says two thousand Japanese paraded through downtown that day (Rydell 1984). Taft, who spent most of two days at the fair on 30 September, toured the Pay Streak (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 205). On Smith Day, 2 September, nearly 5,000 people with the surname Smith came to the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204).

After the fair most buildings went to the University of Washington (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 205). Fair America says the fair's legacy helped lead Seattle to hold its 1962 fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Concessionaires, contracts and wages

The Igorot village was assembled by men who had worked on the Philippine displays at St Louis. In 1904 Samuel M. McGowan, Truman K. Hunt and Edmund A. Felder formed the International Anthropological Exhibit Company (Rydell 1984). By early 1905 it had split. Hunt organized the Igorot Exhibit Company, and Felder and Richard Schneidewind formed the Filipino Exhibition Company (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Schneidewind had been fired in 1901 as a postal clerk in Manila over alleged complicity in a smuggling scheme (Rydell 1984). When Taft had second thoughts about further Filipino concessions, Goode cabled the secretary of war, and Taft relented (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

At Chutes Park in Los Angeles in 1906, a military recruiting officer found the Filipinos "unanimous in their desire to return" home (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). To prevent a mutiny the concessionaires raised wages, which they held in trust (Rydell 1984). By the following year Hunt was in a Memphis jail for embezzling the Igorots' wages (Rydell 1984).

Water sports and naval visitors

As with any coastal fair, the aquatic aspects played a central role (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204). Naval vessels from China, France, Great Britain, Germany, Japan and Russia lay in Puget Sound (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204). The programme included yacht races for international cups, a sailing race to Juneau and back, and Eskimo kayak races (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204). Taft opened the fair from the White House by touching a telegraph key set with Klondike gold (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 202).

Afterlife of the site and exhibits

At the close, many exhibits were crated and sent to Chicago for the United States Land and Irrigation Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 205). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the fair provided the basis of buildings and landscaping for the university in whose grounds it was held, and that it was teetotal because state law forbade liquor sales on the campus (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89). They call that university Washington University, where other texts say University of Washington (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), so the name is probably a slip. The Century 21 fair of 1962 was first meant to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the 1909 fair, and used a site of only 74 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 129; Allwood et al. 2001p. 129). The site is still used as the Seattle Centre (Allwood et al. 2001p. 129).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell's 1984 chapter reads the Portland and Seattle fairs as imperial showpieces. Their designers linked industrial growth to Pacific markets, and they promoted a vision of racial progress (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). In the 1993 book, Rydell argues that the Philippine displays presented the people as resources to be exploited, which underwrote a promise of an imperial future of abundance (Rydell 1993p. 21; Rydell 1993p. 21). Rydell, Findling and Pelle place Seattle in a national synthesis of two parts: an economic case for overseas expansion, and a racial case that progress toward civilisation could be read from innate racial characteristics (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They also record anti-imperialist and African American opposition to the fairs' displays (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The reference works by Wilson (1990) and Bowers (2008) describe the fair mainly as a promotional and commercial event, centred on Pacific trade and Alaska. Neither argues that race was its organising idea, though Bowers says the Chinese village was less sensational than the Igorot show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). Hotta-Lister (1999) looks at Japanese reactions. The author argues that Japanese visitors made no complaint about the village displays at Seattle, and puts the difference from London down to social class (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192). Benedict (1983) names the fair only in captions, and Maloney (2012) only in an endnote on its landscape (Benedict 1983p. 57; Maloney 2012p. 214).

The scholars agree on the Pacific trade framing (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 202; Rydell 1984). They differ on whether the racial displays were the fair's main purpose or one part of a commercial event. Rydell, in 1984 and in the 2000 book with Findling and Pelle, treats race and empire as central (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Wilson and Bowers treat commerce and boosterism as central (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 202).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan was the only Asian government to exhibit at the fair (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 215; Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207). Japan's was one of only two national buildings constructed on the site, the other being Canada's (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). An appendix of international exhibitions in Hotta-Lister gives an expenditure of 100,000 yen for Seattle (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222).

A delegation of business and civic leaders, headed by Shibusawa Eiichi, visited the fair and toured the United States as guests of the federal government and the Associated Chambers of Commerce (Rydell 1984; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). Rydell says the promoters recast Japan's role as a vision of commercial harmony, which the Seattle press summed up in a slogan (Rydell 1984). Shibusawa told Seattle businessmen that restricting emigration was in Japan's interest (Rydell 1984).

Rydell records hostility alongside the welcome. The Asiatic Exclusion League had held its first international convention in Seattle a year before the fair (Rydell 1984). McGraw, a vice-president of the exposition, said that Japanese could not be approved as citizens of the republic (Rydell 1984). Rydell summarises the attitude: Japanese were welcomed as customers, but they were still regarded as "little brown men" (Rydell 1984).

Hotta-Lister notes that the Japanese living in the United States were mostly unskilled labourers, and that Japanese visitors made no complaint about village displays at Seattle (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192). Japanese naval forces stayed on hand through the fair, and warships from Japan, China, Russia and other nations anchored in Puget Sound (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 204).

The Philippines and Filipino peoples

The Philippines was one of the US colonial possessions displayed at the fair (Rydell 1984). The federal exhibit grouped the islands under a heading on natural resources and primitive peoples (Rydell 1993p. 21). Rydell says ethnological villages were standard at American fairs after 1893, so Seattle was not unusual (Rydell 1993p. 21). The Smithsonian's display ended with representations of Filipinos, shown as Negrito, Igorot and Moro types (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). The War Department's Philippines Building drew nearly five thousand visitors a day, and its exhibits of hemp, cigars and beef suggested the islands' prosperity was limited only by their productive capacity (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Fair America says that the building taught visitors that American occupation was needed to uplift Filipinos (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The Igorot village was a private concession. One reference work says the village was inhabited by Filipinos hired by a touring exhibition company (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207). Dosch, who later directed exhibits at Seattle (Rydell 1984), is reported to have said that Asian exhibitors at the St Louis fair of 1904 had been treated so badly that they were "thoroughly disgusted with all Expositions" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 215). Hotta-Lister says this is why other Asian governments stayed away from Seattle (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 215).

China and other Asian presence

Japanese and Chinese villages stood on the Pay Streak (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 207; Rydell 1984). The texts do not say who ran the Chinese village. Bowers says it was less sensational than the Igorot show, but still showed opium dens and violence that recalled the Boxer Rebellion in China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). Rydell says displays of markets in Japan and China made an American commercial empire in Asia seem inevitable (Rydell 1984).

Japanese reception

The Japanese delegation, headed by Shibuzawa Eiichi, landed at the Great Northern docks from James J. Hill's steamship Minnesota and spent five days touring industry and commerce (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). A Seattle Commercial Club resolution urged citizens to show good manners to their transpacific neighbours and to reject race prejudice (Rydell 1984). On the midway, a rickshaw concession that used a Japanese flag was seen as adding to anti-Japanese and cheap-labour sentiment before Japan Day (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 203). Hotta-Lister notes that Japanese had complained bitterly about a Japanese village in London in 1885 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 192).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Labour and the open shop — Unions and socialists opposed the fair over its open-shop hiring, and Seattle workers protested on Labor Day 1909. (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)

Questions for study

  1. Why did Japan alone among Asian governments exhibit in Seattle, and what did the welcome given to the Japanese delegation do to the racial politics that other scholars find at the fair? (Hotta-Lister 1999; Rydell 1984)
  2. Were the Pay Streak villages teaching visitors about racial progress, or were they entertainment? Can both readings be right at once? (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  3. The reference works and Rydell's chapter both describe the same fair. What does each leave out? (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell 1984)
  4. How far did labour opposition change the fair's programme, such as the Labor Day events? (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  5. Why do the sources give different totals for the same attendance, and what does that tell us about how such figures were produced? (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  6. Goode called the Igorot village "a fine anthropology and ethnological display" (Rydell 1984). Benedict calls the sensational panoramas caricatures of the serious exhibits (Benedict 1983p. 56). Which description fits the village better, and who was qualified to judge it?
  7. The Seattle notices in eastern papers during the 1907 panic were meant to bring in workers (Rydell 1984). How far did the Pay Streak concessions depend on labour recruited from outside the city?
  8. The Historical Dictionary dates the planning to 1906 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 206), while Rydell places it during the Portland fair (Rydell 1984). Which timeline is right, and what would each source have to have seen to date it that way?

7The Expositions in Portland and Seattle: “To Celebrate the Past and to Exploit the Future”Asia

Rydell argues that the Lewis and Clark Centennial in Portland (1905) and the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition in Seattle (1909) were built to link the Pacific Northwest to Asian markets and an American commercial empire, and that organized labor contested both. The chapter traces the closed-shop dispute, the referendum drive and the open-shop hiring that led to the Labor Day protest at Woodland Park. Its evidence is the exhibits and villages: the Igorot village on Portland's Trail and Seattle's Pay Streak, the Smithsonian and War Department displays of the Pacific possessions, the Oriental Exhibits Building, and the Japanese delegation's visit to Seattle under the slogan hands across the sea. Rydell also follows the concessionaires Felder and Schneidewind and their Igorot troupe through amusement parks, where a complaint that the performers wanted to go home was answered with higher wages. The chapter ends with the fairs' legacies in Portland's Guild Lake industrial district and the University of Washington campus.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
2Fairs of the Imperial Erap. 45Asia

Rydell, Findling and Pelle examine how American fairs from 1898 to 1915 presented the new overseas empire as natural and the racial hierarchy that justified it. They begin with the Trans-Mississippi Exposition in Omaha, where an Indian Congress, staged battles and an Old Plantation Village taught visitors about conquest and slavery, and move to Buffalo in 1901, where a color scheme ranked races from dark to light and a Filipino village cast American rule as uplift. St. Louis in 1904 receives the most space, for its anthropology displays and the Olympic events held alongside the fair. The authors then cover the Portland and Seattle fairs, which relied on Igorot and Eskimo villages, and the 1915 fairs in San Francisco, with its eugenics congresses, and San Diego, which reinvented the city's Spanish past and displayed human evolution. The evidence comes from fair records, newspapers and visitors' recollections. The chapter argues that these fairs were designed to show the desirability of colonialism and to justify racial hierarchy, and it closes by noting that the war which followed would reshape the next generation of fairs.

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions gives the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition a multi-page entry by J. D. Bowers quotes Taft's aim of exploiting Alaska's resources and Pacific-slope trade, and says the fair was meant to show that the wild frontier was gone. Most of the entry describes opening events, the Japanese participation and the Pay Streak villages, including the Igorot and Inuit displays, which Bowers describes as sensational shows of primitive peoples. The entry ends with a bibliographical essay that is the book's main guide to further reading on the fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. Rydell's chapter 'The Expositions in Portland and Seattle' treats the two fairs together as 'imperial theme parks' promoting trans-Pacific trade and racial progress, and the Seattle material forms its second half. Rydell argues that the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition offered Japan as a trading partner while its Pay Streak villages and federal displays presented Filipinos, Alaskans and other Pacific peoples as stages of racial development and as sources of resources. He shows that the fair's directors had to answer labour opposition, which called it a 'scab fair', and anti-Japanese hostility on the Pacific Coast, and used Japan Day and the 1909 Japanese delegation to do so. Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Rydell, Findling and Pelle give the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition about two pages in their section 'Fairs of the Imperial Era'. They describe a fair organised by local businessmen, financed mainly by railroads, banks and newspapers and opposed by labour, whose Pay Streak villages presented racial characteristics as the marker of progress. The authors say the federal Philippines displays and the Filipino building taught visitors that American rule was needed to uplift Filipinos and to serve American commerce. In their introduction they treat the fair as one thread in a nationalising synthesis that combined economic expansion overseas with racial ideas, which the 1893 Chicago fair had set out.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. Elsewhere coverage is slight. The interwar chapter notes the Asian ornamental elements and Pacific Basin focus of San Francisco's 1939-40 fair, with elephant towers modelled on Cambodia's Angkor Wat. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction and conclusion name Osaka 1970 and Aichi 2005 as later Japanese fairs. No passage mentions Thailand, Indochina or the Dutch East Indies.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
'An Exhibitionary Culture' section, pp. 20-24

Rydell's section on turn-of-the-century fairs cites the Seattle fair briefly as an example. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. Wikipedia

The Alaska–Yukon–Pacific Exposition, acronym AYP or AYPE, was a world's fair held in Seattle in 1909 publicizing the development of the Pacific Northwest. It was originally planned for 1907 to mark the 10th anniversary of the Alaska Gold Rush, but the organizers learned of the Jamestown Exposition being held that same year and rescheduled. The fairgrounds were hosted on an undeveloped portion of the present day campus of the University of Washington.

  • Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition (1909): The Olmsted Legacy HistoryLink.org (Jennifer Ott) · encyclopedia article
    Essay by Jennifer Ott on how landscape architect John C. Olmsted designed the fairgrounds on the University of Washington campus and how the clearing and construction left lasting features on the campus.
  • 1909 Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition Seattle Municipal Archives · official body
    City archives guide to the fair with a short history, about 20 links to 1907-1909 City Clerk documents (petitions, letters, agreements), captioned images and maps, and links to city ordinances and departmental annual reports for 1908-1909.
  • AKA: Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition, Japan Pavilion, Seattle, WA Pacific Coast Architecture Database (PCAD), University of Washington · scholarly project
    Database record for the Japan Building at the 1909 fair: constructed 1908-1909, dedicated 21 July 1909, since demolished. Notes that unsold exhibit goods were to be returned to Japan in October 1909. The only Japanese-pavilion web source we found that was opened.
