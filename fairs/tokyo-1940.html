Grand International Exposition of Japan (1940)

紀元二千六百年記念日本万国博覧会 (Kigen 2600-nen Kinen Nihon Bankoku Hakurankai)
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Grand International Exposition of Japan was planned for Tokyo and Yokohama, to open on 15 March 1940 and run until 30 August, to celebrate the 2,600th anniversary of the mythological ascension of Emperor Jimmu (Kargon et al. 2015p. 83; Kargon et al. 2015p. 89). The government suspended it on 15 July 1938, because of the war with China and shortages of steel, and it was never held (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90). Kargon et al.'s chapter uses the unbuilt fair as a test case for reactionary modernism (Kargon et al. 2015p. 83). Tickets sold for it were honoured at the Osaka 1970 fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

In May 1930 a group of businessmen and officials from Tokyo and Yokohama formed a committee to hold an international exposition. On 10 October 1932 a resolution was passed unanimously to hold it in Tokyo in 1940, to coincide with the 2,600th anniversary of the accession of Emperor Jimmu (Kargon et al. 2015p. 85). Earlier plans had not succeeded. Fairs planned for 1912 and 1917 did not materialise, and a fair linked to the Tokyo Peace Memorial Exposition of 1922 could not be prepared in time (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405).

The chapter tests Jeffrey Herf's concept of reactionary modernism against the plans and against Japan's pavilion at the 1939 New York World's Fair (Kargon et al. 2015p. 84). Its argument is that the exposition set modern technology inside an emperor-centred ideology, and presented technology as part of Japanese tradition rather than as a Western import (Kargon et al. 2015p. 83; Kargon et al. 2015p. 85). The exposition was aimed at creating "a world of wonder where Japanese imperial subjects and other international visitors could not only be enthralled" (Kargon et al. 2015p. 85).

Organisers and finance

The Association of the Japan International Exposition was created on 31 May 1934. Its president was the Tokyo mayor, Ushizuka Toratarō, and its vice-presidents were the Yokohama mayor, Ōnishi Ichirō, and the industrialist Hoshino Seki (Kargon et al. 2015p. 85). The Diet endorsed the plan in 1935 and approved funds for supervision in May 1936 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 85; Kargon et al. 2015p. 86). The cabinet agreed on 25 August 1936 that the Association should promote the event (Kargon et al. 2015p. 86).

After Konoe Fumimaro became prime minister in June 1937, Prince Chichibu became president and Konoe vice-president (Kargon et al. 2015p. 87). Fujihara Ginjirō was recommended as president of the organising body, and the chapter describes his book of 1936 as an attempt to marry Japan's modernity with its traditions (Kargon et al. 2015p. 86). The cost was put at ¥44.5 million, with a further ¥15 million for related public works. Admission tickets were to be sold in advance to meet part of the cost, and some went on sale in 1938 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90).

Attendance estimates differ. The chapter gives a planning estimate of forty-five million (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88), and the encyclopedia says forty million were expected (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430). The texts do not reconcile the two figures.

Site and buildings

The site combined reclaimed land on Tokyo Bay around Tsukishima with a site at Yokohama, together about thirty-three square kilometres (816 acres) (Kargon et al. 2015p. 89). Most pavilions were to stand at Tokyo, and Yokohama was to have pavilions for oceanography and fisheries and an aquarium (Kargon et al. 2015p. 89). The encyclopedia describes the Yokohama site as a subsidiary one for maritime products, an aquarium and a music hall (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430).

The centre of the plan was a Commemoration Hall to the founding of the nation, which would show images, relics and displays of the development of the empire (Kargon et al. 2015p. 89). A design competition in 1937 was won by Takanashi Shōju, whose shrine-like building with a tower the chapter reads as a sign of the nationalist mood of the late 1930s (Kargon et al. 2015p. 89). Other planned buildings included a Hall of Science, a Hall of Art and Education, an architecture workshop and a Hall of Communications (Kargon et al. 2015p. 89; Kargon et al. 2015p. 93). The administrative building at Tsukishima, finished in September 1937 in an East Asian style, was converted to care for sick and wounded soldiers (Kargon et al. 2015p. 92).

Exhibits and attractions

The planned exhibits presented Japanese industry as the result of technical skill. An English-language guide to the exposition credited Japan's success to hard-working, frugal labour, manual skill and the continuous use of the latest technical knowledge (Kargon et al. 2015p. 93). Nippon Kōbō, the design studio, illustrated the guide's section on industrial Japan, and its cover used a woodblock design with Mount Fuji (Kargon et al. 2015p. 95). A building for the tourist trade was also planned (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90).

The chapter links the exhibits to the new communications technologies of the 1930s. Shin Mizukoshi, whom it cites, has written that the proposed Olympics encouraged engineers to develop television (Kargon et al. 2015p. 93). The chapter judges that television was likely to have been shown in the Hall of Communications (Kargon et al. 2015p. 93).

Olympics and anniversary

The Olympic Games were to be held in Tokyo in 1940, directly after the Berlin Games of 1936, and Tokyo was awarded them the day before the Berlin Games opened (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88). The government meant the exposition to show the achievements of Emperor Jimmu and his successors, who had ruled in an unbroken line for twenty-six centuries (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88). The chapter says that the exposition, had it been held, would have reinforced the imperialist project of Japan (Kargon et al. 2015p. 84), and that promotional material for the Olympics and the exposition showed both Japan's tradition and its modernity (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88).

Visitors and reception

The fair never opened, so the texts describe no visitors' experience (Kargon et al. 2015p. 6). The planned message was that Japan had modernised rapidly during the Meiji era while keeping its own culture, and that visitors would be assured that indigenous traditions had not been forgotten (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88).

Why it was not held

The chapter links the cancellation to the war with China from 1937 and to shortages of building materials, especially steel. These left Japan little option but to withdraw from hosting the Olympics and the exposition (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90). The government officially suspended the exposition on 15 July 1938 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90).

The encyclopedia and the dictionary give a different account. Both say that war intervened and that the plans were postponed indefinitely (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 405). The 1990 dictionary says the plans were well advanced when war broke out in Europe (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). The chapter, by contrast, says that the outbreak of war with China in 1937 forced the government to cancel the event (Kargon et al. 2015p. 83). It also judges that the aesthetic management of the exposition was less thorough than in Germany and Italy, yet that "the 1940 exposition (and Olympics) shows us the contours of what seems to suggest ambitions that echo those in the Axis nations" (Kargon et al. 2015p. 105).

Its place in the series

The chapter places the Tokyo plan among the two planned fairs of the period that were never opened, the other being Rome 1942 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 2; Kargon et al. 2015p. 6). The dictionary records an earlier Tokyo plan for 1912, postponed to 1917 as the Grand Exhibition of Japan and cancelled by the First World War (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 404).

Two later links are recorded. The dictionary says Japan's 1965 bid for the 1970 fair was accepted partly because of the cancelled 1940 fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339). The encyclopedia says Japan's interest in the Osaka fair arose partly from planning for the 2,600th anniversary (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 345). In Nagoya, Hopson writes that the mayor of the 1937 peace exposition kept its scale modest in deference to the Tokyo Olympics and the anniversary celebrations (Hopsonp. 144). The chapter notes that the Japanese pavilion in New York in 1939 stressed tradition on the eve of the 2,600th anniversary (Kargon et al. 2015p. 94).

Aftermath and legacy

The chapter argues that the cancellation meant few plans for major buildings were realised (Kargon et al. 2015p. 99). It reads the plans as a turn to the past in search of an alternative to Western modernism, a move it links to the postmodernism that flourished later (Kargon et al. 2015p. 107). It also cites the Tokyo National Museum, begun in 1932 and completed in 1938, as a late example of the Imperial Crown style (Kargon et al. 2015p. 99).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial ideology — The exposition was to present the imperial line as unbroken and to draw attention to the emperor (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88; Kargon et al. 2015p. 104).
  • Reactionary modernism — The chapter tests Herf's concept against the plans and treats the fair as a case of technology framed as Japanese tradition (Kargon et al. 2015p. 83; Kargon et al. 2015p. 107).
  • Olympics and anniversary — The Olympics and the anniversary were planned together, and the anniversary shaped other events such as Nagoya's peace exposition (Kargon et al. 2015p. 88; Hopsonp. 144).
  • Technology and communications — Television and the Hall of Communications were to show technology as part of the empire (Kargon et al. 2015p. 93).
  • War and suspension — The sources give different causes for the end of the fair, from war with China to war in Europe (Kargon et al. 2015p. 90; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339).
  • Afterlife in later fairs — Tickets were honoured at Osaka 1970, and the cancelled fair is linked to that fair's bid (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 430; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 339).

Questions for study

  1. How far can a fair that was never built show the ideas of its planners, and what evidence would test that claim?
  2. Why do the texts give different causes for the fair's end, and which account should students rely on?
  3. Which attendance figure is the more reliable, and what does the difference between them reveal about the planning?
  4. How did the plans for a 1940 fair in Tokyo shape the choices of other Japanese cities, such as Nagoya in 1937 and Osaka in 1970?

Kargon, Robert H., Karen Fiss, Morris Low and Arthur P. Molella. World’s Fairs on the Eve of War: Science, Technology, and Modernity, 1937–1942. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2015.
1Introduction: World’s Fairs, Modernity, and the Demand for Authenticityp. 1Asia

The introduction sets out a comparative study of five fairs planned in the 1930s, three built (Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939) and two planned but never opened (Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942). Its argument is that these fairs became arenas of ideological struggle over modernity, as liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define the future. It traces how world's fairs since the Crystal Palace of 1851 taught populations to accept industrial change as progress, and how after the First World War, with the older empires gone, nations sought a unifying authenticity to animate modern technology. It then sketches the international crises of 1931 to 1936, from Manchuria to the Rhineland, which made the fairs' messages more confrontational. The national messages are outlined in turn: imperial Rome, racial unity in Germany, Japan's pan-Asian mastery, and the democratic displays of France and the United States. The introduction ends by describing the fairs as propaganda contests that preceded a war of armies, and by noting that the war of ideas proved significant for its outcome.

Brown, Kendall H. “Fair Japan: Japanese Gardens at American World’s Fairs, 1876–1940.” Journal article.
Performing on the World Stagep. 14Asia

Brown argues that Japanese gardens at world's fairs were staged performances, not neutral displays of beauty. Japan's official image split into a modern, industrial pavilion and a timeless, feminine garden. Brown ties this to fairs timed around Japan's wars, from Chicago in 1893 to San Francisco and New York in 1939 and 1940. The gardens, he concludes, naturalized a politically charged message, presenting Japan's imperial present as the natural expression of an unchanging culture.

In The Wake of Exposition Gardensp. 16Asia

Brown considers the gardens' legacy as relations between Japan and the United States soured from about 1913. American fascination with Japanese culture was separated from views of Japan as a political power, and the feminine, pastoral image became easy to dismiss once Japan was a threat. He asks whether the propaganda delayed American responses to Japanese expansion, citing the St. Louis displays of 1904 and the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939 and 1940.

This appendix to the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions lists expositions that were planned but never held, from New York 1913 and Baltimore 1914 to Calgary 2005, each as a short history. It does not argue a thesis; it records how each proposal was made, which sites and themes were chosen, and where it stopped. The causes recur: world wars and the Great Depression, economic slumps, doubts that a city could afford the cost, governments and mayors withdrawing support, and rival bids that won the BIE vote. Several entries are detailed, notably Philadelphia 1976, Los Angeles 1981, Paris 1989, Chicago 1992, Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, which trace the bidding, financing and political fights. Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
5Imperial Subjectification: The Collapsing Spaces of a Wartime Cityp. 168Asia

Henry examines how the late colonial state, after war with China began in 1937, tried to make Koreans in Keijo (Seoul) loyal subjects of the emperor, a process the chapter calls imperial subjectification. Its evidence comes from the Keijo nippo's coverage of the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition, the torch relay from Ise to Korea Shrine and a school tour of Japan's sacred sites, and from the Namsan shrines, the militarised autumn festival, Seoul Nation-Protecting Shrine and the campaign to install Ise amulets and miniature shrines in homes. Henry sets the official claim that rising shrine visits proved growing reverence against the complaints of Shinto priests about coercion and formality, and against photographs that show Koreans bowing in a Confucian manner or keeping their hats on. The chapter argues that loyalty was performed under observation and that the state could measure it only by counting. It ends by showing how the cult fell short in the home, where amulets were discarded and shrines misused, and by noting a Christian seminary professor who visited Namsan while keeping his own faith.

Hopson, Nathan. “‘A Bad Peace?’: The 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition.” Journal article.
Accommodation and Survivalp. 144Asia

Hopson shows how the expo survived pressure from Tokyo and the Imperial Japanese Army. Concern not to rival Tokyo or upstage the 1940 Olympics led the mayor to keep the scale modest and call the event pan-Pacific. The army's demands produced a Hall of National Defense and Aviation, with a panorama of modern warfare and a radio tour in April 1937. The mayor's defence was to recruit Prince Higashikuni as chairman, which shielded the expo from direct military criticism.

In the contents of this work

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
4Performing Japan in the ‘World of Tomorrow’p. 113Asia

Peterson argues that Japan's pavilion at the 1939-1940 New York World's Fair staged a gendered performance of diplomacy and femininity for American audiences, at a time when Japan was moving towards war. The pavilion, reputedly modelled on the Ise-Jingū Shrine and designed by Nippon Kōbō, combined halls and a garden in which kimono-clad women reeled silk, served tea and arranged flowers, and the press releases pitched these scenes to newspapers' women's pages. Peterson then turns to the 1939 and 1940 Japan Day ceremonies, where diplomats recited a love-fest narrative of friendship and Perry's opening of Japan, even as relations soured. The Takarazuka Revue, which played the fairgrounds in May 1939 with a gender-bending, modern show, was dismissed by New York critics who wanted a Japan of cherry blossoms and willowy maidens. The chapter concludes that Americans wanted Japan to stay a fixed image of tradition, and that Japan's own staging helped keep that image in place until Pearl Harbor.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Kargon and colleagues examine five international expositions planned in the years before the Second World War: Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939, which were built, and Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942, which were planned but never opened. The volume argues that these fairs were arenas of ideological struggle in which liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define modernity and to prepare their peoples for war. The introduction sets them against the history of fairs from the Crystal Palace of 1851 and the crises of the 1930s, and each chapter treats one fair through its planning documents and press coverage. The book closes with the first postwar universal exposition, Brussels 1958, which carried the Cold War rivalry into a fair devoted to peaceful atomic energy.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure as one case rather than as a theme. Japan is the only Asian nation with a substantial role, and its treatment is concentrated in chapter 5, "Modernity on Display: The 1940 Grand International Exposition of Japan", on the Tokyo and Yokohama exposition cancelled in 1938: the Commemoration Hall competition, the Nippon Kōbō studio, Bruno Taut's writings on Japanese architecture and the 1942 "Overcoming Modernity" symposium. Japan's participation in fairs run by others is briefer: the Paris 1937 pavilion by Sakakura Junzō (chapter 2), the New York 1939 displays (chapters 4 and 5), and the low-profile exhibit at Brussels 1958 (chapter 7). Chapter 3 mentions a visit to Düsseldorf by Prince Chichibu. Elsewhere Asia is context or comparison. Chapter 5 links the exposition to the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, the Manchukuo express "Asia", Kenzō Tange's 1942 remark that admiring Angkor Wat was "the mark of an amateur", and a Thai proposal for a Japan-Thailand cultural centre in Bangkok. China appears as the war that cancelled the Tokyo fair and, in chapter 1, as the Manchurian crisis of 1931. Apart from Japan, the passages show no East or Southeast Asian nation exhibiting at these fairs and no Asian colonial display. The only exhibition held in Asia is Osaka 1970, mentioned in passing in chapter 7.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hopson examines the 1937 Nagoya Pan-Pacific Peace Exhibition, a city-branding event meant to present Nagoya as an industrial and cultural centre. Its ideal of capitalist peace through trade was compromised by the Imperial Japanese Army, which forced in a national defence hall and live-fire displays, and by pressure from Tokyo. Hopson argues that the expo still shows the diversity of Japanese politics before the war with China. After an introduction, the article covers local promotion, accommodation with the army, a comparison with Paris 1937 and a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. The article concerns a Japanese exhibition in Nagoya, so Japan is its subject throughout. The chapter 'Local Promotion and Capitalist Peace in the Pacific Age' carries most of this, covering the prefectural, colonial and firm pavilions such as Toyota, Mayor Ōiwa's 'Great Nagoya' campaign and the expo's place in a new Japan-led 'Pacific Age'. 'Accommodation and Survival' treats the Imperial Japanese Army's demands and the recruitment of Prince Higashikuni as chairman, and the introduction places the expo in the diversity of Japanese politics before the war with China. Beyond Japan, East Asian participation is limited to Japan's colonies and puppet regimes. The pavilions of Manchukuo and the East Hebei regime, and the Taiwanese and Korean pavilions named in the conclusion, are presented as Japanese imperial display, and the East Hebei Hall and Day are read as evidence of Japan's ambitions in China. Southeast Asia is slight: a Dutch Indies exhibit appears among pavilions of other imperial powers, alongside Ceylon, a South Asian case outside the question. Visitors are described as mostly from Japan and its colonial possessions, and no Southeast Asian participation or colonial display of people is discussed.
