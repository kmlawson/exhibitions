Empire Exhibition (Scotland)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Empire Exhibition, Scotland, opened at Bellahouston Park in Glasgow on 3 May 1938 and closed on 29 October (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). Greenhalgh calls it the last great imperial exhibition, and Roche says Britain returned to the imperial theme for the final time there (Greenhalgh 1988p. 61; Roche 2000p. 59). Historians study it for its modernist architecture, its colonial displays and its treatment of Scotland as both an industrial partner in the empire and a Highland periphery (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 291; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the depression. Its idea was conceived in 1931 as a way to promote employment and advertise Scottish industries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). After 1936 the Scottish Development Council planned and promoted it, backed by a guarantee fund of £750,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). Limond names the shipyard owner James Lithgow as the prime mover, working through the Scottish National Development Committee, which in October 1936 committed itself to an event meant to stimulate Scottish work and production (Lawn 2009p. 188; Lawn 2009p. 188). Limond explains that the imperial framing was a device to get round the objection of the Department of Overseas Trade, which held that such an exhibition would breach the international agreement policed by the Bureau International des Expositions, the body that limited how often such events could be held in one country (Lawn 2009p. 188). The stated objects included illustrating the progress of the British empire, fostering empire trade and stimulating Scottish production (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297).

Finance, organisation and site

The exhibition cost £10 million to construct (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). The Earl of Elgin was president, Cecil M. Weir convenor of the Administrative Committee, S. J. Graham general manager and Thomas S. Tait architect (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 421). The fair was sited at Bellahouston Park rather than Kelvingrove, which had housed Glasgow's major exhibitions since 1888, and it stood beside the Ibrox football stadium (Lawn 2009p. 188; Lawn 2009p. 189). The site was built around a hill 170 feet high and covered 175 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). The two dictionaries give the loss as £118,691, covered by the guarantors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 291). The survey's table gives £118,591 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Architecture

Tait designed almost every structure on the site, which gave the fair what Limond calls a modernist unity, with a tower on the park's only hill as its centrepiece (Lawn 2009p. 189; Lawn 2009p. 189). The buildings were flat-roofed and angular, with smooth surfaces, metal frames and pylons (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). MacKenzie, who wrote the entries in both dictionaries, finds them far more satisfying than the concrete of Wembley (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 291). Greenhalgh argues that the Scottish Pavilion and the central Tower owe much to the Tourist Pavilion by Robert Mallet-Stevens, a prototype for later exhibition architecture (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168). Allwood says that Tait broke away from classical design for British exhibition buildings (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). Geppert says the fair featured a Tower of Empire as its main attraction (Geppert 2010p. 325). Limond says the site has largely been dismantled, and that only the former Palace of Art remains (Lawn 2009p. 189), while Geppert's appendix row for the fair records the demolition of the tower in July 1939 (Geppert 2010p. 260).

A preview in The Times of 11 March 1938 described the tower as a streamlined steel skyscraper rising from the park's only hill (Lawn 2009p. 189; Lawn 2009p. 189). A restaurant at its foot had branches growing through the floor, and a second restaurant on the brow of the hill was shaped like a ship's hull pointing at the United Kingdom pavilion (Lawn 2009p. 189; Lawn 2009p. 189). The same preview says that three government departments, the Ministries of Health and of Agriculture and Fisheries and the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research, shared that pavilion (Lawn 2009p. 189). It also says that the Dominions had separate pavilions, some by their own architects, all approved by the exhibition architect (Lawn 2009p. 189; Lawn 2009p. 189).

Tait gathered about him a team of young architects, many of whose names, Allwood says, reappeared at postwar exhibitions, Basil Spence among them (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111; Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). Specific associations, such as the Bee-keepers, and companies, such as Shell-Mex, ICI and Dunlop, had their own buildings rather than sharing the Scottish or imperial pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297).

Nations and colonies on show

Greenhalgh describes a Colonial Avenue at the Glasgow International, containing national pavilions and a multi-nation Colonial Court (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Dominions and colonies were, in Greenhalgh's account, displayed according to their economic usefulness, the standard British format since 1893 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). The official catalogue lists the territories grouped in the Court, from Malaya and the West Indies to Hong Kong, the Falkland Islands and Somaliland, around a central exhibit of their trade with Britain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). The Dominions (self-governing members of the empire) and Colonial avenues ran between the pavilions of the imperial territories, and Kingsway carried the pavilions of the British government, the city of Glasgow and an exhibit on the Women of the Empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). Geppert notes that one of Glasgow's three main axes was also called Kingsway (Geppert 2010p. 312). The bibliography in the 1990 dictionary says the Imperial Institute in London was the main coordinator of the dominions and colonial exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292). Limond says that countries unable to build their own pavilions were represented in the Institute's Central Colonial Hall (Lawn 2009p. 190). Limond also records that the South African authorities shipped over a private collection of African art and objects, while Australia relied on a propaganda film (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190).

Limond describes two revolving globes, estimated at 15 feet across, on top of one of the buildings in the Imperial Institute's Central Colonial Hall (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190). A pavilion carried a map of Canada in burnished copper (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190). Australia relied on film, with almost continuous screenings of the propaganda documentary A Nation is Built (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190).

Greenhalgh reads the Colonial Court as a diorama. The lesser territories were fused into one economic unit, with Britain at the centre of their world (Greenhalgh 1988p. 63; Greenhalgh 1988p. 63). He says the display aimed to make the colonies feel British to the ordinary visitor, and quotes the message as "this is yours, see how naturally it fits into" the visitor's own way of life (Greenhalgh 1988p. 63; Greenhalgh 1988p. 63).

Peoples on display

Limond describes living exhibits, groups of people brought in to represent colonial cultures, and writes that this was "a long-standing practice at such events, though one that attracted controversy in this case" (Lawn 2009p. 190). George Padmore and other activists staged protests against the practice (Lawn 2009p. 190). Greenhalgh treats the Irish village BallyMaclinton, first shown in 1908, and the Scottish village that joined it in 1909 and reappeared at Glasgow in 1938 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106). Greenhalgh writes that "These were ideologically difficult villages for the British to create" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106), because the villages raised the status of Ireland and Scotland in relation to the colonies (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). The women's sections at Wembley and at Glasgow both took empire as a central theme, and the Queen was patron to both (Greenhalgh 1988p. 187).

Limond reads the mock Highland village as a return to the historical pastiche that earlier Glasgow shows had made popular, and he cites McArthur and Lorimer for it (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190). The Scottish village of 1938 was called An Clachan and was meant to convey the rugged Scottish spirit that had helped to build the empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). Filipová notes that Walter Scott had crystallised the identification of Scottishness with Highlandism through the ceremonial visit of George IV to Edinburgh in 1822 (Filipová 2015p. 212; Filipová 2015p. 212).

Greenhalgh's reading is sharper. Quoting McArthur, he says that core countries define the regions they are not, so that Ireland and Scotland had to be different for the English to differentiate themselves and rule (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107; Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). He says the Highland idyll was "lifted from its context" and made to stand for the essence of Scottish culture (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). He adds that BallyMaclinton was conceived when the Tories Bonar Law and Carson were stirring Ulster to revolt (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107; Greenhalgh 1988p. 107).

Visitors, education and reception

The visitor total is given as 12,593,232, plus about 600,000 who attended stadium events outside the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297). Limond estimates 12 to 13 million visitors (Lawn 2009p. 191). Roche's table gives an attendance of 2.6 for the fair, read in millions on the scale of the other rows, with no profit figure (Roche 2000p. 43). Limond reports a general consensus that Glasgow had excelled itself in modernist design (Lawn 2009p. 189). The closing events included a son et lumière show, a fly-past of military aircraft and a Peace Pavilion in an obscure corner of the site (Lawn 2009p. 191). Glasgow City Corporation paid for all the pupils in its schools to attend, in line with the educational value the period gave to visits to industry and to the empire's products (Lawn 2009p. 190). Limond argues that even in 1938 every effort was made to show what government was doing to make life longer and happier (Lawn 2009p. 196), and that the event's pleasures were more populist than grand but consonant with an age of mass education (Lawn 2009p. 196). Greenhalgh judges the fine art palaces of 1911 and 1938 "barely above the mediocre", and says that only a retrospective of the Glasgow school prevented the 1938 display from being a total failure (Greenhalgh 1988p. 210; Greenhalgh 1988p. 210).

The Scottish Council for Art and Industry showed the newest and best in domestic design. The Fitter Britain exhibit included models of a nursery school and of parts of an elementary school, and was reported as pointing towards a better managed educational future (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190). A sculptured figure at the United Kingdom pavilion stood for scientific research, as Nature described it in April 1938 (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190).

Allwood records that, despite the wettest summer on record, more than 12 million visitors came into the park that summer (Allwood et al. 2001p. 111; Allwood et al. 2001p. 111). The Glasgow politician Patrick Dollan judged that the fair had shown "a finer and more colourful way of living", and that people could educate and enjoy themselves without drabness (Lawn 2009p. 191; Lawn 2009p. 191).

Glasgow's reputation in 1938

Glasgow's image was already a problem before the fair opened. Limond argues that the 1888 exhibition was partly an exercise in managing the city's reputation (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185), and he notes that Thomas Annan's photographs of the worst slums, taken between 1868 and 1871, had fixed the popular picture of a poor, dirty city (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185). Filipová says that Glasgow's exhibitions outdid Edinburgh's in size and receipts, and that Glasgow became known as Britain's premier exhibition city outside London (Filipová 2015p. 211; Filipová 2015p. 211).

By the late 1930s, Limond writes, Glasgow was widely seen as locked into terminal decline (Lawn 2009p. 187). Gang violence in the slums drew grim national attention (Lawn 2009p. 188). In 1935 the novel No Mean City, based loosely on the city's razor gangs, made a fictional gang leader, Johnnie Stark, a best-seller (Lawn 2009p. 188; Lawn 2009p. 188; Lawn 2009p. 188). Limond thinks the planners of 1938 may not have been directly influenced by it, but the novel gave their work an added edge as Glasgow tried again to prove its worth to the world (Lawn 2009p. 188; Lawn 2009p. 188).

How historians have interpreted it

The fullest readings come from MacKenzie's two dictionary entries. MacKenzie treats the fair as a showcase in which Scottish industrialism was set in an international but distinctively imperial economy (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 291). MacKenzie writes that "The exhibition therefore presented an interesting three-tiered structure: empire, metropole, and Celtic periphery." (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292). MacKenzie's own book, Propaganda and Empire, is cited as placing the fair in its propagandist and ethnic context (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292).

McArthur reads the fair through Scotland's place in the cultural and economic periphery, according to the 1990 dictionary's bibliography (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292). Greenhalgh's Ephemeral Vistas is described there as surveying the design, cultural, artistic, national and gender implications of the exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292). Limond places 1938 on an arc of educational aspiration that rises from the 1880s to the 1950s and then declines (Lawn 2009p. 196).

Roche reads 1938 as the last return of the imperial theme in Britain, and sets out a general account of imperial expos that aimed to assert empire among rival powers, justify it to the home public and win over elites among the colonised peoples (Roche 2000p. 59; Roche 2000p. 59). Geppert places the fair in comparison with Wembley and finds that it received far less international attention, as the London site did (Geppert 2010p. 311).

The texts agree that the fair was imperial and modernist. They differ on what it was for. MacKenzie stresses the economic and national argument, Limond stresses education and civic pride, and Greenhalgh stresses the ideological strain of showing Scotland and Ireland as colonial-style villages. The attendance figures also differ, from the 12.6 million of the dictionaries to Limond's 12 to 13 million and Roche's table entry of 2.6, and the loss differs by £100 between the dictionaries and the survey.

Limond disputes a view, which he attributes to Greenhalgh's 1997 work, that British exhibitions became overly didactic over time (Lawn 2009p. 196; Lawn 2009p. 196). He calls the imperial story of 1938 a form of benign, liberal imperialism, an inward and outward paternalism (Lawn 2009p. 190; Lawn 2009p. 190). He judges the educational aims of the events before 1988 to have been vague, pious, naively optimistic and sometimes paternalistic, but sincere (Lawn 2009p. 196; Lawn 2009p. 196).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Colonial territories from Malaya to Hong Kong

The clearest Asian presence in our texts is in the colonial section. Greenhalgh writes that at the Glasgow International a Colonial Avenue was built, with national pavilions and a multi-nation Colonial Court (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). The official catalogue lists Malaya, Ceylon, British North Borneo and Sarawak, and Hong Kong among the territories grouped there, around a central exhibit of their trade with Britain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). The backdrop showed tropical forests and rice and maize fields (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh places this in the British habit of displaying colonies by their economic use (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62).

India

Hoffenberg says that imperial authorities and their colonial agents continued to represent India through traditional pavilions, bazaars and craftsmanship at the final great imperial exhibitions, Wembley in 1924 to 1925 and Glasgow in 1938 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 276). In the passages we hold, Greenhalgh's Indian palaces are at Wembley, and Greenhalgh's remark on Glasgow concerns the scale of the imperial theme (Greenhalgh 1988p. 61; Greenhalgh 1988p. 61). Hoffenberg sets this official display against a different event, the All-India Khadi and Swadeshi Exhibition of 1937 to 1938, a notable example of nationalist reworking of the imperial vision (Hoffenberg 2001p. 276).

Who organised and paid

The texts name the Imperial Institute in London as the main coordinator of the dominions and colonial exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292), and Limond places the colonies without pavilions in the Institute's Central Colonial Hall (Lawn 2009p. 190). None of our texts says which colonial authority or merchant paid for the Asian territories' displays.

What the texts do not say

None of our texts names a pavilion of an East Asian or South Asian state at the fair. They give no Asian visitors, commissioners or observers, and say nothing about how Asian visitors reacted to the displays.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Modernist architecture — the texts treat the Bellahouston site as the fair's distinctive strength, with Tait's unity of design, the flat-roofed buildings and the tower (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Greenhalgh 1988; Lawn 2009; Allwood et al. 2001; Geppert 2010).
  • Imperial display and colonial avenues — the Colonial Avenue and Court, the Dominions avenues and the economic framing of colonies run through the accounts (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Lawn 2009).
  • Human showcases — living exhibits, the Irish and Scottish villages and the protests against colonial displays are described in Greenhalgh and Limond (Greenhalgh 1988; Lawn 2009).
  • Scotland's place in the empire — the fair set Scottish industry in an imperial economy and used a Highland identity as the national character (Findling & Pelle 1990; Findling & Pelle 2008; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Attendance and finance — the dictionaries agree on 12,593,232 visitors and a loss of £118,691, while the survey and Limond differ on figures (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Lawn 2009; Roche 2000).
  • Education and mass culture — Limond reads the fair as a civic and educational event within a century of Glasgow exhibitions (Lawn 2009).
  • Women of the Empire — the women's sections at Wembley and Glasgow both took empire as a theme (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008).

Questions for study

  1. Greenhalgh and Roche both call 1938 the last imperial display in Britain. What would change if the fair were compared with the New York fair of 1939 instead of with Wembley?
  2. Why did the Irish and Scottish villages seem ideologically difficult, and what did the Highland village contribute to the fair's account of Scottish identity?
  3. The attendance and loss figures differ between reference works and a survey. Which figure should a student cite, and why?
  4. Limond treats the fair as an educational event, yet records living exhibits and protests. Can both readings stand together?
  5. Who decided what the Colonial Court showed, and what do our texts leave unanswered about the commissioning of colonial and Asian displays?
  6. Limond says the 1938 planners may not have been influenced by No Mean City. Which sources, such as the Times previews or the Glasgow Herald supplements noted in the Encyclopedia, would show whether the fair's publicity answered the novel's image of the city? (Lawn 2009p. 188; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 298)
  7. Greenhalgh and Limond both see the fair as ideological, but one calls its pedagogy didactic and the other sincere if paternalistic. Can the living exhibits and the Highland village be read as both at once?
  8. Where did An Clachan sit in MacKenzie's three-tier structure of empire, metropole and Celtic periphery, and did visitors see the Highland village as part of the empire or as a separate Scottish story? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 297; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 292)

Chapters that treat it at length 2 in 2 works

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
Epilogue: Recessional: Imperial Culture and Colonial Nationalismp. 273Asia

The epilogue argues that exhibitions from 1851 to 1914 presented a traditionalist vision of empire, built on relics, royal ceremony and the preservation of supposedly disappearing cultures, and that this vision later served colonial nationalism as well. Hoffenberg cites the Sanchi Tope cast shown in London in 1871, the Gwalior Gateway at South Kensington in 1886, and Irish antiquities shown at Dublin in 1853 as examples of preservation that gave commissioners authority and their sponsoring states legitimacy. Antiquarian protection laws in England and the colonies supplied the legal frame. Displays of Indian art and labour were later claimed by the Indian National Congress for its swadeshi movement, while the Melbourne Trades Hall Council used the exhibition form to promote Australian goods and labour. The contrast between the neoclassical Australian pavilion and the durbar-style Indian pavilion at Wembley in 1924-25 shows how architecture still encoded hierarchy. The conclusion is that a neoimperial network of Commonwealth exhibitions continues to shape identities into the postcolonial era.

In the contents of this work

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
More of a school than a show? Events in Glasgow, 1888-1988David Limondp. 183

Limond traces how the educational aims of Glasgow's exhibitions changed over a century, using the 1888 International Exhibition, the 1938 Empire Exhibition, the 1951 Exhibition of Industrial Power and the 1988 Garden Festival. The argument is that the nineteenth-century event relied on self-improvement and left visitors to draw their own conclusions, that the 1938 and 1951 events were openly didactic and modernist, and that by 1988 the organisers had abandoned educational purpose, offering school parties a 'day out'. Evidence comes from contemporary press reports, guide-catalogues and an interview with the 1988 principal architect. The 1888 event is read as a response to Glasgow's reputation for poverty and violence, the 1938 event as an imperial showcase whose schoolchildren the city paid to attend, and the 1951 show as a story of uninterrupted technological progress. The chapter ends by arguing that the earlier aspirations were sincere, if paternalistic, and that their loss reflects a wider retreat from exhibitions as teaching.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Wikipedia

The Empire Exhibition was an international Exhibition held at Bellahouston Park in Glasgow, Scotland, from May to December 1938. The Exhibition offered a chance to showcase and boost the economy of Scotland and celebrate Empire trade and developments, recovering from the depression of the 1930s. It also marked fifty years since Glasgow's first great exhibition, the International Exhibition (1888) held at Kelvingrove Park. It was the second British Empire Exhibition, the first held at Wembley Park, London, in 1924 and 1925. Its function was similar to the first National Exhibition in Paris in 1798 and to the first International Exhibition, the Great Exhibition in London in 1851, attended by 6 million visitors. It was declared open by King George VI and Queen Elizabeth on 3 May 1938 at the Opening Ceremony in Ibrox Stadium, attended by 146,000 people.
