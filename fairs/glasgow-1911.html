Scottish National Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Scottish National Exhibition was held in Glasgow in 1911, the Glasgow response to the Scottish National Exhibition at Edinburgh in 1908 (Filipová 2015p. 145p. 211; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). Limond cites a 1999 article by Rembold that gives its title as the Glasgow History Exhibition, 1911 (Lawn 2009p. 202). The passages give no organisers, opening date or attendance figure. Greenhalgh counts Glasgow 1911 among the larger shows that carried the imperial theme (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58), and Roche's table records it as profit-making (Roche 2000p. 43).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The Scottish series

Findling and Pelle list the event in an appendix as the Scottish National Exhibition, Glasgow, 1911 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426). Wilson Smith dates the series of large Scottish exhibitions from Edinburgh's 1886 show and says the Edinburgh exhibition of 1908 was followed by "a Glasgow response in 1911" (Filipová 2015p. 211). He reads the alternation as rivalry between the two cities and says Glasgow won it, its exhibitions being bigger and more successful in attendance and receipts (Filipová 2015p. 211). He gives no figures for the 1911 show itself.

Walker and Ramsey, the Glasgow architects who designed the Canadian pavilion at Wolverhampton's 1902 exhibition, continued to work on national exhibition grounds at Edinburgh (1908) and Glasgow (1911) (Filipová 2015p. 145). The passages do not say that they designed the Glasgow grounds.

Empire and colony

The texts describe the 1911 show as imperial in its framing. No passage names a colony or dominion taking part, a colonised people, a native village or a colonial product at it, and none gives the organisers' aims. Roche counts Glasgow in 1888, 1901 and 1911 among the imperial expos and festivals that the British held (Roche 2000p. 42). Greenhalgh says that Glasgow's shows of 1901, 1911 and 1938 "carried the imperial theme" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58). He also places the Scottish Village among the white villages, which appeared sporadically after its London debut in 1909, including at Glasgow in 1911 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh is the most specific author here, and briefly so. He says the fine art palaces at Glasgow in 1911 and 1938 were restricted to standard disciplines, and that the insistence on Scottish artists left the 1911 display "barely above the mediocre" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 210). He argues that the Irish and Scottish villages were hard to create because they raised questions about the status of Ireland and Scotland in relation to the colonies (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106). Limond calls the 1901 and 1911 shows "far from insignificant" and sets them aside, so his chapter offers no account of the 1911 show (Lawn 2009p. 184).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial framing — Greenhalgh and Roche class the 1911 show with the imperial exhibitions, but neither describes colonised peoples at it (Greenhalgh 1988p. 58; Roche 2000p. 42).
  • Scottish rivalry — Wilson Smith reads the Edinburgh and Glasgow exhibitions as a contest between two cities, which he says Glasgow won (Filipová 2015p. 211).
  • White villages — Greenhalgh places the Scottish Village among the white villages and argues that such villages raised questions about Scotland's place in the empire (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106).
  • Fine art and national display — the 1911 fine art display, limited to standard disciplines and dominated by Scottish artists, is judged mediocre (Greenhalgh 1988p. 210).
  • Money — Roche's table records the 1911 show as profit-making, but its visitor figure is illegible in the extraction (Roche 2000p. 43); Wilson Smith's remarks on receipts concern the Glasgow exhibitions as a group (Filipová 2015p. 211).
  • Names and sources — the show is listed as the Scottish National Exhibition in the encyclopedia appendix (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426), and as the Glasgow History Exhibition in the 1999 article Limond cites (Lawn 2009p. 202).

Questions for study

  1. Greenhalgh and Roche place the 1911 show among imperial exhibitions, yet the passages describe a Scottish village and name no colonised people. What made a Scottish national exhibition imperial? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106; Roche 2000p. 42)
  2. Wilson Smith measures the Glasgow exhibitions by attendance and receipts, but no text gives figures for 1911 alone. What sources would supply them? (Filipová 2015p. 211; Roche 2000p. 43)
  3. Limond sets the 1901 and 1911 shows aside as far from insignificant. What would a fuller account of the 1911 show add to his argument about Glasgow's educational aims? (Lawn 2009p. 184)
  4. The encyclopedia and dictionary both place the show in an appendix of excluded fairs. What does that suggest about how reference works judged a national exhibition? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395)

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
Ch. 6 (Marta Filipová, on Wolverhampton), p. 145; ch. 9 (Wilson Smith, 'Exhibitionary Edinburgh' section), p. 211

Two chapters of this edited volume touch on the 1911 Glasgow show, each briefly: Filipová notes that the Glasgow architects who designed Wolverhampton's 1902 Canadian pavilion went on to work on national exhibition grounds at Edinburgh and Glasgow, and Wilson Smith places the Glasgow show in a rivalry with Edinburgh that he says Glasgow won. Neither chapter examines the 1911 show's content, visitors or any imperial dimension.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
Ch. 3, 'Imperial display', p. 58; ch. 4 'Human showcases', p. 106; ch. 8 (fine art palaces), p. 210; index entry 'G.E. 1911' (l. 12386)

Greenhalgh's book mentions the 1911 Glasgow show in three places, each briefly: as one of the larger imperial shows, as one of the occasions when the Scottish Village appeared, and as a fine art display judged mediocre. He gives no description of the show itself.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Ch. 'More of a school than a show? Events in Glasgow, 1888-1988', David Limond, pp. 183-187, in Lawn (ed.), Modelling the Future

Limond sets the 1901 and 1911 Glasgow exhibitions aside as far from insignificant while he concentrates on the 1888, 1938 and 1988 events and the 1951 show.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.
