Expo 2005 Aichi was the 2005 World Exposition, held on the eastern outskirts of Nagoya in Aichi Prefecture, Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). The Bureau of International Expositions (BIE) chose Japan over Calgary in June 1997 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Its theme, Nature’s Wisdom, was set on a site that was scaled back after environmental protest (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399; Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). The fair closed on 25 September with 22,049,544 visitors against a predicted 15 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Allwood, Allan and Reid doubt that it will revive interest in the medium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Nagoya had failed to secure the Olympic Games early in the 1980s, but the region still wanted a major international event (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). A national development plan of 1987 named Nagoya and the surrounding towns an industrial and technological nucleus zone (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). In October 1988 regional and local bodies pushed for a world exposition in Aichi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Two months later Japan’s delegate told the Bureau of International Expositions that Japan would host the first international exposition of the twenty-first century, in the eastern outskirts of Nagoya (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). The trade ministry that had run Expo 70 in Osaka was also discussing another such event (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399).

The BIE chose Japan (Aichi) over Canada (Calgary) in June 1997 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Japan had reportedly waged a vigorous, expensive campaign to win (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Allwood, Allan and Reid explain that Expo 90 in Osaka had filled the programme, so 2005 was the next free slot, and that the award followed a close contest with Calgary (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). The theme was Nature’s Wisdom, with several sub-themes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the theme as Beyond Development: Rediscovering Nature’s Wisdom (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196).

Organisers and finance

Within a month of the decision, a working group chaired by Shoichiro Toyoda, honorary chair of Toyota, set up the Japan Association for the 2005 World Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Toyoda later became chair of the association (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). The government chose Taizo Watanabe as Commissioner General, a name Appendix C prints as Tanzo Watanabe (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399p. 421). Shoichi Nakagawa, Minister of Economy, Trade and Industry, was the official in charge of the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399).

The balance sheet for 1997 to June 2006 shows expenditure of ¥256 billion ($2.17 billion), income of ¥268.9 billion ($2.28 billion) and a net surplus of ¥12.9 billion ($109 million) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). The enterprise benefited from national and regional subsidies, lottery receipts and corporate donations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404).

Site and buildings

The first site was forest and farmland (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). A nest of breeding goshawks was identified in the proposed site area, Kaisho Forest, on 12 May 1999 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Allwood, Allan and Reid call the discovery a major setback that forced a costly replanning after five years of work (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167). The organisers scaled the expo back, left the forest and natural features largely intact, and moved development to already-used or less sensitive ground (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399).

The sources give different areas. Allwood, Allan and Reid say the site was “downsized to a manageable 182 hectares” (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167). The encyclopedia names two Japan pavilions, a 158-hectare Nagakute area and a 15-hectare site at Seto (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400). The texts do not reconcile these figures.

The Global Loop, a pedestrian boulevard of 2.6 kilometres, linked the groups of pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400). Six Global Commons held temporary warehouses for exhibits from groups of countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400). The planned low-energy transport was infrequent: visitors queued for gondolas and waited for a tram in the summer heat (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400).

Exhibits and attractions

The Global House showed the head and tusks of a 12,000-year-old woolly mammoth (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400). A Nature’s Wisdom Award went to national pavilions for design and for the effect of their message. The last official expo awards had been given at Brussels in 1958 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 401). The theme-oriented attitude of the organisers, encouraged by the BIE, produced cohesive national pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 402).

Two corporate zones dominated the northern approach (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400). The Toyota Group pavilion was the best placed and most dominating pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 402). The American pavilion was mainly about Benjamin Franklin and was produced by BRC Imagination Arts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 403). Its private-sector deputy commissioner said that none of the funds for the pavilion came from the federal government, and Toyota made a substantial contribution (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 403).

Visitors and reception

Most pavilions had queues. Advance reservations shortened the wait for popular venues, but the liveliest shows could mean two to three hours in line (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Crowds sought out the American pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). On closing day the total was 22,049,544 visitors, far above the predicted 15 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Allwood, Allan and Reid report that the target audience had been reduced to 15 million in the face of recent events (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167). The encyclopedia concludes that the Japanese liked their fair and that “attendance was strong” (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Peterson gives no Aichi figure, but he sets Aichi beside the 28 million anticipated for Osaka 2025 and implies that Aichi’s attendance was close to that level (Peterson 2020p. 290). The texts do not reconcile this with the 22 million total.

Politics and controversy

The green lobby condemned “the use of a greenfield site” for an event with a theme of nature’s wisdom (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). Allwood, Allan and Reid recall the unrest that had marked the building of Narita Airport (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). They write that it says much for Japanese democracy that a dispute settled quickly in many nations forced a costly replanning in Japan (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167). The encyclopedia credits prodding from the BIE for the decision to scale back (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Once the bid was won, the host prefecture ran into a storm of environmental protest (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196).

Its place in the series

Expo 90 in Osaka came before Aichi (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). Allwood, Allan and Reid note its 82 nations and its record attendance of over 23 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). A Tokyo proposal for a World City Expo 96 on reclaimed land in Tokyo Bay was cancelled by the new Governor, Yukio Aoshima, less than a year before the opening (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166). Calgary’s losing bid would have celebrated the centennial of Alberta and Saskatchewan as provinces (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 434). Peterson lists Aichi with Tsukuba (1985) and Okinawa (1975) as Japan’s smaller specialised fairs (Peterson 2020p. 290). Allwood, Allan and Reid call Aichi essentially a specialised exposition, more modest than Hanover 2000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). Under the 1988 amendment, one BIE-registered event may take place in each five-year cycle (Allwood et al. 2001p. 196). Massidda counts Aichi among four expos of the new millennium that drew more than 135 million visitors together (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192).

How historians have interpreted it

Alfred Heller, in the encyclopedia entry, calls the fair “the greenest ever” (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). He ends by describing it as a reminder of Japan’s continuing eminence in eastern Asia and the world at large (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Allwood, Allan and Reid are more doubtful. They doubt that Aichi will regenerate interest in the medium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167), and they voice unease about whether Japanese industry could act as the big-spending exhibitors that such fairs need (Allwood et al. 2001p. 167). Peterson, writing mainly about later Japanese plans, reads the 2025 plan as an attempt to jump-start the faltering Osaka economy (Peterson 2020p. 290).

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Other Asian participants

Korea’s pavilion used the motif Light of Life, expressed through five colours (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 401). Korea and Germany won gold prizes for large pavilions, and the Philippines and the Netherlands won gold prizes for small pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 401). The Philippine pavilion used the coconut to show its many uses (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 402). In the Global Commons, Asia had 17 countries, and Oceania and Southeast Asia had 22 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 400).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Nature and sustainability — The encyclopedia calls the fair the greenest ever and describes its forest zone, recycling and nature trails (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399). Allwood, Allan and Reid show the same theme drawing a green lobby protest over the greenfield site (Allwood et al. 2001p. 166).
  • Site and environmental protest — A goshawk nest and the greenfield site forced a replanning, and the BIE pressed the organisers to scale back (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399; Allwood et al. 2001p. 167).
  • Japanese state and corporate leadership — A national plan, a Toyota chair and corporate pavilions dominated the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399p. 402).
  • Finance and BIE rules — The balance sheet shows a surplus, and the 1988 regime allows one registered event every five years (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404; Allwood et al. 2001p. 196).
  • Bidding and rival cities — Calgary lost in 1997, and Tokyo cancelled World City Expo 96 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 399p. 434; Allwood et al. 2001p. 166).

Questions for study

  1. After the goshawk nest was found, how far did the organisers keep the nature theme because it had already been chosen, and how far did the site change the meaning of the theme?
  2. The encyclopedia gives 22,049,544 visitors, but Peterson implies a figure close to 28 million. Which account is more reliable, and what evidence would settle it?
  3. Allwood, Allan and Reid doubt that Japanese industry would be the big-spending exhibitors that such fairs need. How far did the corporate pavilions at Aichi, such as the Toyota pavilion, answer that doubt?
  4. Why did a cancelled Tokyo fair and a losing Calgary bid matter to the story of Aichi, and what do the three bids show about the BIE's choice of host?

Denton examines how museums of the Japanese occupation in the PRC have moved from a heroic story of resistance toward one of victimization, and argues that this shift serves nationalist and economic aims. The chapter analyses three post-Mao sites. The Memorial Hall of the War of Resistance in Wanping moves between a chronological overview, dioramas of atrocity and a martyrs hall, and its 2005 revision stresses national confidence. Denton links these shifts to the decline of class-struggle narratives, to popular culture and to Holocaust memorial precedents. The chapter concludes that the museums use shared suffering to channel resentment toward Japan, though the victim narrative sits uneasily with the CCP's heroic resistance narrative.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. Each case is a chapter, organised by fair, using archival and ethnographic methods. The conclusion argues that state-directed national representation has declined as the form became costly for host cities, and that Asia has become the leading host of expositions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are the whole subject of this work, and their coverage is central. Japan is the main case in chapters 2 and 4 (the government site, 'Japan Beautiful' concession and fine arts galleries at San Francisco 1915; the pavilion and Takarazuka Revue at New York 1939-40). China fills chapter 3 (the official site and the Chinese Village at San Francisco 1915, with Chinatown's role) and chapter 5 (Expo '88 in Brisbane). The Philippines (chapter 6) and Indonesia (chapter 7) are studied through their New York 1964-65 pavilions, South Korea (chapter 8) and Thailand (chapter 9) through Milan 2015, and chapter 10 treats the Chinese Milan pavilion and Asia's rise as host. Most of the participation is by states, commissioners and merchants: the Japanese Commission, the Chinese commissioners Chen Qi and T.C. Chu, and the Six Companies running the Joy Zone village. Displays organised by others are also discussed. Chapter 2 notes that a Formosa tea house names a Japanese colony and reads Japan's Red Cross display as propaganda, and chapter 4 describes New York critics who wanted Japan as cherry blossoms and willowy maidens. Asian women are mainly performers and press subjects, notably in chapters 6 and 7. Asian visitors feature only as crowds at the Chinese Village groundbreaking. Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Burma and the Malay states are not discussed in the chapter overviews.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

In the conclusion, Rydell, Findling and Pelle list the planned Aichi World Expo 2005 near Nagoya beside Hannover 2000, as evidence that world's fairs were still being held. The text was written while the Aichi plans were still under way, and it makes no further comment on the fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. These were displays of Filipinos organised by American officials, promoters and anthropologists, not Filipino participation. China appears in a Chinese Village at Omaha in 1898, which the authors call little more than a souvenir shop, and in the Chinese village at San Francisco in 1915, which framed China as ripe for American investment. Japan appears through the Fair Japan concession at St. Louis, with its Ainu display, and through Japanese exhibits sent to Portland, which were hampered by reported discrimination against Asians at St. Louis. Elsewhere coverage is slight. The interwar chapter notes the Asian ornamental elements and Pacific Basin focus of San Francisco's 1939-40 fair, with elephant towers modelled on Cambodia's Angkor Wat. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction and conclusion name Osaka 1970 and Aichi 2005 as later Japanese fairs. No passage mentions Thailand, Indochina or the Dutch East Indies.

Wikipedia

Expo 2005 (2005年日本国際博覧会, Nisengo-nen Nippon Kokusai Hakurankai) was a world expo held for 185 days between Friday, March 25 and Sunday, September 25, 2005, in Aichi Prefecture, Japan, east of the city of Nagoya. Japan has also hosted Expo '70 Osaka (World Expo), Expo '75 Okinawa (Specialised Expo), Expo '85 Tsukuba (Specialised Expo), Expo '90 Osaka (Horticultural Expo) and Expo 2025 Osaka (World Expo). The site of the expo would become the Expo 2005 Aichi Commemorative Park after it ended and is now the home of Ghibli Park.
