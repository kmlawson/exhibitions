Century 21 Exposition (Seattle World's Fair)

The Century 21 Exposition ran in Seattle from 21 April to 21 October 1962, on a 74-acre site near downtown (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Allwood et al. 2001p. 129). It began as a fiftieth-anniversary celebration of the 1909 Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition and became a Space Age fair after Sputnik, with the Space Needle, a monorail and a federally funded US Science Exhibit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 473). Historians use it to study urban renewal, Cold War science and the influence of Disneyland on fair design (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 323; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 476; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 4).

Origins and aims

Seattle's proposal began in the mid-1950s as a celebration of the 1909 exposition's fiftieth anniversary, meant to revive an economy hit by layoffs at Boeing (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319). The first idea was a regional Festival of the West, meant to put Seattle on the map and revive downtown (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 22). Downtown leaders wanted the fair near the central business district, to direct investment to a part of the city that seemed to be passed by (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 323).

Sputnik changed the purpose. After the Soviet launch in October 1957, politicians and scientists used the Seattle fair to affirm American leadership in science and space, following a Brussels exposition of 1958 in which the US pavilion had been overshadowed by Soviet displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319). Organisers adopted the name Century 21 for the fair's futuristic focus (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324).

The dates moved. The fair was first projected for 1961 and shortened to six months to win endorsement from the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319). The texts give 1962 (Samuel 2007), planned for 1961 (Samuel 2007) and 1961-62 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The reference works give 21 April to 21 October 1962 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324).

Fair America gives a different account of the name. In early 1957 the legislature created a world's fair commission, and Governor Victor Rosselini, working with an informal group of supporters, chose the name Century 21 Exposition (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The encyclopedia entry credits the organisers instead (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324). The same book reports an earlier plan: in the autumn of 1956 the commission recommended a Festival of the West for July to October 1960 and 1961, because there was not enough time to prepare an exposition for 1959 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). In March 1957 the commission appointed Ewen Dingwall, executive director of the Washington State Research Council, as director. Its design standards board included Lawrence Halperin of San Francisco and Minoru Yamasaki of Detroit (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Organisation and finance

Edward Carlson chaired the Century 21 Corporation, and Ewen Dingwall was its director (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319). Voters approved a $7.5 million bond in November 1956 for land and construction, including conversion of the civic auditorium into an opera house (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Congress appropriated funds for the fair only in late 1959 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Four texts give four figures for the US Science Exhibit: slightly less than $10 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325), $9.5 million (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477), $9.2 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 320) and $10 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 23).

Organisers first asked the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) for attendance estimates. After judging them unreliable, they hired Economics Research Associates in 1960, whose figures came close to Century 21's own estimate of 10 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60).

The results are disputed too. The organisers claimed a net profit of $100,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 329). Another text gives about $1,000,000 in the black (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481), and a third says the fair earned enough to cover its costs (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A private consortium financed the Space Needle, which cost $4.5 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 23; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 23). Four Disney employees were hired full-time as advisers on management and operations (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 476).

Skee gives the reasons for the change of consultant. SRI's team had proposed that the fair and civic centre become a permanent "major spectacular fete", and its attendance figures rested on that plan (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60). The board dropped the idea because it did not fit BIE regulations. SRI would not revise its figures, so staff took their own figure of 10 million from SRI's projections (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60). ERA suggested basing its estimates on the past attendance of comparable attractions, such as city zoos (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60). Its role then grew until it was "an extension of the exposition staff", estimating revenues, helping with contracts and settling internal disputes (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 61).

Cost figures diverge sharply. Fair America puts development of the 74-acre site at $77 million and says about 75 per cent of the construction was permanent (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Samuel puts Seattle's outlay at a putative $80 million, against New York's planned $1 billion (Samuel 2007). Badger's appendix gives a cost of $100,000,000 (Badger 1979p. 131). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that the site scheme came out $32 million over budget, and that a committee member warned the organisers were leading the community down the road to bankruptcy (Allwood et al. 2001p. 131; Allwood et al. 2001p. 131). The underwriters had gone along with the plan reluctantly, hoping at least to recover their money (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60). Warren, citing an October 1963 municipal study, gives seven million out-of-state visitors who spent a further $320,000,000 off-site on food, lodging, transport and entertainment (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481).

Site, architecture and exhibits

The fair occupied 74 acres in the Warren neighbourhood, about a mile north of downtown (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 323). In 1959, 203 buildings there were demolished (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 476). A monorail carried visitors from downtown, but it never became a city transit system (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Space Needle was 605 feet tall in one text (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319) and 606 feet in another (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Minoru Yamasaki designed the US Science Exhibit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325). Its six buildings stood around arches and pools (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477). Visitors watched a ten-minute Eames film, rode the Boeing-sponsored Spacearium and saw a Horizons of Science exhibit (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477). NASA's pavilion showed satellite models and a mock-up of the Saturn V engine cluster (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25). John Glenn and Wernher von Braun toured the fair on 10 May 1962 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 26). The World of Tomorrow set a future city beside slides of atomic blasts and fall-out shelters (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479).

Maloney notes that Seattle, like the other later American fairs he lists, did not exceed 100 acres, unlike New York's fair of more than 600 acres (Maloney 2012p. 234). Yamasaki first sketched a soaring tower for the science exhibit, then redesigned it once the Space Needle design was known. The result was five interconnected buildings around a courtyard, with white arches rising more than a hundred feet above reflecting pools. Leslie and Mercelis call the style "space gothic" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 23; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 24). Warren says the science exhibit was the most discussed and best-funded pavilion, planned by a team of over one hundred scientists (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477). Pearce says the State Department asked the Eameses for an introductory film for what she calls five government pavilions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 212), while Warren and Allwood describe six buildings (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477; Allwood et al. 2001p. 131). Pearce adds that the historian Thomas Kuhn wrote the history section of The House of Science (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 212). Allwood describes that six-screen film as twelve minutes long, where Warren describes the Eames film as ten minutes (Allwood et al. 2001p. 131; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 477).

NASA's pavilion was underwritten by a dozen major contractors, among them Boeing, Hughes, Aerojet, McDonnell and Rocketdyne (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25). Samuel says NASA's display cost about $2 million and included the craft in which Alan Shepard made the first US suborbital flight (Samuel 2007). Fair America mentions the same capsule (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Ford's space ride carried one hundred passengers at a time on a tour of the earth, moon and planets (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25). Leslie and Mercelis put the attendance at Boeing's Spacearium, inside a Fuller dome, at 4.3 million by the close (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25).

The State of Washington's World of Century 21 pavilion, designed by Paul Thiry, hung 3,700 aluminium panels from more than five miles of cable, and was built to become a sports arena after the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479). General Motors, Pan Am, RCA, the American Library Association and France occupied its edges (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479). The Fine Arts Pavilion had five galleries, including art of the Ancient East and Northwest Indian art (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480). The World of Entertainment reused civic buildings. The 1927 Civic Auditorium became the Opera House, the Civic Ice Arena became the Arena, and the Food Circus filled the former 1939 National Guard armory (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480).

Visitors, attractions and reception

The reference works give 9.6 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Allwood et al. 2001p. 209). Another reference work records more than 10 million paid admissions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 320), and other texts give some ten million (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000) and under 9 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 250). The US Science Exhibit was the most popular part of the fair, with more than 6.7 million guests; the Space Needle and the World of Tomorrow each drew fewer than 3 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 326).

The midway drew crowds too. About ninety per cent of visitors went to the Gayway and Show Street zone, against fewer than half for the Science Pavilion (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481). Show Street's promoter, Gracie Hansen, set out to save the fair from science, and city officials closed a Show Street attraction more than once for decency violations (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480). Billy Graham's Sunday Crusade broke the fair's weekend attendance records (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 121). Forecasts had put attendance at 7.5 million, and the fair exceeded that by a third (Samuel 2007; Samuel 2007).

Warren counts twenty different amusement rides and forty-five games of chance on the Gayway. Outside concessionaires designed it, and their work was left to the last minute. A ferris wheel was not ready on opening day (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480). Fair America also gives twenty rides, a fun house and a cable-car terminal (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Show Street acts are named differently. Warren lists "Peep Backstage, USA", "Girls of the Galaxy" and "A Night in Paradise" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480). Rydell, Findling and Pelle name a Paradise revue, Backstage USA, a wax museum called Paris Spectacular, and Planet Eve, in which topless showgirls seduced performers dressed as astronauts (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The two lists overlap only in Backstage USA and a Paradise show. Russell Lynes, a critic for Harper's, wrote of the fair: "It is friendly and gay and feels like a girl in a flowered hat." (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)

Politics, nations and controversy

Congress barred governments that were Communist and held any Pacific Rim people in subjugation. That excluded North Korea, North Vietnam and the People's Republic of China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325). The Soviet Union and eastern European communist states declined to send official exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325). The texts disagree on how many nations took part: Allwood, Allan and Reid give 24 countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 131); Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary gives more than forty-nine nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 319); and Rydell, Findling and Pelle give some forty-nine nations and West Berlin (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Findlay describes the fair's Cold War framing bluntly: "The exposition could have been named America’s Cold War World’s Fair." (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325) Religion also shaped the fair. Christian Witness commissioned a pavilion opposite the Science Pavilion, and the Moody Bible Institute ran Sermons from Science (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 25).

Legacy for Seattle

After the fair the grounds became Seattle Center, which the city took over (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 328; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The US Science Pavilion became the Pacific Science Center (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Downtown benefited little from the new centre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 328), and the monorail remained an unreliable relic (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 329). Roche counts the Space Needle as permanent event heritage, as the Atomium is in Brussels (Roche 2000p. 132).

Seattle Center opened to the public on 1 June 1963. Buildings covered about thirty per cent of the 74 acres and the rest was landscaped, beside a 1,500-car garage (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481). Warren notes that the Gayway, renamed the Fun Forest, survived against all recommendations for fifty years (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481). It was dismantled in 2011 and replaced by the Experience Music Project and the Chihuly Garden and Glass museum (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 482). The monorail, 1.2 miles long, did not become the prototype of urban transit that some had hoped (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Fair America reports that the company formed to run the site after the fair failed, and that the city contracted its operation to Greater Seattle, Inc. (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The British pavilion became a branch of the Seattle Art Museum, and the Coliseum became home to the city's basketball and hockey franchises (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). In the mid-1980s the city again consulted Disney. Disney advised keeping the Space Needle and the Science Center but clearing other well-loved buildings. Residents and officials broke off the relationship before any work began (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 482).

How historians have interpreted it

Findlay, in the encyclopedia entry, reads the fair mainly as urban renewal shaped by downtown's decline and Boeing's dominance (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 323). Findlay argues that the cultural gains helped the metropolitan area more than downtown (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 328), and quotes one observer who called the fair "a thinking man’s world’s fair" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 326).

Warren reads the fair through Disney. Warren argues that planners used Disney's management skill to build a suburban-style image of urban life that would replace the blight of American inner cities (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 476). Warren calls the Seattle Center "a Disney-esque interpretation of urban space" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 481). Warren also argues that the World of Century 21, unlike the fair's first two worlds, introduced a thread of cynicism (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479).

Leslie and Mercelis read Century 21 as America's answer to Sputnik. With the Soviets absent, they argue, the US had an unopposed showcase for its space programme. One young visitor, quoted by the authors, said: "It was as if a spaceship from a friendly future had landed in our own backyard." (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 22) Skee argues that the consultants' attendance estimates shaped planning, and that the fair showed smaller BIE-sanctioned fairs could renew cities without a universal fair's extravagance (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 62).

Samuel uses Seattle as the yardstick for New York. Seattle had official BIE recognition (Samuel 2007), and its success led New York's backers to see a market for global expositions (Samuel 2007). Allwood, Allan and Reid call the fair a jewel-box fair and argue that its BIE registration and federal funding set a standard later American fairs failed to meet (Allwood et al. 2001p. 129; Allwood et al. 2001p. 148). Mandell places the fair among US fairs dominated by corporate public relations (Mandell 1967p. 119).

The texts agree that Sputnik gave the fair its science theme, that the Space Needle became its emblem and that the site remained a civic asset (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 324; Allwood et al. 2001p. 131; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 328). They differ on the purpose. Findlay and Warren put urban renewal and downtown at the core. Leslie and Mercelis and Samuel put the Cold War there, and Skee puts the consultants there (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 323; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 22; Samuel 2007; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 60).

Benedict treats the Space Needle as an exception among fair structures. Most focal structures were torn down when a fair closed. The few that survived, the Space Needle among them, became symbols of their cities, and their original meaning was forgotten or recalled with difficulty (Benedict 1983p. 14). Roche, by contrast, counts the tower as permanent event heritage (Roche 2000p. 132).

Warren reads Disney's role as one of planning rather than design. No Disney-designed pavilion or attraction stood on the site, and Disney's staff worked behind the scenes (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 474). Walt Disney had made a cartoon for the 1939-40 New York fair and designed the Circarama for Brussels in 1958 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 476). Warren also records a May 1958 visit by the Seattle contingent to the Burbank studios (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 475).

Leslie and Mercelis end on a more hopeful note. The fair did not transform downtown as its boosters intended, but it marked Seattle's emergence as a high-tech hub. Gates and Allen visited it as children and remembered it (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 26).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say little about Asian presence at this fair. They say nothing about Asian visitors, commissioners or observers, and they do not say who paid for any Asian display.

Congress's rule against Communist governments that held Pacific Rim peoples in subjugation excluded North Korea, North Vietnam and the People's Republic of China (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 325). Foreign contributors were offered exhibit space rent-free and asked to show modern industry and commerce as well as traditional handicrafts and tourist attractions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 478).

The Republic of China's pavilion, which Warren identifies as Taiwan's, highlighted traditional textiles, ceramics and furniture behind an ornate Asian façade, a few feet from Britain's Hall of Science and Technology (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 478). Warren judges that the foreign pavilions had little architectural or thematic consistency, and that noticeable juxtapositions arose (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 478).

Japan had two presences. A Japanese Village in the Show Street area, described in the guidebook as a replica of a Japanese temple with 24 miniature shrines and a traditional garden, drew visitors who wanted calm as well as the thrills of rides and striptease shows (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 480). A second Japanese entry was also placed on Show Street, which Warren finds puzzling (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that many souvenirs bearing the Space Needle were made in Japan, which seemed to have cornered the 1962 souvenir market (Allwood et al. 2001p. 131). The US Science Pavilion's architect, Minoru Yamasaki, was a Japanese American whose family had been interned during the Second World War (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The Philippine government could not fully fund its Seattle pavilion and turned to private donors (Peterson 2020p. 169). Samuel notes that a Chun King's Chinese Inn had been a big hit at the Seattle fair, without saying who ran it (Samuel 2007).

Warren reads the foreign pavilions as a contrast with the fair's science worlds, which set Western progress against foreign displays presented as traditional and exotic (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 479).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Cold War and space race — After Sputnik the fair became a showcase for American science and space, and Congress excluded Communist states (Findling & Pelle 2008; Molella & Knowles 2019).
  • Urban renewal and downtown — Downtown interests saw the fair as a route to renewal, and the texts disagree on how far it helped downtown (Findling & Pelle 2008; Hollengreen et al. 2014).
  • Disney's influence — Disney staff advised on management, and Disney's landscaping shaped the grounds (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Samuel 2007).
  • Science and religion — The US Science Exhibit and religious pavilions set faith and science beside each other (Molella & Knowles 2019; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Midway and entertainment — The Gayway and Show Street drew most visitors, and their adult shows caused controversy (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Finance and recognition — BIE recognition and reported profit, which the texts give in conflicting amounts, shaped comparisons with New York (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001; Samuel 2007).

Questions for study

  1. Why did a fair planned as a regional celebration become a national Cold War display, and what did that shift cost its local aims?
  2. How far did the fair benefit downtown Seattle, and what evidence would settle the disagreement between the texts?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
11The Resumption of a Great Traditionp. 128

Allwood's final chapter covers the post-war revival of international exhibitions, starting with the Festival of Britain of 1951. Its South Bank site, the narrative display theme compiled by Ian Cox, the Dome of Discovery and the routing of crowds that gave rise to 'Townscape' are described. The chapter then turns to Brussels 1958, the first international exhibition after the war, centred on the Atomium and the Water Ladder, with the Philips pavilion by Le Corbusier and the American and Soviet pavilions. Italia 61 in Turin is discussed as an attempt to organize exhibitions around the theme of 'Man at Work', which the author judges a failure, though Nervi's Palazzo del Lavoro is valued. The Seattle Century 21 fair of 1962, with its Space Needle, Monorail and United States Science Pavilion, is the last case, and the chapter concludes that it left the city a ready civic centre and a profit. The argument is that exhibitions moved from subject-based halls towards thematic display and from national showcases towards science and civic legacy.

It closes by suggesting that, given more time, the office might have pioneered interactive multimedia.

8.3To Work and Play and Live in the Year 2000: Creating the Future at the 1962 Seattle World’s FairStacy Warrenp. 473

Warren argues that the 1962 Seattle fair, the Century 21 Exposition, was the boldest attempt yet to use a world's fair as a tool of urban redevelopment. Its planners cleared the old Warren neighborhood, demolishing 203 buildings, and designed a compact site of 74 acres as a suburban image of city life, with Disney supplying behind-the-scenes management advice rather than attractions. The chapter moves through the fair's five worlds. The World of Science, centered on Minoru Yamasaki's U.S. Science Exhibit and a film by Charles and Ray Eames, is the most fully described. Warren then sets the pessimistic World of Tomorrow, whose Threshold and Threat presentation mixed visions of Seattle in 2000 with images of atomic war, against the commercial pavilions, the Gayway midway and the adult attractions of Show Street. Attendance of 9.6 million and a small profit serve as the evidence of success. Warren ends by tracing how the site became Seattle Center, with its Fun Forest until 2011, and how a 1980s proposal to use Disney as urban consultants was rejected by residents.

The World’s Fair in the Era of the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of ProgressArthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowlesp. 3Asia

The introduction argues that world's fairs both mirrored and helped shape Cold War culture, and that the fairs of 1958 to 1970 were proxy fronts in the rivalry between the United States and the USSR over science and technology. Molella and Knowles open with Brussels 1958, where replicas of Sputnik drew attention, and with the American responses at Seattle in 1962 and New York in 1964-1965. They then set out the recurring themes of the volume: space and atomic energy, the communications revolution, modern architecture, and the anxieties that technological change produced. The Bureau International des Expositions and the United States' uneasy relationship with it receive attention, from Robert Moses's defiance in 1964 to the 1999 funding restrictions. The chapter ends by sketching the five parts of the book, which move from Brussels to North America, then to Asia and Australia, and finally to the end of the Cold War. For an undergraduate it is a clear map of the volume's scope and its global, interdisciplinary framing.

1Expo ’58: Nucleus for a New EuropeStuart W. Leslie and Joris Mercelisp. 11

Leslie and Mercelis examine Expo '58 in Brussels as a stage for Belgium's ambition to make the city the capital of a united Europe. They describe the Atomium, whose motto was 'Atom = Hope', and show that its nuclear exhibits, like those of the other national pavilions, promoted national programmes over collaboration. The European Coal and Steel Community pavilion is read as a public-relations effort that promoted integration through narratives of progress, while Euratom, which had no pavilion, is treated as the next step. The chapter then turns to urban change: the fair justified new ring roads, parking and office complexes, and it gave Belgian architects commissions that shaped the city. A further section contrasts the American pavilion, with its 'soft pitch', Stone's circular building and the fashion show, with the Soviet pavilion, whose Sputnik models drew the crowds. A brief epilogue moves on to Seattle 1962 and the Space Needle. The authors conclude that Brussels gained the European institutions, but that the fair's promise of supranational unity was only partly realised.

4Bringing the Fair to Town: Harrison “Buzz” Price and International Expositions in the United States after 1945James D. Skeep. 57

Skee follows Harrison 'Buzz' Price, the consultant whose firms Economics Research Associates and Harrison Price Company produced attendance estimates and feasibility studies for several American world's fairs. The chapter begins with Price's background, from wartime bombing surveys to SRI, and with Century 21 in Seattle, where his firm replaced an SRI estimate with a more cautious one based on comparable attractions such as zoos. Skee then turns to HemisFair '68 in San Antonio, where Price's team argued that Mexican American residents had discretionary income to spend, and to the Louisiana World Exposition of 1984, where the projection of 11 million visitors was not met, the city forced the fair into bankruptcy, and 7.3 million attended. Skee argues that Price's quantitative, systems-based methods belonged to a Cold War consensus among American advisers, and that the disclaimers Price adopted after New Orleans turned his forecasts into assumptions rather than promises. The coda ties this to the later spread of data analytics.

Wikipedia

The Century 21 Exposition (also known as the Seattle World's Fair) was a world's fair held April 21, 1962, to October 21, 1962, in Seattle, Washington, United States. Nearly 10 million people attended the fair during its six-month run. As planned, the exposition left behind a fairground and numerous public buildings and public works; some credit it with revitalizing Seattle's economic and cultural life (see History of Seattle (1940–present)). The fair saw the construction of the Space Needle and Alweg monorail, as well as several sports venues (Washington State Coliseum, now Climate Pledge Arena) and performing arts buildings (the Playhouse, now the Cornish Playhouse), most of which have since been replaced or heavily remodeled. Unlike some other world's fairs of its era, Century 21 made a profit.
