Nanyang Industrial Exposition

南洋勸業會 (Nanyang quanye hui)
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Nanyang Industrial Exposition (南洋勸業會, Nanyang quanye hui) was China's first national exposition. It was held at Jiangning Park in Nanjing from 5 June to 29 November 1910 (Hurp. 124; Hurp. 135). The Manchu governor-general Duanfang proposed it in 1908 (Hurp. 125), and Chen Qi was its managing director (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178). Hur argues that it was a brief moment when the Qing court and constitutional reformers seemed to agree (Hurp. 162; Hurp. 166).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Nanyang in the title did not mean Southeast Asia. Hur notes that the name covered the coastal provinces of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian and Guangdong, although many people assumed it meant Southeast Asia (Hurp. 127). The idea of a national fair predates Duanfang. In 1904 the overseas Chinese merchant Zhang Zhenxun proposed one to the Empress Dowager (Hurp. 125). In 1908 Chen Qi and Yan Qizhang proposed a site of about 600 mu around Jiangning Park (Hurp. 125). Duanfang took up the plan and submitted a petition on 7 December 1908 (Hurp. 126). Two reference entries give the date of Tuan Fang's proposal as 15 December 1908 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 209; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 213), so the sources differ on the date.

Hur reports Duanfang's argument that national wealth and power came from industry, which competition would develop (Hurp. 127). Duanfang's regulations followed the domestic encouragement-of-industry exhibitions of Japan (Hurp. 129). Fernsebner describes the organisers as presenting the fair as a survey of the nation's goods, for domestic use and for export promotion (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178).

Organisers and finance

Duanfang was minister-superintendent of trade in the South, and his office was in Nanjing, which made the city a convenient venue (Hurp. 127). His office was staffed by forty people, with Chen Qi as secretary and Xiang Ruikun as assistant manager (Hurp. 128). Hur says Chen later became managing director (Hurp. 126). Fernsebner gives Chen Qi as managing director and Xiang Ruikun as managing vice-director (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178), and Peterson also names Chen as managing director (Peterson 2020p. 84).

The budget rose from 500,000 to 1,000,000 taels, about 600,000 USD (Hurp. 135). A note gives 265,000 USD from the throne and 144,500 USD from local chambers of commerce (Hurp. 135). Fernsebner calls the fair a joint undertaking of state and private sponsors (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178).

Duanfang moved to the north in June 1909, and Zhang Renjun succeeded him as president, with less enthusiasm (Hurp. 131). The two reference entries name Tuan Fang as governor-general and Chang Jen-chun as the viceroy who opened the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 213; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210). Their officials lists give Chang Jen-chun as successor to Tuan Fang, with H. E. Chin Chi as director general and Yang Shih-chi as adjudicator general, and they do not list Chen Qi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 389).

Site and buildings

The sources disagree on the site. Hur gives 1,000 mu, about 165 acres (Hurp. 135). The two reference entries give 90 to 100 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 213). One entry places the site on the Yangtze near the Nanking railway station, beside a park with about fifty lakes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210), and says ground was broken in July 1909 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210).

The building counts also differ. Hur says the organising committee ran fifteen buildings, designed by the Shanghai firm Atkinson and Dallas (Hurp. 135), and he counts over thirty pavilions in all, including a four-storey monumental tower with a lift (Hurp. 136). The reference entries count 26 buildings and pavilions, all white except one red-brick house (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 213; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210). The entrance was a palace-style gate facing south, and the exit was a European-style gate facing west (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 214). Hur adds an entertainment quarter called Chibizhiyule, with a Russian circus and a zoo (Hurp. 136).

The opening was late. Hur says rain slowed the building and that many provincial exhibits had not arrived. The opening, planned for the first day of the fourth lunar month, was held on the 28th day, 5 June (Hurp. 135). Hur says some pavilions were not complete until a month after their opening ceremonies (Hurp. 137). The entry in the dictionary says some buildings were unfinished until September (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 213).

Security and the opening

The fair was planned amid alarm. The New York Times warned of unrest in Nanjing (Hurp. 132). The American minister had a warship ready, foreign warships stood by, and the Qing sent warships and troops (Hurp. 133). Inns were required to submit guest books to the police, and the government imported rice and set up charity granaries (Hurp. 138).

Hur quotes the American explorer William E. Geil, who wrote that the government's precautions were useless: "There was no violence at all." (Hurp. 137). Hur argues that the calm was the result of the Qing's precautions (Hurp. 137). One reference entry also describes the opening as peaceful (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210).

Who took part

Hur says 22 provinces built pavilions (Hurp. 136), while one reference entry says 15 Chinese provinces took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210). The same entry says 14 foreign nations and 78 private companies or agencies took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210). Hur reports three Foreign Samples Buildings (Hurp. 139), and elsewhere says that the four nations housed in them were the United States, Britain, Germany and Japan (Hurp. 144).

Students and farmers also came. Some counties sent farmers who could read, and Lu Xun led more than two hundred students from his school (Hurp. 158).

Visitors and reception

By mid-October about 300,000 Chinese and 5,000 foreign visitors had come, and daily numbers were often above 10,000 (Hurp. 139). One reference entry says attendance was lower than estimated, and blames adverse publicity about unrest: "affect the attendance figures. It did not." (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210). The dictionary entry says the fair did not make a profit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 214). Admission was criticised as dear: Hur quotes a Japanese paper's comparison of 30 sen with 10 sen in Japan, and reports that the ordinary ticket was reduced from 3 jiao to 1 jiao (Hurp. 138).

Most Chinese newspapers were descriptive or positive (Hurp. 150). Shenbao was the exception, and criticised the fountain, the pavilions, the entertainment and the awards (Hurp. 151). Hur explains the restraint by the constitutional movement: Shenbao did not need to provoke a government that was moving towards constitutional rule (Hurp. 153).

Politics and controversy

Hur calls the fair "a splendid but momentary display" of a China moving towards constitutional government (Hurp. 162). Zhang Jian organised a cooperative association for the fair, and a research society of 790 members studied the exhibits (Hurp. 154). In 1910 the Qing had agreed to a parliament and a cabinet (Hurp. 165). The cabinet formed in spring 1911, with many Manchu princes, disappointed the reformers (Hurp. 166).

Fernsebner gives a different emphasis. Her organisers aimed to train a new citizenry through the displays, and Xiang Ruikun advocated "education in material things" for that purpose (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 183).

Its place in the series

Hur treats the fair as an extension of the Osaka exhibition of 1903 and the St Louis exposition of 1904 (Hurp. 125). Duanfang had sent a collection of curios to Osaka (Hurp. 55). Chen Qi and Chen Huide had written a critical book on the St Louis displays (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 176). Peterson says Chen's criticism of St Louis led to a new method of collecting goods through inspectors, which he used at Nanjing (Peterson 2020p. 100). Zhang Yuanji proposed ten years of preparation and a permanent showroom based on the Nanyang products (Hurp. 157). Chen Qi later led the Chinese contingent at San Francisco in 1915 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 184). Peterson mentions a national fair held in Nanjing in 1911, but he does not connect it with the 1910 exposition (Peterson 2020p. 82).

How historians have interpreted it

Hur reviews earlier scholarship. Wang Xiang saw the fair as the entry of China into the early stage of capitalism in industry and commerce (Hurp. 124). Nozawa Yutaka read it as an expression of the growing bourgeoisie's support for capitalist development (Hurp. 124). Michael Godley, Hur says, examined the relations among the groups that prepared the fair (Hurp. 124). Hur's own reading is that the fair marked a reconciliation between the court and constitutionalists (Hurp. 165). Fernsebner reads it as economic nationalism (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178). The reference entries reach a more measured verdict: the fair was not a failure in all regards (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Japanese visitors and exhibits

Japan supplied most of the foreign visitors. Hur says the majority of foreign visitors were Japanese, many of them travelling on discounted fares offered by the NYK line (Hurp. 140). A group of twelve businessmen from Osaka, Yokohama, Tokyo, Kobe, Kyoto and Nagoya visited, and Japanese newspapers sent reporters (Hurp. 140). Japanese exhibits in the Foreign Samples Building were mainly export goods such as cotton cloth, sugar and seafood, together with army and navy displays (Hurp. 139).

Japan asked to be treated as a Chinese exhibitor rather than a foreign nation, and the United States led the protest against this (Hurp. 144). Japanese exhibitors hung Rising Sun flags, which the fair made them take down, and the United States, Britain and Germany then agreed to use the fair's official flags (Hurp. 144).

Taiwan, Manchuria and Southeast Asia

Hur says that Taiwanese exhibits had been offensive to China at Osaka and St Louis (Hurp. 148). A Japanese-language Taiwan journal urged Taiwanese merchants to send goods to Nanjing, but Hur found no evidence that they were shown and says that further study is required (Hurp. 148).

Hur lists a pavilion of the combined provinces of Dongsanxing, Shanshan and Yungui, without saying what it displayed (Hurp. 136).

Overseas Chinese from Southeast Asia took an active part in the fair. Zhang Yu'nan gave 100,000 taels, and Lian Bingnong bought the first ticket for 10,000 taels (Hurp. 159). Their hall held Batavian objects beside Chinese commercial goods (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 180), and Fernsebner reads the display of Southeast Asian native goods as a claim to a hierarchy of civilisation (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 188). When Xu Boxing, an overseas Chinese merchant, died in Nanjing, the office held a memorial service (Hurp. 160).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Constitutional reconciliation — Hur argues that the press did not attack the court because the court had promised constitutional government (Hurp. 162).
  • Economic nationalism and training citizens — Fernsebner shows the organisers using the fair to promote export industry and to train a disciplined population (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179).
  • Overseas Chinese — Southeast Asian merchants financed a hall, bought tickets and held a memorial in Nanjing (Hurp. 159; Hurp. 160).
  • Japan and the foreign pavilions — Japan's demands for status and its flag dispute show rivalry inside the fair (Hurp. 144).
  • Public order — fears of unrest led to police checks, troops and foreign warships (Hurp. 132).
  • A contested record — the texts give different site sizes, building counts and officials (Hurp. 135; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210).

Questions for study

  1. Why did Shenbao criticise the fair's quality but not the government, and what does this show about the press in 1910? (Hurp. 152)
  2. Hur stresses constitutional reconciliation and Fernsebner stresses economic nationalism. Can both explain the fair, and where do their accounts diverge? (Hurp. 165; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 178)
  3. Why did Japanese visitors and goods attract both welcome and suspicion? (Hurp. 141; Hurp. 141)
  4. How should a reader weigh the conflicting figures for site size and buildings when no source settles them? (Hurp. 135; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 210)

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
FourThe Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, the Last Show of the Qing Dynastyp. 124Asia

Hur turns to the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, held in Nanjing from June to November 1910, the first national exposition hosted by China and the last of the Qing dynasty. The chapter traces the project to Duanfang, the Manchu governor-general who proposed it in 1908 and modeled its regulations on Japan's domestic exhibitions, and to his successor Zhang Renjun after Duanfang's transfer in 1909. Hur describes the preparations, including a site of about 165 acres, pavilions for twenty-two provinces, foreign samples buildings, and the security measures taken amid fears of unrest in Nanjing. The main argument concerns the Chinese press, which, unlike its coverage of Osaka and St. Louis, was mostly descriptive or positive, a shift Hur ties to the constitutional movement and to newspapers and organizers who backed it. The chapter also covers the large Japanese visiting groups and the overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia. It concludes that the exposition was a momentary display of an integrated, constitutional China that faded with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

In the contents of this work

Conclusionp. 163Asia

Hur concludes the dissertation by arguing that the Qing government saw world exhibitions as a diplomatic arena in which to present China as a modern state, while Chinese reformers and revolutionaries used the same displays to attack the court. Osaka, St. Louis and Nanjing are recalled as cases in which Chinese exhibits were exaggerated, distorted or ignored by the press. The chapter notes that 'national humiliation' rhetoric, which had targeted foreign powers, was turned inward against the Qing. Hur then argues that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories that treat exhibitions either as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist, and calls for audience-oriented approaches. The conclusion ends with comparisons between Chinese and Japanese exhibition traditions, and with a note on the transnational Chinese press and a late Qing public sphere that extended beyond the national boundary.

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Introduction: communities real and imagined: world’s fairs and political meaningsDavid Raizman and Ethan Robeyp. 1

Raizman and Robey introduce the volume by asking how the world's fairs of 1851 to 1915 used manufactured goods to express national identity. They argue that earlier design history treated fairs as a foil to modernism, and that the fairs were networks of exchange rather than single, unified displays. Pevsner's dismissal of the Crystal Palace is challenged by later critics, from Banham and Sparke to Greenhalgh, Bennett and Mitchell, the last three reading the fairs as imperial spectacle. The editors use Anderson's 'imagined communities' to tie national identity to material culture. Their evidence is the Albert Memorial, Owen Jones's interior and Grammar of Ornament, and the contributors' cases: the 1888 Copenhagen Nordic Exhibition, the Nanjing and South American centenaries of 1910, Italy at Brussels and Antwerp, and the Finnish pavilion at Paris in 1900. They end with the outbreak of World War I, when the ideal of 'peaceful competition' broke down.

9When the local is the global: case studies in early twentieth-century Chinese exposition projectsSusan R. Fernsebnerp. 173Asia

Fernsebner follows Chinese exposition managers, mostly state officials and commercial elites, through three fairs: St. Louis in 1904, China's first national exposition at Nanjing in 1910, and the Panama-Pacific fair in San Francisco in 1915. Her argument is that these elites sought control over how China was represented. At St. Louis they protested against displays arranged by the Western-staffed Qing Customs Service, including bound-foot shoes and opium pipes. At Nanjing they used the fair to promote economic nationalism and to train a 'new people', drawing on provincial displays of soap, furniture and textiles and on a hall for overseas Chinese. The San Francisco section centres on a complaint about the China Pavilion's teahouse and on 'Underground Chinatown', an attraction that the Six Companies and commissioner-general Chen Qi protested and that closed in March 1915. The chapter concludes that these disputes joined local, transnational and global concerns, and that the same dynamics recur in China's later hosting of Expo 2010.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
6Aftermath of the Exhibitionp. 153Asia

Hotta-Lister asks whether the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London was, as Japanese opinion held, a failure, and tests its effects in economic, financial, diplomatic and racial terms. On trade, the author sets Japanese and British statistics beside the exhibitors' sales figures to show that Japanese exports to Britain fell overall in 1910 and 1911, while matting, raw silk, shell buttons and toys rose, and treats the Exhibition as one plausible contributor. Loans get the same caution: the government's conversion loans and the municipal and South Manchurian Railway issues were raised largely through the usual banks, so the Exhibition at most reinforced existing confidence. The chapter then follows the tariff dispute, the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in July 1911, drawing heavily on British press reactions. Its racial discussion centres on the Official Guide's claims of similarity between Japanese and British people and on Japanese complaints about Chinese and Ainu displays. It concludes that the Exhibition had only a limited effect, though it was not the failure that Japanese critics claimed.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
AbstractAsia

Hur's abstract summarises a dissertation on the Qing government's attempt to stage modern statehood at world exhibitions and how that attempt was undermined. The Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan and Chinese reformers each shaped China's image, and criticism of the Qing grew after Osaka in 1903 and St Louis in 1904. It ends with the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which Chinese intellectuals came to support once the government promised constitutional reform.

In the contents of this work

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. About the work as a whole Asia: central

Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. Each case is a chapter, organised by fair, using archival and ethnographic methods. The conclusion argues that state-directed national representation has declined as the form became costly for host cities, and that Asia has become the leading host of expositions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are the whole subject of this work, and their coverage is central. Japan is the main case in chapters 2 and 4 (the government site, 'Japan Beautiful' concession and fine arts galleries at San Francisco 1915; the pavilion and Takarazuka Revue at New York 1939-40). China fills chapter 3 (the official site and the Chinese Village at San Francisco 1915, with Chinatown's role) and chapter 5 (Expo '88 in Brisbane). The Philippines (chapter 6) and Indonesia (chapter 7) are studied through their New York 1964-65 pavilions, South Korea (chapter 8) and Thailand (chapter 9) through Milan 2015, and chapter 10 treats the Chinese Milan pavilion and Asia's rise as host. Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Nanyang Industrial Exposition, more commonly known as the 1910 Nanking or Nanjing Exposition, was a world's fair held in Nanjing, China, during the end of the Qing dynasty. It opened on 5 June 1910 and lasted 177 days.
