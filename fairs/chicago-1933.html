Chicago · 1933–1934

A Century of Progress International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Century of Progress International Exposition ran in Chicago from 27 May 1933 to 31 October 1934, on a lakefront site of reclaimed land (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 273; Allwood et al. 2001p. 109). Its organisers raised private money, built the fair around science and industry, and made a profit that few American world's fairs of the period achieved (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213; Allwood et al. 2001p. 109). Historians use it to study how a Depression-era fair sold progress, corporate products and national identity, and whose progress it left out (Rydell 1993p. 11; Ganz 2008). It also became the model for the New York fair of 1939-40 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 60).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Planning began in the mid-1920s. Mayor Dever called a planning meeting on 8 April 1926, a board formed in January 1928 under Rufus G. Dawes, and Lenox R. Lohr became general manager in April 1929 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213). Charles Dawes arrived in Chicago in May 1929 to raise ten million dollars from local sources (Ganz 2008). The name was a compromise. Ganz says sceptical backers asked for a change, and the organisers then made progress the theme after Black Tuesday, the 1929 stock-market crash (Ganz 2008). The motto was "Science Finds, Industry Applies, Man Conforms." (Ganz 2008; Samuel 2007). Ganz argues that this idea of progress made science and technology its prime movers, a vision that did not match the experience of women, African Americans and ethnic groups, who fought for space and a voice at the fair (Ganz 2008).

Organisation and finance

Congress endorsed the fair in February 1929 on condition that sponsors raise $5 million; Rydell reports that by November the organisers had secured $12,176,000 for the principal of Guaranteed Gold Notes, a form of guaranteed debt, with Julius Rosenwald among the leading subscribers (Rydell 1993p. 119). Other texts give a smaller figure. The Chicago World's Fair Legion raised $634,000 from April 1928, and a $10 million bond issue, secured on 40 percent of gate receipts, was announced a day before the 1929 crash (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 213; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 266). The federal government paid $1 million for its own pavilion, and eighteen states paid about another $1 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214).

Ganz traces the cooperative, thematic halls to the 1928 advice of Ernest T. Trigg, who had chaired Philadelphia's 1926 Sesqui-Centennial (Ganz 2008). The organisers repudiated the jury system and awards, so the fair gave no prizes (Giberti 2002). Rydell says the fair offered free space to corporations: twenty built pavilions in 1933, against nine at the 1893 Chicago fair (Rydell 1993p. 122).

The 1933 season failed to bring in enough money, so the board reopened the fair in 1934 mainly to pay bondholders, the people who had bought its bonds, in full (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 275; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 272). Roosevelt visited late in 1933 and suggested a second season (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Samuel adds that Roosevelt backed the fair as economic and psychological recovery (Samuel 2007). The final accounts showed a surplus of $688,165 and a final profit of $160,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 276). Findling records that observers praised the careful financial planning (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 270). The cost figures disagree: Badger's table gives $42,900,989 (Badger 1979p. 131), while Gelernter and Samuel describe an investment of $47 million (Gelernter 1995p. 340; Samuel 2007).

Site, architecture and exhibits

The site was a strip of 427 acres, about three miles long and seldom wider than a quarter mile, from Twelfth to Thirty-ninth Street (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 214). Maloney describes an artificial site of 800 acres in Lake Michigan (Maloney 2012p. 157), and Badger's table gives 424 acres (Badger 1979p. 131).

The Architectural Commission included Hubert Burnham, son of Daniel H. Burnham, Harvey Wiley Corbett, Paul Cret, Raymond Hood and Ralph T. Walker, and Joseph Urban was director of colour (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 270). The architects rejected neoclassical design for flat, windowless walls and cheap materials, and Urban's scheme used twenty-three colours (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The whole fair had to be painted in under three months in bad weather, and casein paint, a milk-protein paint, solved the problem of Urban's strong colours (Allwood et al. 2001p. 109). Benedict argues that the fair turned from the formal, classical layouts of earlier fairs, the beaux-arts style, to modernistic architecture (Benedict 1983p. 111). Findling says the bold buildings carried modernism into 1930s industrial design, though many architects had serious reservations about their style (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 273).

Exhibits stressed processes in action rather than static machines, and blue-ribbon competition was dropped (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 272). The Hall of Science was the main building, with exhibits on mathematics, physics, chemistry and the biological sciences (Benedict 1983p. 34). Corporate buildings were among the main attractions, including General Motors, Chrysler and a Sinclair pavilion with animated dinosaurs (Benedict 1983p. 25). The art exhibits ran under an agreement of June 1932, and just under three million people paid 25 cents to see them (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270).

Nations, colonies and peoples on display

Rydell argues that after touring the 1931 Paris colonial exposition, the organisers urged colonial villages and native bazaars for Chicago (Rydell 1993p. 82). The midway had an African village concession called Darkest Africa, run by Louis DuFour, a Native American village and a large Mayan Temple, which Rydell calls the fair's answer to the Angkor Wat reproduction at Paris (Rydell 1993p. 83p. 84). Foreign participation was run from London under Sir Henry Cole (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270). Belgium's model village was so successful in 1933 that other nations built villages for 1934, and China, Czechoslovakia, Italy, Japan, Sweden and the Ukraine had their own pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 272; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270). Greenhalgh says the number of foreign peoples on show was drastically reduced, and names only Moroccan, Chinese and Belgian villages among the foreign ones (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105).

The midway's curiosities included midget villages and a two-headed baby (Benedict 1983p. 57). A General Exhibits booth used a woman, Anna Robinson, as Aunt Jemima (Ganz 2008). The replica cabin of Jean Baptiste Point du Sable, the first settler of the Chicago area, drew mild protests against racial discrimination (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 272; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270). About 75 African Americans worked on the grounds in 1933 and about 300 in 1934, mostly as toilet cleaners (Rydell 1993p. 166).

Ganz describes the Committee on Nationalities, led by Major Felix Streyckmans, which favoured acculturation over total assimilation, and records that Mayor Anton Cermak urged each group to take part (Ganz 2008).

Visitors and reception

The reference works give about 39 million paid admissions for the two seasons. The 1933 season recorded 22,320,456 visitors and 22,565,859 ticket sales, and 1934 ticket sales were 16,486,377 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 272; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 276). These sales add up to 39,052,236, the figure in Badger's table and in Samuel's comparison (Badger 1979p. 131; Samuel 2007). Other texts give more. Rydell, Findling and Pelle say some 48 million came over the two seasons (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Ganz's introduction gives more than 39 million paid, and the epilogue reports that organisers claimed nearly fifty million (Ganz 2008).

The Ford pavilion of 1934 drew 70 percent of daily visitors, according to the dictionary entry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 272); Schrenk gives a figure above 75 percent (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 184).

Black visitors met a fair with little room for them. Rydell, Findling and Pelle write that "Virtually absent from the Century of Progress were African Americans" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Negro Day in August 1933 drew much smaller crowds than expected (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000); Roche says Philip Randolph organised it (Roche 2000p. 86). Claude Barnett, editor of the Chicago Defender, first promoted the fair, but his correspondents grew disillusioned with how it handled race (Rydell 1993p. 165). In 1934 the Illinois legislature passed a measure requiring full and equal enjoyment of the fair by all persons (Rydell 1993p. 170; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Ganz says the fair had no Woman's Building and that organisers rejected exhibit lists built around women's daily lives (Ganz 2008). Mary Beard told a meeting that the fair gave a fuller history of man than of women (Ganz 2008). Weimann records that the International Conclave of Women Writers showed 2,000 books by women (Weimann 1981p. 392), and that the National Council of Women's display included a sixty-foot mural of women's history (Weimann 1981p. 598).

The midway drew much comment. Ganz says the organisers at first looked the other way at its sexual display, because they needed its revenue, and that Sally Rand's naked entrance on horseback at the May 1933 opening party won her a lucrative place (Ganz 2008). Rand was fined $25 in August 1933, and later fined $200 and sentenced to a year in jail, but the case was dismissed on appeal (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 271).

Politics and controversy

The fair was also a stage for national claims. Shepherdson-Scott says the small Manchuria pavilion on the Japanese grounds bore immense political weight (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539). The American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol undercut Japanese claims of dominance in the region (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539).

The German-American Group invited the Graf Zeppelin, and the Zeppelin Company agreed to fly if the Post Office issued a special stamp; the group bought 2,000 of the stamps in the Depression (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 145). On the airship's visit of 26 October 1933, the starboard tricolour was shown, not the swastika-marked port side, by Eckener's choice. The display became the fair's most powerful and divisive emblem of national identity (Ganz 2008).

Corporate rivalry also shaped the displays. Ford stayed away in 1933 after General Motors announced a working assembly line (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 183; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The General Motors pavilion let spectators watch Chevrolets assembled, and the corporation cancelled Diego Rivera's mural commission over the Lenin figure in an earlier mural (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 184).

Women's space at the fair

Ganz argues that the fair never gave women a collective building, and that the nearest attempt failed through rivalry among women over status (Ganz 2008). Dorthea Goodrich, who had been known as Dolly Ledgerwood Matters, proposed a Temple of Womanhood with a domed convention hall, and Daniel H. Burnham's office drew its plans (Ganz 2008). The design had a separate entrance and separate facilities for African American women, which Ganz sets against Goodrich's promise of a temple without ethnic or racial boundaries (Ganz 2008). Fair officials offered space as a concession, subject to proof of finance within sixty days (Ganz 2008). Lenox Lohr withdrew approval the day before the clubwomen's meeting of 8 June 1932, after an anonymous tip about her past had reached Martha McGrew (Ganz 2008). The fair later paid Goodrich $1,200 for breach of contract (Ganz 2008).

The sharpest conflict was between the fair's official hostess and its administrator. Helen Dawes chaired the Social Committee, and in March 1934 she wrote to Martha McGrew, Lohr's assistant, demanding respect (Ganz 2008). Her husband's view was no kinder: Rufus Dawes wrote to his brother that "the female of the species is rather more belligerent than the male" (Ganz 2008). Ganz concludes that professional women such as McGrew and Helen Bennett accepted the male-led version of progress, so no collective women's space emerged inside the grounds (Ganz 2008). Bennett told the fair trustee Janet Fairbank that the council's exhibit was not a women's exhibit at all (Ganz 2008). The National Council of Women's sixty-foot mural left out working-class women and women of color (Ganz 2008). The Chicago Woman's Club building at 72 East Eleventh Street, just outside the main gate, was the one women's space that clubwomen controlled (Ganz 2008).

Du Sable cabin and the clubwomen

Annie Oliver's National De Saible Memorial Society was founded on the evening of 3 April 1928, after a talk by Robert S. Abbott of the Chicago Defender (Ganz 2008). Alderman Robert R. Jackson proposed a city appropriation of $20,000 for a Du Sable memorial, and city council support covered the cabin's $1,500 erection cost (Ganz 2008). Fair officials refused the site, first through the head of concessions and then, after Mary McDowell's inquiry in 1932, on four grounds: the cabin copied the Fort Dearborn reproduction, was not distinctive, was of doubtful value and lacked popular appeal (Ganz 2008). A concession was granted four months before the opening, and an official called the cabin "one of the few distinct contributions of the negro race to the Exposition" (Ganz 2008).

The replica measured 8 by 12 feet and was roofed with corn and wheat stalks (Ganz 2008). The booklet's cover, by Charles C. Dawson, shows Du Sable surveying his homestead with the Wrigley Building and Tribune Tower behind him (Ganz 2008).

Ganz gives the paid attendance at Negro Day, 12 August 1933, as about four thousand, against an estimated fifty thousand at the Bud Billiken parade a week later (Ganz 2008). He names Chandler Owen as chair of the National Negro Day Committee, with A. Philip Randolph as one of its members (Ganz 2008). This goes against the attribution of the day to Randolph given above (Ganz 2008).

Horticulture and the florists' dispute

Maloney describes a 1932 fight over the Horticultural Building, where Kate Brewster's committee, formed in May 1932, opposed the commercial florists (Maloney 2012p. 161). The committee called the florists' concession a speculative commercial enterprise whose main interest was making money (Maloney 2012p. 161). Brewster asked for the committee to be disbanded, and the Executive Committee accepted the resignations in October 1932 (Maloney 2012p. 161p. 163). The fair then dropped the Soldier Field plan and broke ground that autumn on a 91,000-square-foot building with about twenty-five garden tableaux over five acres (Maloney 2012p. 163). Maloney argues that the small themed gardens transformed fair horticulture because they were designed for backyard gardeners (Maloney 2012p. 164).

The displays were large. The American Gladiolus Society showed more than 375 varieties from 200,000 corms (Maloney 2012p. 163). Inter-State Nurseries of Hamburg, Iowa, was the sole sponsor of the rose garden, which grew from more than 6,000 bushes of 104 varieties in 1933 to 15,000 bushes of 300 varieties in 1934 (Maloney 2012p. 165). Maloney's figure of 800 acres was the plan of March 1928, and the finished grounds covered just over 425 acres (Maloney 2012p. 157).

The midway and the courts

Ganz argues that the courts sent the problem of midway nudity back to the fair and the police, while the fair profited from it (Ganz 2008). In July 1933 Superior Court Judge Joseph B. David refused to stop total nudity at a suit brought by the lawyer Mary Belle Spencer, and said that the human body was a beautiful thing (Ganz 2008). On 31 July 1933 Mayor Edward J. Kelly and State's Attorney Thomas J. Courtney toured the midway and ordered the Oriental Village, Streets of Paris and Old Mexico shows cleaned up or closed (Ganz 2008).

The money explains the reluctance. Entrance fees, food and liquor at the Streets of Paris reached $100,000 a day, and its peep shows took up to $500 a day (Ganz 2008). Jessie E. Binford concluded that "the way to make money is to show a naked woman" (Ganz 2008). In 1934 nudity returned, and the Life Class added a nude male model (Ganz 2008). On 7 July 1934 Judge John P. McGoorty refused to close the Olympia show, calling the displays a disgrace that the fair should profit from (Ganz 2008).

The stratosphere balloon

Ganz argues that the fair's stratosphere balloon was presented as an American achievement, although the Swiss physicist Auguste Piccard designed its gondola (Ganz 2008). Dow Chemical built the gondola in Dowmetal, Goodyear-Zeppelin made the envelope, and Union Carbide supplied the hydrogen and liquid oxygen (Ganz 2008).

Spectators gathered at Soldier Field after midnight on 5 August 1933, and the balloon came down in the rail yards a mile away when a valve rope caught in its pleats (Ganz 2008). Settle and Major Chester Fordney tried again from Akron on 20 November 1933, reached a height recognised by the Bureau of Standards, then came down in a swamp (Ganz 2008). In October 1934 Jeanette Ridlon Piccard reached 57,579 feet and became the first woman in the stratosphere, and Dow asked for its logo to be removed once it learned that a mother of three was aboard (Ganz 2008).

Film and Photo League protest

Leshne argues that the Workers Film and Photo League's two films and its pamphlet recover a resistance that later history largely obscured (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 371p. 376). The first title of Conditions in Chicago reads "Progress in Chicago is more than just a word," and the next one answers, And yet—evictions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 372).

Century of Progress opens with a Ripley's sign at the fair and mocks the sideshows, including the African Dips concession (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 375). Leshne says it ends with rare footage of the 1933 cotton strike in California (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 376). Police arrested William Twigg near a crowd of 800 in Hyde Park, and Louis Siminow was arrested for showing films without a licence in San Diego (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 373). The pamphlet Chicago on Parade, edited by Herman O. Duncan, a University of Chicago graduate student, answers the opening-day speeches of Rufus Dawes, Mayor Kelly, Bishop Stewart and Governor Horner (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 373). Its photographs show Hoovervilles, Rooseveltbergs and the Sky Ride tower seen over shanties (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 374). Leshne traces many of them to Conrad Friberg, who used the alias C. O. Nelson (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 373).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell argues that the century-of-progress expositions were "theaters of power" (Rydell 1993p. 11). In this view the fairs tried to restore faith in business, government and science during the Depression, and offered a corporation-led, consumer-centred and racially exclusive vision of America's future. Rydell stresses the alliance between scientists and corporate sponsors, and the way scientific exhibits served the modern corporation (Rydell 1993p. 104).

Ganz reads the fair as a privately financed, civil-military enterprise that used optimism about science and technology to answer the Depression, a new deal for progress (Ganz 2008). Ganz argues that this vision did not fit the experience of women and minority groups (Ganz 2008). In a separate essay in the volume Meet Me at the Fair, Ganz reads fair souvenirs as expressions of ethnic and racial pride (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 146).

Findling's encyclopaedia entries present the fair as a success "by most standards" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 273). Findling stresses careful finance and the influence of the architecture on later design, and notes that the fair had received relatively little historical research (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood presents it as one of the few such events of the period to make a profit, and says the organisers' refusal to cancel kept it, and its jobs, going during the Depression (Allwood et al. 2001p. 109).

Other authors shift the emphasis. Schrenk sees corporate display moving from explaining production to reinforcing brand recognition through rides and entertainment (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 181). Samuel argues that the 1930s fairs, Chicago's included, carried consumerist ideas, and that thrift was presented as against the nation's long-term interest (Samuel 2007). Maloney reads the gardens as a midwestern form of modernism, and shows the horticultural exhibit pulled between commerce and education (Maloney 2012p. 157p. 161). Roche argues that expositions tended to promote racism and cultural exclusion, and cites Chicago's Negro Day and employment struggles (Roche 2000p. 86). Kargon and colleagues report that Lewis Mumford argued in 1935 that the Chicago fairs looked back over past achievement rather than towards planned social progress (Kargon et al. 2015p. 61).

The sources agree on the commercial character of the fair and on the exclusion of African Americans. They differ on what the fair means. Rydell and Ganz read it as an ideological project about power and progress, while Findling and Allwood treat its finances and success as the central story. Benedict and Maloney locate its importance in design, and Samuel and Schrenk in consumer and corporate display, while Roche stresses exclusion.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan and Manchuria

Japan had the largest Asian presence that the texts describe. Shepherdson-Scott says the Japanese government first announced it would not take part, then changed its mind after learning that the Chinese government was organising an exhibit (Shepherdson-Scottp. 544). The Manchukuo government paid 155,000 yen and the South Manchuria Railway 95,000 yen, a total of about $75,000 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545). The pavilion opened on 10 June 1933. At about 1,800 square feet it was small, and it averaged about 5,000 visitors a day, roughly 2 percent of the spectators (Shepherdson-Scottp. 541). The Foreign Ministry feared the Manchuria label, so the exhibit was annexed to the Japan pavilion as the South Manchuria Railway Building (Shepherdson-Scottp. 545).

Its displays presented development as already achieved, with a diorama of an affluent Manchurian family and a relief map of Manchuria and Korea that included Jehol (Shepherdson-Scottp. 548p. 552). Shepherdson-Scott says the official literature did not use the name Manchukuo, and that the pavilion was largely absent from the fair's official photographs and view book (Shepherdson-Scottp. 561). Benedict records a pearl model of Mount Vernon, costing half a million dollars, shown by Japan (Benedict 1983p. 18). Maloney notes that Japanese buildings from the 1933 fair were sited on the Wooded Island, and that a Japanese teahouse was moved there (Maloney 2012p. 78p. 169).

China

Peterson, who treats Chicago briefly, says China had a significant presence there, though Japan largely defined how Asia was represented (Peterson 2020p. 118). Findling says China had its own pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 272; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270). Greenhalgh names a Chinese village among the few foreign villages of 1933 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105).

The Golden Temple of Jehol, the summer residence of Manchurian emperors, was reproduced in 28,000 pieces in China and sent to Chicago for assembly (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 272; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 270). Shepherdson-Scott describes it as 65 feet long, built by about eighty Chinese craftsmen, sponsored by the trustee Vincent Bendix, and standing where the Japan and China areas met (Shepherdson-Scottp. 557). The texts do not say who organised or paid for China's own pavilion. The Chinese Lama Temple later went to New York (Cotter 2009).

South and Southeast Asia

The texts record no South or Southeast Asian display at this fair. Rydell notes that the Mayan Temple was the fair's answer to the Angkor Wat reproduction at the 1931 Paris colonial exposition (Rydell 1993p. 83).

Visitors and reception

The texts say little about Asian visitors, commissioners or observers. Shepherdson-Scott concludes that the whitewashed vision of Manchuria did not persuade its American audience (Shepherdson-Scottp. 561).

How scholars read it

Shepherdson-Scott argues that the fairgrounds became a site of contested diplomacy, and that the idea of Manchuria shown there was not a single one (Shepherdson-Scottp. 562). Benedict reads the pearl model as the borrowing of prestigious symbols by a foreign exhibitor seeking trade (Benedict 1983p. 18).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Science as the fair's message — The motto and the Hall of Science presented science and industry as the drivers of progress, and Ganz argues that this vision left out women and minority groups (Ganz 2008; Samuel 2007; Ganz 2008).
  • Corporate pavilions — Companies built the fair's main attractions, and Schrenk sees the corporate displays turning into brand entertainment (Benedict 1983p. 25; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 181).
  • Race and exclusion — African Americans had few jobs and a small place on the grounds, and Negro Day drew small crowds (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 166; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Gender and women's exhibits — Weimann records women's displays that were often organised apart from men's exhibits, and organisers rejected lists built around women's lives (Weimann 1981p. 392; Ganz 2008).
  • Nations and colonial display — Chicago was criticised for having few international pavilions, and its colonial-style villages and Mayan Temple reflected the Paris colonial fair of 1931 (Cotter 2009; Rydell 1993p. 83).
  • Money and recovery — The fair was privately financed, reopened to repay bondholders, and made a small profit, a rare result in the Depression (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 275; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 276; Allwood et al. 2001p. 109).

Questions for study

  1. If the fair's message was that science and industry drive progress, whose experience did the texts show it leaving out, and how did the organisers' choices make that exclusion possible?
  2. Should a fair be judged by its balance sheet, as Findling and Allwood do, or by its ideology, as Rydell and Ganz do? What would each measure miss?
  3. How far did the Paris colonial exposition of 1931 shape Chicago's displays, and what does that imitation tell us about the transfer of ideas between fairs?
  4. Shepherdson-Scott treats the Manchuria pavilion as a diplomatic instrument. Did the fair's corporate and national displays serve diplomacy in the same way?
  5. Attendance and cost figures in the texts disagree widely. How should a historian handle conflicting numbers for a mass event?
  6. Benedict sees a break from earlier fairs in architecture, while Schrenk sees a shift in corporate display. Which reading fits the evidence of the pavilions better?
  7. Ganz shows that class decided who controlled women's space at the fair. Did the same divisions shape who could speak for women at the 1893 Chicago fair, or does the 1933 case differ?
  8. Maloney and Ganz both show corporate and commercial interests displacing educational exhibits. Which sources would show whether visitors valued the gardens and factory displays for the same reasons the organisers did?
  9. Leshne's films were made to be shown to working audiences. Who saw them, and how far could a film shown in a radical hall answer the fair's own publicity?

A book about this fair, chapter by chapter 10

Ganz, Cheryl R. The 1933 Chicago World’s Fair: A Century of Progress. Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 2008.

Ganz's book is a history of A Century of Progress, the 1933-34 Chicago fair, which was privately financed during the Depression to revive the city's economy and reputation. Its argument is that the organisers used cooperative science exhibits and the idea of progress to generate optimism, presenting technology rather than people as the driver of progress, while Midway entertainment, women, African Americans, ethnic communities and aviation pressed their own meanings on the fair. Chapters move from the Sally Rand shows and the Chicago boosters who financed the fair to its architecture and corporate exhibits, then to women's spaces, the Du Sable cabin, ethnic pavilions and the Graf Zeppelin and stratosphere balloon, and end with an epilogue on the fair's objects and afterlife.

Introduction

Ganz introduces A Century of Progress, the 1933–34 Chicago fair financed privately during the Depression to revive the city's economy and reputation. The chapter argues that organizers, unlike the competitive exhibitors of 1893, used cooperative science exhibits and the idea of progress to generate optimism, presenting technology rather than people as progress's driver. It notes that women, African Americans and fan dancers pressed their own meanings of progress on the fair, and it left a lasting mark on Chicago.

In the contents of this work

1Sally Rand and the Midway

Ganz examines Sally Rand's fan and bubble dances and the Midway shows around them at the 1933 Chicago fair, arguing that the fair's need for crowds and revenue, combined with lax policing, turned popular entertainment into sexual spectacle. Rand's Lady Godiva ride through the Streets of Paris in May 1933 led to a paid engagement and record box-office receipts. Evidence comes from court cases in which judges declined to close the shows, from the shifting nudity rules, and from peep shows such as Visions of Art and the Olympia show. The chapter also traces the repeal of Prohibition, which let concessions sell liquor, and the Hollywood codes that followed. Ganz concludes that Rand, more than the Sky Ride, became the fair's enduring icon of optimism.

In the contents of this work

2Chicago Boosters Set the Stage

Ganz traces how Chicago's business and civic leaders, led by the Dawes brothers and Lenox Lohr, moved from the idea of a centennial celebration in 1926 to a financed world's fair in 1933. Wikipedia

A Century of Progress International Exposition, also known as the Chicago World's Fair, was a world's fair held in the city of Chicago, Illinois, United States, from 1933 to 1934. The fair, registered under the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), celebrated the city's centennial. The theme of the fair was technological innovation, as reflected in its, sleek, modern, Art Deco design, and in its unofficial motto "Science Finds, Industry Applies, Man Conforms". The exhibits at the fair intended to demonstrate how science connected with people's daily lives and how the marriage of science, technology, and industry would lead to national recovery and prosperity. Its architectural symbol was the Sky Ride, a transporter bridge perpendicular to the shore on which one could ride from one side of the fair to the other.
