Preliminary Philippine Exposition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliography

The Preliminary Philippine Exposition was planned for Manila to show Filipinos the exhibits being sent to the Louisiana Purchase Exposition at Saint Louis (Rydell 1984). William Powell Wilson, chairman of the Philippine Exposition Board, and Gustavo Niederlein, its director of exhibits, planned it alongside a permanent commercial museum (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). The museum opened in February 1903 and closed in May; the exposition was never held (Rydell 1984). The book gives the episode one paragraph, as a prelude to the colonial display at Saint Louis (Rydell 1984).

Background and aims

Wilson was Taft's appointee to direct the Philippine Exposition Board (Rydell 1984). He had founded and headed the Philadelphia Commercial Museum, which the book describes as an institution that "wedded science to the interests of American business expansion overseas" (Rydell 1984). From late 1902 he and Niederlein worked in the Philippines (Rydell 1984). Under the congressional act that created the Board, they collected material for a permanent commercial museum in Manila and for a preliminary exposition that would show Filipinos the exhibits bound for Saint Louis (Rydell 1984). The author's note 31 cites Niederlein's manuscript on the Manila museum and preliminary exposition (Rydell 1984). The text gives no date for the preliminary exposition itself (Rydell 1984).

The museum and the exposition

The museum was meant mainly to give American business interests commercial data on the islands. It opened in February 1903 but closed in May, when its exhibits were needed to complete the display for Saint Louis (Rydell 1984). The preliminary exposition "never materialized" for the same reason (Rydell 1984).

Colonial purpose and ethnology

The book reads both projects as part of the government's aim to institutionalise American colonial rule, and says their motives shaped the Saint Louis exhibit (Rydell 1984). The museum had an ethnology division that illustrated "tribal and racial exhibits in every detail" to show Filipinos how far they had to travel before self-rule (Rydell 1984). That ethnological emphasis reappeared at Saint Louis, where it dominated the Philippine Reservation (Rydell 1984). A reproduction of the Manila commercial museum stood at the centre of the reservation (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial rule and tutelage — the book reads the Manila museum and exposition as steps toward institutionalising American rule, and as lessons on Filipinos' distance from self-rule (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984)
  • Commercial information — the museum was intended mainly for American business interests, and its exhibits were absorbed into Saint Louis (Rydell 1984)
  • Ethnology as display — the Manila museum's ethnology division anticipated the ethnological emphasis of the Saint Louis reservation (Rydell 1984)
  • Organisers — Wilson, with his Philadelphia museum background, and Niederlein, his recommended exhibits director, ran the project (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984)

Rydell gives the Preliminary Philippine Exposition a single paragraph inside his chapter on the Louisiana Purchase Exposition. He describes William Powell Wilson and Gustavo Niederlein collecting material in the Philippines for a permanent commercial museum in Manila and for a preliminary exposition meant to show Filipinos the exhibits bound for Saint Louis. The museum opened in February 1903 and closed in May, and the preliminary exposition never materialised. The author uses the episode to argue that these Manila projects expressed the government's aim of institutionalising American colonial rule, and that their ethnological emphasis carried over into the Saint Louis Philippine Reservation.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.
