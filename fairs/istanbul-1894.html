Istanbul Agricultural and Industrial Exposition (planned)

Dersaadet Ziraat ve Sanayi Sergi-i Umumisi
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyOn the web

The Istanbul Agricultural and Industrial Exposition (Dersaadet Ziraat ve Sanayi Sergi-i Umumisi) was a planned exhibition for Istanbul in 1894. The Ottoman government conceived it in 1893 under Abdülhamid II as a permanent exposition on a 142,000-square-metre site on the northern side of the Golden Horn, near Şişli (Çelik 1992p. 139–140; Çelik 1992p. 143–144; Çelik 1992p. 143–144). It was never held (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 19). Çelik says a major earthquake in 1894 damaged the capital and drained the government's limited funds (Çelik 1992p. 139–140). He uses the unbuilt plan as evidence of the Ottoman search for an architectural philosophy (Çelik 1992p. 145–146).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The Ottoman government planned two exhibitions in Istanbul, one in 1863 and one in 1894. Çelik calls the first a successful venture (Çelik 1992p. 139–140). The 1863 event was the Ottoman General Exposition, which Hanson also records as held in Istanbul (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 19; Çelik 1992p. 141–142). The stated goal of the 1894 plan was to promote the development of the country's wealth and well-being (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

Çelik places both Istanbul expositions within a wider argument about the Ottoman Empire and Egypt. For both, he writes, the visibility of the fairs was crucial, because their restructuring efforts were meant to make them part of modern civilisation, and so of the Western world (Çelik 1992p. 139–140). Hanson makes a similar point, briefly: Turkey and its territories joined Western fairs to show that their reforms had succeeded and to project a new, modern identity (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 18).

Site and buildings

The site covered 142,000 square metres on the northern side of the Golden Horn, near Şişli (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). The buildings, including an imperial pavilion, would cover 44,000 square metres, and a rail system would facilitate communication on the site (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). Çelik describes the planned layout. Foreign machines would stand to the left of the entrance, livestock and dairy farming to the right, and a glass-roofed central hall would host the inaugural ceremony. A field for agricultural experiments and a hippodrome were planned for the northeast section (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

Unlike the 1863 exposition, the new one would be permanent and would close for four months each winter (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). The organising committee decided that the pavilions should be built of long-lasting materials: stone, brick and iron (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

Architecture and style

Çelik says the style of the pavilions was a major concern. A government document argued that promotion included promotion of architecture, and that the science of architecture had been forgotten in the Ottoman Empire (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). The document proposed that rules be drawn from Ottoman architectural science or from Arabic, Moorish, Indian, African and Andalusian styles, but some buildings would be designed in the new manner, a Renaissance style based on European architectural rules (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). Çelik reads this as the basic dilemma of choosing an architectural style, reduced to a choice between Islamic and European (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

Raimondo D'Aronco, a practitioner of the Italian stile floreale, was chosen as architect of the exhibition (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). His landscape scheme placed a People's Palace at the centre of the grounds (Çelik 1992p. 145–146). When he presented his drawings, the sultan conferred a decoration on him and agreed to hire Italian master builders (Çelik 1992p. 145–146). A model measuring 3.0 by 2.5 metres was then brought before the sultan (Çelik 1992p. 145–146). Only two of D'Aronco's drawings survive, in the archives of the Dolmabahçe Palace. One shows a setting for ceremonies, possibly the imperial pavilion, and the other the British pavilion (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). Çelik describes the first as Islamic forms in new materials and the second as a typical stile floreale structure (Çelik 1992p. 143–144; Çelik 1992p. 145–146).

Çelik judges the plan ambitious in its social aims, its hopes for economic benefits, and the grandeur of its architecture (Çelik 1992p. 145–146). He sees it as an arena of architectural experimentation, like the exhibitions in Western cities (Çelik 1992p. 145–146).

Organisers and foreign participation

The major exhibits were to be agricultural, industrial and artistic products of the empire, but foreign goods would also be displayed (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). Some foreigners would sit as regular members of the committee that organised the exhibition (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). The exhibition would also double as a marketplace, where products were offered for sale and buyers as well as visitors were admitted (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

The stated aim was to bring under the eye of agricultural and industrial Europe a complete collection of the products of the empire. It would also show native industrialists and farmers foreign methods, so as to make Turkey economically less tributary to foreign countries (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).

Empire and colony

The texts show an Ottoman state project in the imperial capital, not a colonial exhibition. They name no colony taking part, no colonial pavilion or product, and no colonised or Indigenous people on display. Çelik's general claim, that the Ottoman Empire and Egypt used fairs to make themselves part of modern civilisation, concerns the fairs as a whole, and the texts do not tie it to the 1894 plan specifically (Çelik 1992p. 139–140). The only foreign role in the plan that the texts name is the presence of foreigners on the committee and among the goods on show (Çelik 1992p. 143–144). Hanson's Tunisian material follows directly after her sentence on the 1894 plan, but the text does not connect the two (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 19). The texts say no more than this.

Why it was not held

Çelik says the second exposition did not materialise because a major earthquake in 1894 caused great damage to the capital and required large government expenditure from funds that were already limited (Çelik 1992p. 139–140). His introduction gives the same explanation: the Istanbul event fell apart because of financial problems resulting from the earthquake (Çelik 1992p. 7–8). Hanson records only that the event was planned but never held (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 19).

Çelik notes that the pavilions were never built, and that most of the drawings and the model have been lost (Çelik 1992p. 145–146). Because of that loss, the surviving drawings and the record of the style debate are the main evidence for the plan (Çelik 1992p. 143–144; Çelik 1992p. 145–146).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Ottoman modernity — Çelik and Hanson both read the plan as part of an Ottoman effort to be seen as modern, through participation in fairs (Çelik 1992p. 139–140; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 18).
  • Architectural style — the choice between Islamic and European forms, and the Italian stile floreale of D'Aronco, is the plan's main documented debate (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).
  • Economic aims — the exhibits, the marketplace and the aim of making Turkey less dependent on foreign goods (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).
  • Foreign participation — foreign goods and foreign committee members were planned (Çelik 1992p. 143–144).
  • Failure and loss — the earthquake of 1894, and the loss of the pavilions, drawings and model (Çelik 1992p. 139–140; Çelik 1992p. 145–146).

Questions for study

  1. Çelik calls the plan ambitious, but no building was constructed. What can an unbuilt exhibition, known mainly from drawings and a lost model, tell historians about the state that planned it? (Çelik 1992p. 145–146)
  2. The organisers treated architectural style as a question of promotion. Why would a government document link the science of architecture to national display? (Çelik 1992p. 143–144)
  3. The texts describe the plan's economic aims but not its visitors or reception. What further sources would show whether the plan was meant for an Ottoman public, a foreign public, or both? (Çelik 1992p. 143–144; Çelik 1992p. 143–144)
  4. Hanson places Ottoman participation in fairs alongside Tunisian displays, and Çelik places it alongside Egypt. What would be gained by comparing the unbuilt 1894 plan with those displays held at fairs? (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 18; Çelik 1992p. 139–140)

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
4Exposition Fever Carried Eastp. 139

Çelik traces how exposition fever reached the Ottoman Empire and Egypt, where governments used fairs to show they belonged to modern civilisation. The Ottoman General Exposition of 1863 filled a large hall on the Istanbul Hippodrome, designed by Bourgeois and Parvillee in a 'new manner' that mixed Islamic and European details. The planned 1894 Istanbul Agricultural and Industrial Exposition, designed by Raimondo D'Aronco, was never built after an earthquake, and only its drawings and model survive in part. In Egypt, the opening of the Suez Canal at Ismailiyya in 1869 was staged with tents, temporary pavilions and a palace for the khedive, and the guest lists and press accounts show how the ceremony presented a spectacle of local peoples. The chapter ends with the Cairo press predicting that the city would become the 'Paris of the Orient' after the festivities.

Substantial discussion 1

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
chapter 4, 'Exposition Fever Carried East', pp. 141-145 (the 1894 plan); Introduction, p. 5

Celik treats the unbuilt 1894 Istanbul exposition in a chapter on how the Ottoman Empire and Egypt used fairs to show they belonged to modern civilisation. The 1894 plan takes about three printed pages, set beside the 1863 Istanbul exposition and the 1869 Suez Canal opening. Celik argues that the plan matters chiefly for what its architecture debate shows about the Ottoman search for an architectural philosophy, since the organisers argued over whether pavilions should be Islamic or European in style. He reconstructs the plan from a government document, the surviving drawings of architect Raimondo D'Aronco and a lost model, and he records that the earthquake of 1894 ended it.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Çelik examines how Islamic architecture was built, displayed and read at nineteenth-century world's fairs, centred on the Paris expositions of 1867, 1878, 1889 and 1900, Vienna in 1873 and Chicago in 1893. His argument is that the fairs claimed scientific accuracy while indulging fantasy, so that the Islamic quarters and national pavilions show both how Europeans constructed the Orient and how Ottoman and Egyptian rulers sought a modern cultural identity. The chapters move from the visitors and displayed peoples, to the street-level quarters, to the national pavilions of the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Iran, Tunisia, Morocco and Algeria, to the Ottoman and Egyptian exposition experience, and then to the influence of pavilion design on neo-Islamic building in Istanbul, Cairo and the West.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive slight attention, and the mentions are passing. In chapter 2 (Islamic Quarters) the account of Garnier's History of Habitation at the Paris 1889 exposition quotes a classification that places Chinese and Japanese dwellings among "contemporary versions of primitive civilizations", a display organised by a French architect for European visitors, and Drevet's 1878 Paris facades for the Siamese and Annamese pavilions are said to need a symbolic architecture alongside the Muslim nations. In chapter 3 (National Pavilions) Drevet's 1878 foreign section again includes Siam and Annam, and Çelik notes that Luxembourg, Greece, China, Japan and Russia also commissioned French architects, but no Chinese or Japanese building is described. Javanese figures in a parade description quoted in chapter 1, and a Javanese village appears in a Midway description at Chicago in 1893 in chapter 2. Asian participation, state commissioners, merchants and Asian visitors are not discussed at all, and the Introduction sets aside colonial discourse about occupied territories, so Indochina and the Dutch East Indies get no treatment as such.

Passing mentions 1

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
chapter 1 by Debra Hanson, 'East meets West', printed p. 19

Hanson mentions the 1894 plan in one sentence, after the 1863 Ottoman General Exposition in Istanbul, to frame the fair participation of Turkey and its territories as a bid to show reform and a new modern identity.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.
