Sesquicentennial International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Sesquicentennial International Exposition ran in Philadelphia from 31 May to 30 November 1926 to mark the 150th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). It was laid out on 1,000 acres of swampy city land beside the League Avenue Navy Yard (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). The texts describe it as a failure: Rydell calls it "the single worst financial disaster in American international exhibition history" (Rydell 1993p. 5). Two features recur in the texts: High Street, its colonial revival street (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248), and the fair's African American and Klan politics (Rydell 1993p. 158).

Origins and planning

John Wanamaker, a member of the 1876 Centennial's finance committee, first proposed the fair in 1916 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). Giberti's note adds that Wanamaker is reputed to have made the proposal and died in 1922, before the fair took place (Giberti 2002). Accounts differ on when serious planning began. Rydell says Philadelphia's leaders made plans before the United States entered the war (Rydell 1993p. 36). Glassberg's entries date serious planning from 1921, when the Sesqui-Centennial Exposition Association was formed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say serious planning began only in 1920, when Mayor J. Hampton Moore called a meeting that set up a Committee of One Hundred (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Federal support was small. Congress refused a $20 million request and later gave $2 million for its own pavilion (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Glassberg says appeals for $20 million from the federal government and $2.5 million from Pennsylvania were ignored, despite President Harding's 1922 endorsement of the idea (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). Brandt says many Philadelphians opposed the fair, and that the federal government delayed and sharply cut its appropriation (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 133).

Money, site and the city

The association chose 1,000 acres of city-owned marsh by the League Avenue Navy Yard, after considering fourteen sites, including the 1876 grounds in Fairmount Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). The ground needed several million cubic yards of fill, and the buildings needed pilings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246).

Most of the money came from the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). Glassberg says Mayor W. Freeland Kendrick became association president in 1924, and the council voted a $5 million guarantee in 1925 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246). Brandt dates Kendrick's election as mayor to 1923, when the fair became a municipal project used to reward political supporters (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 133). By 1929 the city had made seven appropriations totalling $9.7 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246). Rydell, Findling and Pelle put the city's total cost at $17 million, plus $3 million in certificates (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Glassberg says the first director-general, David Collier, quit about seven months before the opening (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say his successor cut the principal exhibit buildings from five to three (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Buildings, exhibits and attractions

Rydell, Findling and Pelle say the fair kept to a historical focus more than most (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Glassberg says colonial revival was the most influential architectural style at the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 247).

High Street, a street of twenty homes organised by the Women's Committee with a $200,000 grant, was its centre (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248). Brandt says the women chose replicas of historic houses rather than a single Woman's Building, and that the architects built replicas of twenty buildings that had stood downtown (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 128; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129). The aim was an overall colonial atmosphere rather than accuracy, and the buildings were faux-brick over wood and staff (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 130). Rydell describes the colonial street scene as one of the most popular attractions (Rydell 1993p. 74).

Other attractions were large. Glassberg describes an 80-foot Liberty Bell of 30 tons (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247); Allwood, Allan and Reid give its weight as 80 tons (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). Glassberg describes a 200-foot Tower of Light (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247), but Allwood, Allan and Reid say the planned tower was stopped halfway (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). A 100,000-seat stadium hosted the Dempsey-Tunney fight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247). Talking films, public address systems and radio advertising first appeared at the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell says the eugenics movement sent a display to the fair in 1926 (Rydell 1993p. 53).

African Americans, the Klan and the opening day

Rydell argues that race was central to the fair's politics (Rydell 1993p. 158). The Ku Klux Klan planned a national meeting at the fair in 1925, ending with a burning cross (Rydell 1993p. 158). Roche says the planners approved the proposal and then withdrew it after protest (Roche 2000p. 86). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say officials revoked the Klan's auditorium permit in July (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000); Rydell has Mayor Kendrick denying a parade permit in June (Rydell 1993p. 161). The texts disagree on how and when the Klan's plans ended.

A Committee on Negro Affairs was formed only eight months before the opening, and the director-general ignored its request for an opening-day speaker (Rydell 1993p. 159). African American proposals for a Dempsey-Wills fight were rejected, but the Dempsey-Tunney fight went ahead (Rydell 1993p. 159). Public facilities stayed segregated, and a separate house was built for black visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248).

On 22 May 1926 Mayor Kendrick invited A. Philip Randolph, a socialist editor and union organiser, to speak at the opening (Rydell 1993p. 160). Rydell writes that "The overwhelmingly white audience was completely unprepared" for the speech (Rydell 1993p. 163). Rydell presents the speech as a break with Booker T. Washington's Atlanta compromise (Rydell 1993p. 163). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say it was the longest of the ceremonies (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The white press did not report its content, and the official history never mentioned it (Rydell 1993p. 163; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Samuel cites the speech as an earlier civil-rights demand at a fair opening than the 1939 protests (Samuel 2007).

Visitors, weather and reception

Rain dampened the opening ceremonies (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), which were broadcast on radio nationally (Roche 2000p. 86). Kendrick insisted on a 31 May opening to coincide with the Shriners' convention, and many buildings were unfinished (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say the fair was "only about 75 percent completed" on opening day (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Rain is recorded differently: on 107 of the 184 days in Glassberg's entries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246), and on 86 of the 130 days in Rydell, Findling and Pelle (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Attendance is also disputed. Allwood, Allan and Reid give 6,408 in their table (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208), while Rydell, Findling and Pelle say attendance passed 6.4 million, though fewer paid the full fifty cents (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Their endnotes cite a New York Times figure of 4,622,211 paid admissions (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger's appendix lists 5,852,783 (Badger 1979p. 131). In October Mayor Kendrick had announced that attendance would fall short of five million, far below the twenty-eight to thirty-five million some had forecast (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The losses differ too: Rydell, Findling and Pelle give a $5 million deficit (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), while Allwood, Allan and Reid give a loss of $206,987 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

The houses of High Street and their interiors

High Street was designed by the Philadelphia firm Okie, Bissell and Sinkler, and was the brainchild of Sarah Dickenson Lowrie and Elizabeth Price Martin (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129). The exteriors were built with the Philadelphia chapter of the American Institute of Architects and with Fiske Kimball, then director of the Pennsylvania Museum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 248; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248).

The Washington house was furnished by the Philadelphia Arts and Crafts Guild under the Daughters of the American Revolution, using an inventory of Washington's presidential furniture (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 136). At least 75,000 people saw its rooms, and the Guild's copies were for sale at its Rittenhouse Square galleries (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 137). The Strawbridge & Clothier department store furnished the Jefferson boarding house with reproductions based on Monticello (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 137).

The Girard house had period rooms in the French Empire style, fitted by the Chapman Decorative Company (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 131). The First Infirmary, staffed by the National League of Women Voters, showed non-voters as "slackers who prefer golf, picnics, the movies and other" diversions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 131). The Shippen house was the only High Street building finished throughout, with bedrooms, bathrooms and a modern kitchen (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 138). Glassberg names its owner Thomas Shippen (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248), where Brandt names Dr William Shippen Jr (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129).

The Saturday Evening Post praised the street for showing that the founders' character could be read in their homes (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 131).

Women on the fair's committees

Brandt dates the Committee of 100 to 1920 and says at least seven of its members were women (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 132). A note gives the list as of 14 February 1921 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 140). The final board had four women among fifty-six directors (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 132). The separate Women's Committee was formed in 1925 and set up sub-committees for walking tours and bus trips to Germantown (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 133).

Ganz says the Women's Board was to have preponderant or exclusive responsibility for child care, rest rooms, emergency aid and hospitality for women visitors, and also supervised the historic houses and walking tours (Ganz 2008). The fair gave women no separate exhibit space and mixed their exhibits with the men's (Ganz 2008).

Black visitors, money and the crowds

The Committee on Negro Affairs, under John C. Asbury, asked for $25,000 for its exhibits at a meeting in the Union Baptist Church (Rydell 1993p. 159). Asbury later secured only enough money to move a handful of college exhibits (Rydell 1993p. 160). Nahum Brascher, editor of the Associated Negro Press, said he had seen scores of white workers on the site and not one black worker (Rydell 1993p. 159).

Mayor Kendrick refused the stadium for a March bout between Dempsey and Harry Wills, saying the stadium was for visitors to the Sesqui, though he did not mention that Wills was African American (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). African Americans dubbed the Dempsey-Tunney fight the "Joke of the Century" (Rydell 1993p. 164).

Rydell puts the crowd at the opening ceremonies at 60,000 (Rydell 1993p. 161). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say rain "kept crowds down" that day (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The two official pageants slighted the role of African Americans in the nation's past, but two other pageants were devoted to black history, one of them, Loyalty's Gift, with a 500-voice chorus and the young soloist Marian Anderson (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248).

Money from the state and the city

The state gave about $1 million and the federal government about $2 million, barely enough to cover their own pavilions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246). The city also supplied services worth about $5 million, mostly from the Department of Public Works (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246).

Buildings, the site and the legacy

The Palace of U.S. Government, Machinery and Transportation was not finished until 20 July, nearly two months after opening (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 247; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247). The three principal halls included the Palace of Agriculture and Foreign Exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 247).

The Persian pavilion, with its Moorish mosaic facade, proved a favourite with the crowds, and radio was ever present through the gigantic horn speakers of WCAU (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). New York State, Ohio and the Colonial Dames also built replicas of colonial houses, among them Federal Hall and Sulgrave Manor (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 248).

The stadium was renamed John F. Kennedy Stadium and stayed in use until it was demolished in 1992 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the city gained a stadium and a reclaimed plot that had been a swampy rubbish dump (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103).

How historians have interpreted it

Glassberg, in both encyclopedias, calls the fair a flop yet argues that it did much to shape the nation's concept of its past (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246). Glassberg reads High Street's colonial world as an Anglocentric elite culture, set against the cultural pluralism of other parts of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248). Glassberg has also written on the colonial revival at the 1926 fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 459).

Rydell reads the fair through race and class: the fairs reflected in microcosm the shifting political and class alignments of the period (Rydell 1993p. 158). Rydell also records that organisers blamed the historicist architecture for the fair's failure, while critics thought the era of great fairs had ended (Rydell 1993p. 5).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle emphasise poor planning and unusually bad weather as the real causes of failure (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They also say the promoters avoided overseas imperialism and modern art in favour of historical themes (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Brandt reads the women's replicas as a moral counterpoint to the corruption of the male-run fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 127), and as a way of selling Colonial Revival goods to visitors (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 138). Brandt sees the women's work as the fair's one success (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 133), whereas Glassberg's entries treat the fair as a flop overall (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246).

Roche places the fair in a general argument that expositions were state-backed, often lost money and tended to exclude African Americans (Roche 2000p. 43; Roche 2000p. 86). Ganz treats the fair as a warning for Chicago's 1933 organisers, who visited it in 1926 (Ganz 2008).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle tie the fair to the European revival that began with Wembley and Paris in 1924-25 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They say Wembley's Empire Exhibition drew twenty-seven million visitors over two seasons, and that the Paris fair made art deco its theme (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Roche's table gives the 1926 fair 6.4 million visitors and a loss (Roche 2000p. 43). Badger's appendix, by contrast, gives 450 acres, receipts of $5,000,000 and a cost of $26,000,000 (Badger 1979p. 131; Badger 1979p. 131). The area differs from the 1,000 acres given above (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246), and the cost is well above the city's $17 million outlay (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger gives no reason for either figure.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say very little about an Asian presence at the Sesquicentennial. The only display named is Japan's. African American exhibits from black colleges were placed in the Palace of Agriculture, next to the national and state exhibits that Japan, Denmark and California had organised (Rydell 1993p. 160). Rydell gives no further detail on the Japanese display, what it contained, who staffed it, or how visitors received it. Who paid for it is not stated. The reference works record no Asian display, and Glassberg's entries describe the fair's architecture, exhibits and attractions without naming an Asian country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 246). Rydell also records that the organisers' anxieties before the war were shaped by a contemporary's account of Europe and Asia at war, but the text does not link this to any display at the fair (Rydell 1993p. 36).

The Japanese display and the foreign buildings

Rydell places the Japanese national exhibit in the Palace of Agriculture (Rydell 1993p. 160), and Glassberg's entry names that building the Palace of Agriculture and Foreign Exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 247). The same entry says that only seven buildings came from foreign nations and does not name them (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 247; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 247). The texts therefore do not show whether the Japanese display had a building of its own.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Financial failure — The texts agree that the fair lost money and blame planning, politics and weather, but they give different figures for the loss, the city's cost and the rainy days (Rydell 1993p. 5; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Allwood et al. 2001p. 103).
  • Colonial revival and High Street — High Street is the fair's best-known feature in the encyclopedias and in Brandt's chapter, and both present it as a colonial street of replicas (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 248; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 127).
  • African Americans, the Klan and Randolph — Rydell gives the opening speech and the Klan dispute the most space, and Rydell, Findling and Pelle treat them more briefly (Rydell 1993p. 160; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Women's Committee and citizenship — Brandt presents the women's replicas as a moral and domestic answer to the fair's corruption (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 132; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 133).
  • Late planning and site — The texts date the start of planning differently, and the site was swampland that needed fill and pilings (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246).
  • Radio and innovation — The encyclopedias credit the fair with talking films and radio advertising, and Roche records radio carrying the opening to a national audience (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 246; Roche 2000p. 161).
  • Lessons for later fairs — Chicago's 1933 planners used the 1926 fair as a warning, and Ganz records that they visited it (Ganz 2008; Ganz 2008).

Questions for study

  1. Why did a fair so centred on the founders also become a stage for racial protest, and do Rydell and Roche explain this in the same way?
  2. How far can High Street be read as a response to the fair's corruption, as Brandt argues, and how far was it simply a popular attraction?
  3. The texts disagree on the start of planning, the number of rainy days, the attendance and the loss. Which sources would a historian need to resolve these differences, and which should be trusted most?
  4. Glassberg calls the fair a flop yet credits it with shaping the nation's past. What would count as success for an exposition, and who should decide?
  5. Was the fair's historicism a retreat from modernity, as Rydell, Findling and Pelle suggest, or a way of using modern technology and publicity to sell the past, as the innovations described in Glassberg's entries suggest?
  6. What did the Chicago planners of 1933 take from the Philadelphia fair, and what lessons did they reject?
  7. Rydell gives 60,000 people at the opening ceremonies, while Rydell, Findling and Pelle say rain kept crowds down. What evidence would settle the size of the opening crowd?
  8. Which of the seven buildings from foreign nations held Japan's display, and who paid for it?
  9. Was the Women's Committee's separation from the male organisers a means of escape, as Brandt argues, or a limit on women's role, as Ganz's comparison suggests?

Ganz, Cheryl R. The 1933 Chicago World’s Fair: A Century of Progress. Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 2008.
2Chicago Boosters Set the Stage

Ganz traces how Chicago's business and civic leaders, led by the Dawes brothers and Lenox Lohr, moved from the idea of a centennial celebration in 1926 to a financed world's fair in 1933. The chapter argues that the fair's management reflected the business, military and engineering model of its leaders, and that earlier failures, including the Hurley plan, the 1927 cancellation and the 1929 federal resolution, shaped what came next. It draws on committee records, the Chicago press, the Dawes brothers' letters and the organization charts Lohr adapted from earlier expositions. Ganz concludes that the Dawes brothers and Lohr, through bond sales, cash-basis building and a strictly hierarchical structure, turned an uncertain civic idea into a fair ready to reshape Chicago's lakefront and reputation.

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
2.4Picturing Female Patriotism in Three Dimensions: High Street at the 1926 SesquicentennialLydia Mattice Brandtp. 127

Brandt examines High Street, the reconstructed colonial street of replica houses that the Women's Committee built in the south-west quadrant of the 1926 Sesquicentennial International Exposition in Philadelphia, where it became the fair's most popular destination. The argument is that the women's organisations used the replicas to present the home as the moral foundation of citizenship, at a moment when the male organisers were embroiled in scandal. The committee's conservative view of women's civic role drew on earlier female-led preservation and municipal housekeeping movements. The chapter then turns to the individual buildings: the Washington House, furnished from an inventory of the president's possessions; the Jefferson boarding house, fitted with reproductions based on Monticello; and the Shippen House, finished by Good Housekeeping as a modern home behind a colonial facade. Brandt shows that these interiors promoted Colonial Revival goods available for purchase, so that a woman's consumption became part of her civic duty. The chapter concludes that the committee made the colonial past stylish and attainable, and presented its own members as proof that the founders' values survived in modern homes.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
SixAfrican Americans in the World of Tomorrowp. 157

Rydell examines how African Americans tried to turn the white supremacist world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s into arenas for social change. He argues that each struggle over representation reflected shifting class and political alignments within the African-American community, as older Republican elites gave way to labour organisers and radical intellectuals. The chapter moves through five fairs: Philadelphia's 1926 Sesquicentennial, where A. Philip Randolph gave an opening-day address amid protests over a planned Ku Klux Klan presence; Chicago's 1933-34 Century of Progress, where the NAACP and a state anti-discrimination law confronted concessionaires; Dallas's 1936 Texas Centennial, where a federally funded Hall of Negro Life embodied an accommodationist compromise and was later demolished; New York's 1939-40 World's Fair, where picket lines met official denials of discrimination; and the 1940 Chicago American Negro Exposition, which federal agencies helped stage. Evidence comes from newspapers, official correspondence and interviews. Rydell concludes that the fairs were battlegrounds rather than mere showcases, though sponsors repeatedly steered reform towards accommodation.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
3Fairs between the World Warsp. 72

Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that fairs between the world wars turned to science, corporate pavilions and the Depression economy, and that they kept African Americans at the margins. The chapter opens with the 1920s boom and Philadelphia's Sesqui-Centennial of 1926, a historical fair that opened unfinished in the rain, and with Long Beach's 1928 fair, built around a North African theme. Its central case is Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, where modernist buildings, Sally Rand's fan dance and a Hall of Science drew crowds, while demeaning attractions and a poorly attended Negro Day marked its treatment of black visitors. The authors then cover San Diego in 1935-36, the regional fairs in Dallas and Cleveland in 1936, where the Dallas Hall of Negro Life was hidden behind shrubbery, and the New York and Golden Gate fairs of 1939-40, with General Motors' Futurama and the Trylon and Perisphere. The evidence comes from fair publicity, press reports and official histories. The chapter concludes that these fairs offered hope in hard times, and suggests that they may help explain why the United States avoided political revolution in the 1930s.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

This encyclopedia gives the Sesqui-Centennial a four-page entry by David Glassberg, who calls it a 'flop' next to the 1876 Centennial. The entry explains the failure through late planning, unfinished buildings, the city's repeated bail-outs and bad weather. It also argues that the fair did much to shape the nation's idea of its past through its colonial revival replicas, while its modern features (sound film, public address, radio advertising) anticipated later fairs. Critics of the proposed 1930s Chicago and New York fairs, the entry says, used the Philadelphia experience as proof that the giant world's fair was over.
