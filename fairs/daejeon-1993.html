Taejon International Exposition (Expo '93)

OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Taejon International Exposition, called Expo '93, was a specialised international exposition held at Taejon, now Daejeon, in South Korea. It ran for 93 days and closed on 7 November 1993 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388). The Bureau of International Expositions sanctioned it in December 1990 to mark a century since Korea first took part in the Chicago fair of 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 386; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). The texts describe it as the first exposition Korea hosted and as a showcase of Korean industry, which drew more than 14 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The Bureau of International Expositions approved the fair in 1990, and Knowles counts it among the mega-events that followed the Seoul Olympics of 1988 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 207). Knowles stresses that "South Korea was the first developing nation ever selected by the BIE for a sanctioned expo" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208). Daejeon was an important choice for politics. It lay in Chungcheong Province, a base of support for President Roh Tae-woo, who promised to build up its science research (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 207). The national plan was a centre of government and corporate research, which Knowles calls Daedok Science Town and Allwood, Allan and Reid call Taeduk Science Town (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 207; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The theme was 'The Challenge of a New Road to Development' in both accounts (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Organisers and finance

The Encyclopedia names Oh Myung as chairman of the organising committee (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 421). Allwood, Allan and Reid call him chairman and commissioner-general, and spell the name Oh Myong (Allwood et al. 2001p. 169). Their table records a loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210). They also write that "This was the sort of expo when any calculation of financial profit or loss was, frankly, nonsense" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Site and buildings

The Encyclopedia describes a 246-acre parcel on the northern bank of the Kapchon River, below U-Sung-Yi-San Mountain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 223 acres of river valley (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Knowles gives 902,000 square metres, more than double the Lisbon site (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208). The Encyclopedia divides the site into five zones, including an International Zone and an amusement zone (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). The Central Zone held a 93-metre symbol tower, the Tower of Great Light (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387; Allwood et al. 2001p. 169).

Exhibits and attractions

The flagship Korea Pavilion took visitors through Korea's past, present and future, and ended with a humanoid robot quintet playing traditional court instruments (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). Samsung's Starquest pavilion had a bronze and steel Moon base, and Ssangyong's Earthscape pavilion had an IMAX screen (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Knowles describes animated commercials for 'Twinkle, the Dream Being' (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the Korean exhibits showed the problems of the environment and the developing world more than their solutions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Who took part

The Encyclopedia gives 108 international governments and 33 United Nations-level organisations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). Knowles and Allwood, Allan and Reid give the same totals (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that "their presence was totally overshadowed by the Korean pavilions" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The Korean exhibits were permanent, and governments were unwilling to spend on a three-month fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The United States had banned official spending, so private corporations carried its display (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Visitors and reception

The Encyclopedia gives more than 14 million visitors, about 147,000 a day, more than 40 per cent above the estimate (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388). It says nearly half the visitors were Korean students and 4.8 per cent were international, against a projected total of 500,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388). Knowles says that almost all of the 14 million were South Koreans, out of a population of 44 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe foreign countries watching visitors queue for hours to enter the Korean spectacles (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170).

Politics and controversy

Knowles sets the fair against the 1980s. General Chun Doo-hwan came to power after the 1979 coup, and the 1980 uprising in Gwangju was met with a massacre of more than six hundred citizens, by Knowles's count (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 207). Knowles says the fair marked the recent end of military government and the rise of a technological economy (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 194). Allwood, Allan and Reid tell the BIE story. Korea's president announced the fair without prior BIE warning, and member states had agreed a moratorium on events (Allwood et al. 2001p. 169). Korea then won a special dispensation through a compromise, and the BIE vote was near-unanimous (Allwood et al. 2001p. 169).

Aftermath and legacy

Knowles reports that the Daedok Science Town kept growing after the fair, and that its grounds remain open with research laboratories and museums near KAIST (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that many buildings put up for the fair were later built into the Taeduk plan (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The Encyclopedia reports that after the closing the city opened the new Korea Expo Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388).

Its place in the series

Knowles places Daejeon with the BIE-approved fairs of the early 1990s, among them Plovdiv in 1991 and Seville and Genoa in 1992 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 194). Allwood, Allan and Reid show that Portugal cited the Korean plan that had allowed Taejon to occur when it argued that Lisbon 1998 could be recognised (Allwood et al. 2001p. 180).

How historians have interpreted it

Knowles reads Daejeon as a national vision, in which a fair marks the end of military rule and shows a technological economy (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 194p. 208). Allwood, Allan and Reid read the fair through its foreign reception. Korean pavilions overwhelmed foreign ones, and the political and economic objectives counted for more than any profit or loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The Encyclopedia's entry is by John R. McGregor, who worked as a press writer for the fair's Expo Press Center (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 461).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Other Asian participants and visitors

North Korea did not mount a pavilion, but the organisers built a special pavilion for North Korean products (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that the Japanese, the Europeans and the developing nations all did their best, though foreign participation was overshadowed by the Korean pavilions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170). The Encyclopedia lists Fujitsu among the firms in the International Zone, but its entry does not describe a Japanese government pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387). The texts say nothing about Chinese or Southeast Asian visitors.

Themes in the scholarship

  • National showcase — the texts present the fair as a display of Korean technology, with Korean corporate pavilions at its centre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 208; Allwood et al. 2001p. 170)
  • Politics after military rule — Knowles reads the fair as marking the end of military government and as a claim to democratic industrialisation (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 194p. 208)
  • Diplomacy and the BIE — the fair was admitted despite a moratorium on events, through a compromise and a special dispensation (Allwood et al. 2001p. 169)
  • Foreign participation — the 108 nations were overshadowed by Korean firms, and governments were unwilling to fund a three-month fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170)
  • Attendance — more than 14 million visitors, with conflicting accounts of who they were (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209)
  • Science city and legacy — the Daedok or Taeduk science town, Korea Expo Park and the OECD (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 207p. 209; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388)

Questions for study

  1. The BIE had no slot for Korea's fair. What did the moratorium and the 1988 amended protocol allow, and why did the BIE accept a fair outside its programme? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 169p. 179)
  2. The Encyclopedia and Knowles disagree about who visited. What evidence would settle whether most visitors were Korean students, or almost all South Koreans? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 388; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209)
  3. Allwood, Allan and Reid judge the fair by its political and economic gains. How far can a fair be judged by its legacy when its financial result is recorded as a loss? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170p. 210)
  4. The texts name Korean firms in detail but say little about other Asian participants. What would a study of Japanese or Chinese participation in Korea need to examine? (Allwood et al. 2001p. 170; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 387)

Knowles asks whether the world's fair still matters after 1989, and examines the fairs planned during or after the Cold War, from Plovdiv in 1991 to Shanghai in 2010. The argument is that these fairs were shaped by the political changes of their moment and in turn helped to shape them, as host cities used them for urban renewal, national self-presentation and reform. The chapter moves fair by fair. Plovdiv's young-inventors expositions of 1985 and 1991 are read as a shift from Cold War showcase to a sign of Bulgarian transition, and Seville 1992, Lisbon 1998 and Hannover 2000 as bids for European integration, reunification and a sustainable image. Daejeon 1993 is presented as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China. Knowles relies on press reports, Bureau of International Expositions assessments and the fairs' own films, and records the American difficulties at Seville and Hannover. The chapter ends by pointing to Dubai 2020 and a possible American return, presenting the fairs as theatres for national visions.

Allwood's chapter covers the exhibitions held in Japan, Australia and Korea between 1975 and 1999, and argues that Japan turned the medium into a state-led tool of regional and economic development, which its neighbours then copied. It opens with Okinawa 1975, where an ocean theme and the Aquapolis floating platform were set on a former battlefield, and moves through Tsukuba 1985, which used computers, robots and audio-visual display to promote a science city. Its main case is Osaka 1990, a garden exposition that the organisers turned into a hybrid expo within the BIE's rules, with 82 nations and a record attendance. The chapter then surveys Brisbane 1988, whose shaded riverside site and state pavilions are described, Taejon 1993, where Korean corporate pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors, and the Kunming 1999 garden expo. It ends with the Philippines' withdrawal from a recognised exposition for Manila in 1992, and concludes that developing nations were still denied a realistic route to hosting.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. These were displays of Filipinos organised by American officials, promoters and anthropologists, not Filipino participation. China appears in a Chinese Village at Omaha in 1898, which the authors call little more than a souvenir shop, and in the Chinese village at San Francisco in 1915, which framed China as ripe for American investment. Japan appears through the Fair Japan concession at St. Louis, with its Ainu display, and through Japanese exhibits sent to Portland, which were hampered by reported discrimination against Asians at St. Louis. Elsewhere coverage is slight. The interwar chapter notes the Asian ornamental elements and Pacific Basin focus of San Francisco's 1939-40 fair, with elephant towers modelled on Cambodia's Angkor Wat. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction and conclusion name Osaka 1970 and Aichi 2005 as later Japanese fairs. No passage mentions Thailand, Indochina or the Dutch East Indies.
