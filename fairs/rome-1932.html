Exhibition of the Fascist Revolution

Mostra della rivoluzione fascista

The Mostra della rivoluzione fascista (Exhibition of the Fascist Revolution) was a propaganda exhibition held in 1932 in the Palazzo delle esposizioni in Rome, to commemorate the first ten years of Fascist government (Filipová 2015p. 323; Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Alberto Alfieri, director of the Institute of Fascist Culture, conceived it, and Adalberto Libera redesigned its façade (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). It was revised in the late 1930s with anti-Semitic and anti-Communist displays and visits fell after the 1938 racial laws (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Its design served as the model for the Naples Triennale d'Oltremare (Filipová 2015p. 323). The texts show no colonial content in it (Filipová 2015p. 323; Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The texts place the exhibition among the regime's propaganda shows of the 1930s. The author of the chapter on E42 says Mussolini and his cultural officials regarded exhibitions as a uniquely powerful tool for swaying mass opinion (Kargon et al. 2015p. 111). He describes the state displays of the period as crudely propagandistic avant-garde design and says this exhibition epitomised that style (Kargon et al. 2015p. 112). Giovanni Arena says it was conceived not as an objective record of the facts but as celebratory propaganda meant to engage visitors emotionally (Filipová 2015p. 324). Alberto Alfieri conceived it and it captured Mussolini's personal interest (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Arena names Dino Alfieri and Luigi Freddi, who were called to cooperate with artists including Mario Sironi, Enrico Prampolini, Gerardo Doctors, Adalberto Libera and Giuseppe Terragni (Filipová 2015p. 324). The two authors give different first names for the Alfieri who was involved: Alberto in one text and Dino in the other (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125; Filipová 2015p. 324).

Site and design

The exhibition opened in 1932 in the nineteenth-century Palazzo delle esposizioni (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Libera redesigned the palace's original façade, and the museum's catalogue called the new façade "a hymn to modernity" (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). The building also carried the triumphal-arch motif that Libera aspired to take to new heights in the E42 arch (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Mario Sironi, an illustrator and Fascist artist, collaborated with Giuseppe Terragni in laying out the building (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Arena says the exhibition's design drew on experimental futurist art and used photomontage and typography (Filipová 2015p. 323). The Naples Triennale was "designed and built using the model" of this exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 323).

Revisions and visitors

The exhibition was updated in the late 1930s to reflect the anti-Semitic turn in Fascist ideology, after the anti-Jewish Racial Laws of 1938 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). The final version became "vulgarly nationalistic", with major additions that warned of the Communist and Jewish menace (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). The author says the racial laws were highly unpopular among ordinary Italians and that visitation fell off sharply with this last revision (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). The author dates the show 1932–1938 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 112). The texts give no visitor figures.

Empire and colony

The texts say nothing of colonies, colonial products or colonised people in this exhibition. Arena's account of the Naples Triennale, which was built using this exhibition's design model, treats that show as a colonial exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 313; Filipová 2015p. 323). The Rome exhibition is described as propaganda for the Fascist regime and its connection with Mussolini and the regime is well documented in the literature (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).

Permanence and plans

The author describes the exhibition as a major museum intended as a permanent installation in EUR, the Roman district planned for E42 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). Plans to turn it into a permanent Museum of the Fascist Revolution collapsed after Mussolini's fall in summer 1943 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).

Its place in the series

The author links it to E42, the Rome world's fair planned for 1942 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 108). He says the exhibition embodied the same blend of modernity and tradition that stamped the E42 plans, and that E42 likely resembled it in design and graphic style (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). He finds no visible official anti-Semitism in the E42 plans in the archival record so far, and suggests such rhetoric may have been muted for the fair (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125). In 1937 Mussolini ordered the Augustan exhibition moved into the same palace, which had previously presented the Mostra della rivoluzione fascista (Kargon et al. 2015p. 126).

How historians have interpreted it

Giovanni Arena reads the Rome show as a model of propaganda design that the Naples Triennale adopted (Filipová 2015p. 323). The chapter in Kargon et al. (2015) treats exhibitions and museums in E42 as artefacts that encode the paradoxes of Fascist modernism (Kargon et al. 2015p. 111). Its discussion of this exhibition is one example of that reading (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Propaganda design — The texts describe the show as celebratory propaganda using photomontage and typography, and say it set a design model for later shows (Filipová 2015p. 323; Filipová 2015p. 324; Kargon et al. 2015p. 112).
  • Modernism and tradition — The building blended a modernist façade with the triumphal-arch motif, which the author reads as the same blend found in E42 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).
  • Racial politics and revision — The 1938 revision added anti-Semitic and anti-Communist displays, and the author links them to the racial laws (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).
  • Visitors and reception — The author reports a sharp fall in visitation after the 1938 revision, with no figures given (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).
  • Permanence and E42 — The show was planned as a permanent museum in EUR, and the plan lapsed in 1943 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 125).

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Kargon, Robert H., Karen Fiss, Morris Low and Arthur P. Molella. World’s Fairs on the Eve of War: Science, Technology, and Modernity, 1937–1942. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2015.
6EUR: Mussolini’s Appian Way to Modernityp. 108

Mussolini's planned Esposizione Universale di Roma, E42, was to open in 1942 to mark twenty years of Fascism, but it never did. This chapter uses its unbuilt plans and the surviving archive as evidence of Fascist culture. Its argument is that E42 fused Roman antiquity with faith in science and technology, a combination it calls the paradoxes of Fascist modernism. The central case is the Arco dell'Impero, a 240-metre triumphal arch by Adalberto Libera and engineers, intended as the fair's emblem and abandoned when technical problems mounted. The chapter then moves through the Marconi obelisk, the Mostra della rivoluzione fascista, the Augustan exhibition that became the Museo della civiltà romana, and the planned Museo delle scienze, whose committees never agreed on the history of science. It ends with the EUR district that was built, read as a monument to totalitarian planning and later as a symbol of postwar alienation in films by Antonioni, Fellini and Pasolini. The fair's future-facing rhetoric, it concludes, rested on a backward-looking myth.

Kargon et al.'s chapter on the planned Esposizione Universale di Roma (E42) gives the 1932 Mostra della rivoluzione fascista a few pages within its discussion of Fascist exhibitions and museums. It covers the building and its designers, Libera and Sironi, the 1938 revision with anti-Semitic and anti-Communist displays, and the fall in visitors after the racial laws. The wider argument is that E42 and its associated exhibitions and monuments fused Roman antiquity with faith in science and technology; the author calls this the paradoxes of Fascist modernism, and the Mostra is one example of it.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Kargon and colleagues examine five international expositions planned in the years before the Second World War: Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939, which were built, and Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942, which were planned but never opened. The volume argues that these fairs were arenas of ideological struggle in which liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define modernity and to prepare their peoples for war. The introduction sets them against the history of fairs from the Crystal Palace of 1851 and the crises of the 1930s, and each chapter treats one fair through its planning documents and press coverage. The book closes with the first postwar universal exposition, Brussels 1958, which carried the Cold War rivalry into a fair devoted to peaceful atomic energy.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure as one case rather than as a theme. Japan is the only Asian nation with a substantial role, and its treatment is concentrated in chapter 5, "Modernity on Display: The 1940 Grand International Exposition of Japan", on the Tokyo and Yokohama exposition cancelled in 1938: the Commemoration Hall competition, the Nippon Kōbō studio, Bruno Taut's writings on Japanese architecture and the 1942 "Overcoming Modernity" symposium. Japan's participation in fairs run by others is briefer: the Paris 1937 pavilion by Sakakura Junzō (chapter 2), the New York 1939 displays (chapters 4 and 5), and the low-profile exhibit at Brussels 1958 (chapter 7). Chapter 3 mentions a visit to Düsseldorf by Prince Chichibu. Elsewhere Asia is context or comparison. Chapter 5 links the exposition to the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, the Manchukuo express "Asia", Kenzō Tange's 1942 remark that admiring Angkor Wat was "the mark of an amateur", and a Thai proposal for a Japan-Thailand cultural centre in Bangkok. China appears as the war that cancelled the Tokyo fair and, in chapter 1, as the Manchurian crisis of 1931. Apart from Japan, the passages show no East or Southeast Asian nation exhibiting at these fairs and no Asian colonial display. The only exhibition held in Asia is Osaka 1970, mentioned in passing in chapter 7.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

On the web

Wikipedia

The Exhibition of the Fascist Revolution (Italian: Mostra della Rivoluzione Fascista) was an art exhibition held in Rome at the Palazzo delle Esposizioni from 1932 to 1934. It was opened by Benito Mussolini on 28 October 1932 and was the longest-lasting exhibition ever mounted by the Fascist regime. Nearly four million people attended the exhibition in its two years. Intended to commemorate the revolutionaries who had taken part in the rise to power of Italian fascism, the Exhibition was supposed to be, in Mussolini's own words, "an offering of faith which the old comrades hand down to the new ones so that, enlightened by our martyrs and heroes, they may continue the heavy task."
