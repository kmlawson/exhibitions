Hygiene Exhibition (Central Association of Sanitation Cooperatives)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliography

In May 1932 the Central Association of Sanitation Cooperatives (CASC) held a hygiene exhibition in Keijō, then under Japanese rule. It ran from 2 to 7 May, was promoted by a film, and gave awards to Korean residents who kept sanitary rules. Henry presents it as one of the campaigns through which Korean elites tried to win the lower classes to their own nation-building programmes. Only one text in the collection mentions it, in a single paragraph, and that text does not name the venue. (Henry 2014p. 162p. 164)

Organisers, film and exhibition

The CASC was founded in June 1925 (Henry 2014p. 159). In 1932 it ran a series of hygiene campaigns in the city's Korean neighbourhoods, beginning in late April with a film on preventing communicable illnesses (Henry 2014p. 164). The first screening took place at Susong Common School. Later showings were at Pagoda Park and Sajik Park, sites that Koreans frequented (Henry 2014p. 164). The film also promoted a CASC hygiene exhibition that was "held from May 2 until May 7" (Henry 2014p. 164). It coincided with the spring cleanup, which sanitation cooperatives now administered in the northern village (Henry 2014p. 164). The text does not name the exhibition's venue (Henry 2014p. 164).

Awards and self-regulation

At the exhibition the CASC gave awards to Koreans who most faithfully followed sanitary rules for cleaning toilets, sewers, kitchens and dustbins (Henry 2014p. 164). Henry reads the awards as a sign of growing confidence that colonised residents could "become agents of their own self-regulation". He adds that Japanese and Korean agents still enforced the rules strictly (Henry 2014p. 164).

Its place in the series

The texts set the 1932 event within a sequence. In July 1922 a traveling hygiene exhibition sponsored by the Osaka company Jintan drew more than three thousand visitors on its first morning (Henry 2014p. 162). In October 1922 officials allocated 2,500 yen to another hygiene exhibition (Henry 2014p. 162). A note gives the first government-organised hygiene exhibition as held in early July 1921 at Pagoda Park (Henry 2014p. 253). The Disease Prevention Exhibition of 1933 ran from 9 to 15 August, with sixteen buildings and over twelve thousand items (Henry 2014p. 163). Henry says the 1933 exhibition and related events drew nearly eighty thousand people, most of them Korean. He gives no attendance figure for 1932 (Henry 2014p. 163). He places the 1932 event "just one year" before the 1933 exhibition (Henry 2014p. 164).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Self-regulation — Henry reads the awards as evidence that colonised residents could police their own hygiene, though officials and Korean agents still enforced the rules (Henry 2014p. 164)
  • Films and public events — the film advertised the exhibition and was shown at a school and at two parks frequented by Koreans (Henry 2014p. 164)
  • Sanitation cooperatives — in 1925 cooperative leaders persuaded officials to let them run the semiannual cleanups, and the 1932 exhibition coincided with the spring cleanup they administered (Henry 2014p. 159p. 164)
  • Elites and nation building — Korean elites used hygienic modernity to attract lower-class Koreans to their own programmes (Henry 2014p. 164)
  • A series of exhibitions — the 1932 event sits between the 1922 hygiene exhibitions and the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition (Henry 2014p. 162p. 163p. 164)

Questions for study

  1. Henry reads the awards as a sign of confidence in colonised residents. Did a prize for compliance express trust, or was it another means of enforcement? (Henry 2014p. 164)
  2. The film was shown at a school and two parks frequented by Koreans. What does the choice of these sites suggest about the audience the CASC wanted to reach? (Henry 2014p. 164)
  3. The text gives attendance for the 1933 exhibition but none for 1932. What records would show how many people attended the 1932 exhibition? (Henry 2014p. 163p. 164)
  4. The 1921 and 1922 events were run by government officials, while the 1932 event was run by the CASC. What does the change of organiser tell us about the aims of each campaign? (Henry 2014p. 162p. 164p. 253)

Henry treats the May 1932 hygiene exhibition in one paragraph, as one of the campaigns that the Central Association of Sanitation Cooperatives ran in Keijo's Korean neighbourhoods. He presents Korean elites as using hygiene films, exhibitions and lectures to build their own programmes of nation building, while colonial officials used the same civic sites to monitor the health of Koreans. For Henry, the awards to residents who best kept the rules show a growing belief that colonised people could regulate themselves, even though both Japanese and Korean agents enforced the rules strictly. The 1932 event is brief; the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition gets much fuller treatment in the same chapter.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
