The second West Lake Exposition (West Lake Exposition, Xihu bolanhui 西湖博览会) was held in Hangzhou in 2002, according to a footnote in Denton's study (Denton 2014p. 275p. 306). The texts do not name its organisers, exhibits or visitors. They treat it as a revival partly commemorating the 1929 West Lake Exposition, a commercial fair with Nationalist framing that Denton discusses at length (Denton 2014p. 47p. 51). Its interest lies in how Hangzhou now remembers that earlier fair (Denton 2014p. 51).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and dates

The texts give two dates for the revival. The footnote says a second fair, "in part to commemorate the first", was held in 2002 (Denton 2014p. 275). The main text says that West Lake expositions "have been held regularly since 2000", and that interest in the 1929 fair inspired their renewal (Denton 2014p. 51). Denton does not reconcile the two dates or describe the sequence of the revived fairs (Denton 2014p. 51p. 275).

The commemorated fair

The fair being commemorated opened in June 1929 and lasted 137 days (Denton 2014p. 47). Denton calls it primarily a commercial venture with nationalist implications. It drew more than twenty million visitors, including some one thousand foreigners (Denton 2014p. 47). Its Revolutionary Memorial Hall was the only free hall, which Denton suggests may have been an enticement to lure visitors away from the commercial halls (Denton 2014p. 51). The texts say nothing of these matters for the 2002 fair (Denton 2014p. 275).

Commemoration and nostalgia in Hangzhou

Denton says the 1929 fair was left out of Mao-era museum histories because of its "commercial and KMT agenda" (Denton 2014p. 51). The fair is now an object of local pride in Hangzhou (Denton 2014p. 51). A West Lake Expo Museum is devoted to its history, and the footnote places it on the north-west corner of the lake, "very near where the original exposition was held" (Denton 2014p. 51p. 276). Denton reads this nostalgia as a product of the new market economy, one that brands Hangzhou as a city with a vibrant commercial past (Denton 2014p. 51).

A photograph in Nanjing

A photograph of the 1929 fair appears in the economics display of the Modern Chinese History Site Museum in the Nanjing Presidential Palace (Denton 2014p. 84p. 85p. 86). Denton says the display presents the Republican era as a period of incipient Chinese capitalism. Denton links the photograph to the nostalgia for the 1929 fair, and to the view that the Nanjing decade had global economic aspirations that resonate with China's present economic transformation (Denton 2014p. 85p. 86).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commemoration of the 1929 fair — the second fair is described as partly a commemoration of the first, and local pride in the 1929 fair is cited as the inspiration for the renewed series (Denton 2014p. 275p. 51).
  • Market nostalgia and city branding — Denton reads renewed interest in the 1929 fair as nostalgia that suits the market economy and brands Hangzhou as commercial (Denton 2014p. 51).
  • Commerce and nationalism — the 1929 fair set Nationalist commemoration against consumer spectacle, and framed consumption as patriotism; the texts say nothing about how the 2002 fair handled this (Denton 2014p. 50p. 51).
  • Museum memory — the 1929 fair is absent from Mao-era displays but appears in a Nanjing photograph and in a Hangzhou museum devoted to its history (Denton 2014p. 51p. 86).

Questions for study

  1. Which date should a student take for the revived series, 2000 or 2002, and what evidence would settle the difference? (Denton 2014p. 51p. 275)
  2. Did the 2002 fair keep the Nationalist commemoration of 1929, or only its commercial framing? The texts do not say, but Denton shows how the 1929 fair's revolutionary discourse sat uneasily with its consumer spectacle (Denton 2014p. 50).
  3. Denton reads Hangzhou's nostalgia as a way of branding the city. What does a commemorative fair offer its host city that the original fair did not? (Denton 2014p. 51)
  4. Why was the 1929 fair left out of Mao-era museums, yet become a source of local pride after reform? (Denton 2014p. 51)

Brief discussion 1

Denton, Kirk A. Exhibiting the Past: Historical Memory and the Politics of Museums in Postsocialist China. Honolulu: University of Hawai‘i Press, 2014.
Ch. 2, 'Exhibiting the Revolution' (pp. 47-52; notes pp. 275-276); ch. 3, 'Commodification and Nostalgia' (p. 86)

Denton's chapter on revolutionary exhibitions uses the 1929 West Lake Exposition in Hangzhou as its main example. A second fair, held in 2002, appears only in a footnote and in one sentence of the main text, and the book gives no details of its organisers, exhibits or visitors. Denton's argument concerns the 1929 fair, a commercial exhibition with Nationalist framing, and the later nostalgia for it in Hangzhou, which Denton reads as a market-era effort to brand the city as commercial. The chapter's larger case is that post-Mao museums again link revolutionary history with commerce and economic development.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Denton's Exhibiting the Past surveys how museums and memorial sites in the People's Republic of China represent the national past, from dynastic times onward. The introduction sets out a narratological method that reads exhibits as visual stories and compares Chinese museums with memory museums elsewhere. The thirteen chapters are organised by museum type rather than by theme: premodern history, the Museum of the Chinese Revolution, revolutionary history under the market, martyrs' memorials, military museums, sites of Japanese atrocity, memorial halls for model heroes and leaders, ethnographic museums and theme parks, red tourism, and municipal urban planning halls. Denton argues that state museums remain politically directed while adapting to market reform, nationalism and the decline of socialism. The conclusion ties these displays to neoliberal narratives of past, present and future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the book's whole subject, because every chapter treats museums and memorial sites in the PRC, so its coverage is central. The concern is how China displays its own past, not how Asian states or peoples take part in international exhibitions. Chapter 6 (Heroic Resistance and Victims of Atrocity) is the most concentrated treatment of another East Asian country: Japan is the aggressor in the War of Resistance Memorial Hall at Wanping, the Nanjing Massacre Memorial Hall and the Unit 731 Museum in Harbin. Chapter 1 (National Origins and Local Identity) treats Korea through the contested kingdom of Koguryo/Gaojuli. Taiwan and Hong Kong recur in chapters 1, 3, 5 and 6, as partners in a shared memory of resistance or as commercial context. Exhibitions in Asia and Asian visitors appear mainly in chapter 2, on the 1929 West Lake Exposition in Hangzhou, which the book says drew some one thousand foreign visitors, among them from Japan, Korea and Indonesia. Southeast Asia is slight. Vietnam appears as a comparison in chapter 2, through Hanoi's revolution museum, and in chapter 9, through its ethnic museum, while Indonesia, Thailand and Burma are cited only in a note to chapter 5 on military museums. No Southeast Asian pavilions, displays or peoples on show are discussed.
