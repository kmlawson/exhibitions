The British Empire Exhibition ran at Wembley, in London, from 23 April to 1 November 1924, and again from 9 May to 31 October 1925 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236). Every dominion and colony able to pay built its own palace, and no foreign nations were invited, so the imperial theme filled the site (Greenhalgh 1988p. 57; Greenhalgh 1988p. 61). Our texts treat it as the largest imperial city in exhibition history and as a test of how empire was staged for the British public between the wars (Greenhalgh 1988p. 57; Geppert 2010p. 177). Historians use it to ask whether empire was being renewed in these years or was already ending.

Origins and aims

The British Empire League proposed an imperial exhibition for Edward VII's coronation in 1902. That idea was not realised until Wembley, and the League worked instead with Imre Kiralfy's London shows (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39). Kiralfy's imperial shows, from the Empire of India (1895) to the Japan-British Exhibition (1910), were the immediate models (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 235). A scheme for 1915, timed to the Prince of Wales's coming of age, was delayed by war and organisational problems (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). Geppert gives three motives for the revival: post-war imperial unity, demobilisation and unemployment, and London's lack of a monumental exhibition to match its status as imperial capital (Geppert 2010p. 143). Geppert places the fair among the projects meant to imperialise London and lessen its inferiority complex about Paris (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 232).

Organisation and finance

A guarantee fund, a pool of money pledged against losses, was created by an Act of 23 December 1920, and the government gave it £100,000 (Geppert 2010p. 142; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). Geppert concludes that the exhibition was "neither government-initiated nor state-sponsored", with no direct subsidy until the Labour government of late 1923 (Geppert 2010p. 142). Roche gives the state a larger role. In Roche's account the government committed half of the £2.2 million needed, public subscription provided the rest, and the government acted as co-guarantor (Roche 2000p. 61). The texts do not settle how much the state committed. The site was bought in 1921 for £100,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 235). The first season drew 17,403,267 visitors, against an expected minimum of 25 million (Geppert 2010p. 146). The organisers lost more than £600,000 before the buildings were sold. The government then took on more responsibility, and the fair reopened in 1925 with some new pavilions (Geppert 2010p. 146; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233). The two-year loss is given as £1,581,905 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 237). Allwood gives a loss of nearly £2m (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107), and Rydell also says nearly £2 million (Rydell 1993p. 66). Badger's table gives receipts of £2,814,935 against costs of £4,396,840, a gap that matches the £1,581,905 deficit (Badger 1979p. 131).

Rydell dates the government's commitment earlier than Geppert does. He says that after the war the British Empire Club, with the active support of the Prince of Wales, revived the idea of a colonial fair, and that Prime Minister David Lloyd George threw the government's weight behind it (Rydell 1993p. 64; Rydell 1993p. 64). Roche also names Lloyd George, a Liberal, among the active supporters of the scheme (Roche 2000p. 61). Geppert places the turning point later, under the Labour government elected at the end of 1923 (Geppert 2010p. 142).

Site, architecture and attractions

The texts disagree on the size of the site. One gives 216 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233), another 200 acres (Roche 2000p. 61), another 88 hectares (Geppert 2010p. 148), and another over 220 acres (Lawn 2009p. 108). Geppert describes a strictly symmetrical plan in the form of a St Andrew's cross (Geppert 2010p. 148). MacKenzie describes a pyramid, with a garden at the northern apex, the largest palaces below it, and the stadium and smaller colonies at the base (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236).

The architects were Simpson and Ayrton. The buildings were mostly classical, built of ferro-concrete (reinforced concrete) on steel frames, and MacKenzie argues that Wembley brought this technique from the United States to Britain on a grand scale (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 235). The Empire Stadium stood on the site of Watkin's Folly, an abandoned London imitation of the Eiffel Tower (Geppert 2010p. 138). It took about 300 working days and £750,000 to build, and 25,000 tons of concrete were mixed by hand (Allwood et al. 2001p. 98). It opened with the 1923 FA Cup Final, watched by an estimated 150,000 people (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 232), and its capacity of 100,000 served the opening and closing ceremonies (Roche 2000p. 63).

Joseph Emberton's kiosks, Kauffer's posters and the lion symbol gave the fair its advertising identity, and critics found the lion Assyrian and un-English (Allwood et al. 2001p. 99). The Neverstop Railway ran 88 cars round the eastern half of the site (Allwood et al. 2001p. 99). A daily Pageant of Empire used 15,000 performers (Roche 2000p. 63). The Government Pavilion held a Court of Honour with an illuminated relief map of the world, which about five million people saw in the first year (Geppert 2010p. 156).

Most pavilions were dismantled or sold after the close. The Burmese pavilion was shipped to Australia for the Melbourne centenary celebrations, and the stadium stayed in use and hosted the 1948 Olympic Games (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234).

The stadium figures given above also need checking against the other texts. Geppert gives the stadium's capacity as 126,500 and says the 1923 Cup Final drew more than twice that number (Geppert 2010p. 134). Roche gives 100,000, and says the Cup Final crowd was dangerously above the designed capacity (Roche 2000p. 63; Roche 2000p. 63). The encyclopedia entry in text 35 gives the stadium's capacity as more than 125,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 232). Its figure of 150,000 for the Cup Final crowd is at (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 232); the citation given above for that figure points to the earlier line, which carries the capacity instead.

Rudyard Kipling named the roads (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 237), and the names give the site a map of empire. Geppert counts 24 kilometres of roadway (Geppert 2010p. 150), and Allwood gives fifteen miles (Allwood et al. 2001p. 99). Behind the main entrance, 'Drake's Way' faced 'Anson's Way' (Geppert 2010p. 150). The New Zealand and Indian pavilions were joined by 'Craftsmen's Way' and 'Engineer's Way' (Geppert 2010p. 150). The main axis ran from the entrance to the Place of Youth in front of the stadium, and was called Kingsway (Geppert 2010p. 150; Geppert 2010p. 150). Geppert says the name was preferred to 'Empire Avenue' and 'King Edward VII Street' (Geppert 2010p. 150). It recalled the Kingsway-Aldwych scheme in London and the King's Way built for New Delhi (Geppert 2010p. 150; Geppert 2010p. 150).

Nations and colonies on show

Fifty-six of the 58 countries of the empire exhibited, and only Ireland and Gibraltar were absent (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233). Every colony and dominion able to pay had its own palace. These included Canada, India, Australia, New Zealand, Malaya, Burma, Newfoundland, South Africa, East and West Africa, Palestine and Cyprus, Fiji, the West Indies, Hong Kong, Ceylon and Sarawak (Greenhalgh 1988p. 57). Benedict argues that the colonial pavilions displayed power rather than reality, and notes that each colony was housed separately (Benedict 1983; Benedict 1983p. 41). The emphasis was on trade, not acculturation (Benedict 1983p. 47). The four most important territories, New Zealand, Australia, Canada and India, had pavilions facing the lakes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236).

Peoples on display

The Official Guide's section on races in residence listed the people who lived inside the exhibition: 20 Malays, 30 Burmans, 160 Hong Kong Chinese and 60 West Africans (Greenhalgh 1988p. 95; Lawn 2009p. 120). Greenhalgh finds the displays more subdued than the White City shows of 1908 to 1914. Greenhalgh reads this not as less racism but as a more guarded racism (Greenhalgh 1988p. 95; Greenhalgh 1988p. 95). Benedict notes that the peoples shown came from non-white societies, while settler groups appeared as technicians or craftsmen (Benedict 1983p. 48).

Schools were a major audience. Grosvenor describes the Board of Education's Weekly Bulletin of Empire Study, which reached at least 125,000 copies a week, 124,000 of them to schools (Lawn 2009p. 110). Its themes were race character and a hierarchy of Empire. It taught that the three great types of men, including the yellow or Mongolic type, were all among the peoples of the Empire, with the white type the most progressive (Lawn 2009p. 112; Lawn 2009p. 112). Birmingham alone sent over 13,000 children to the exhibition on special trains (Lawn 2009p. 115).

The Walled City was built as three 'native villages', each with huts for more than 70 occupants (Geppert 2010p. 167; Geppert 2010p. 167). A Daily News guide said that inside its red mud walls Sierra Leone and the Gold Coast became 'fantastically real' (Geppert 2010p. 167; Geppert 2010p. 167), and that a 'real princess' lived in the compound (Geppert 2010p. 167). Major Sir Frederick Lugard, a former Governor-General of Nigeria, found the design 'true' (Geppert 2010p. 167). The students' complaint of May 1924 was aimed less at the village than at a Sunday Express article, 'When West Africa Woos', whose reporter had questioned a 'princess' about marriage and sexual customs (Geppert 2010p. 167; Geppert 2010p. 168; Geppert 2010p. 168). The Colonial Office told the students they had no 'earthly right' to take up the matter, and the village closed first to the press and then to the public (Geppert 2010p. 168; Geppert 2010p. 168). Officials gave 'the privacy of the craftsmen' as the reason, yet residents could leave the walls only by special permission and under escort (Geppert 2010p. 177; Geppert 2010p. 177).

Visitors' views were divided. Some felt homesick in the Walled City, others noticed a 'European touch', and one saw 'the West Africa of many years ago' (Geppert 2010p. 168; Geppert 2010p. 168; Geppert 2010p. 168). Hughes, citing Hakim Adi, argues that the Wembley experience was central to the formation of the West African Students' Union in the 1920s (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65). The Colonial Office, which had to manage more than 125 African students, courted the Union for fear that mistreated students would turn to anti-colonial groups (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 65).

Visitors and reception

The two seasons drew 27,102,498 visitors in total (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236). Grosvenor gives 18 million for 1924, taken from the Prince of Wales's closing speech (Lawn 2009p. 107). Roche gives around 27 million, and Rydell and colleagues give the same figure (Roche 2000p. 59; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Benedict warns that attendance figures are subject to hyperbole, and that admissions are not the same as separate people (Benedict 1983p. 61).

Reception was mixed. Roche judges the expo a popular event, and says the press and the crowds found it so (Roche 2000p. 63). P. G. Wodehouse and Noel Coward criticised it (Roche 2000p. 63). Geppert records four argument patterns in press coverage: fostering trade, bonds of empire, the unity of mankind, and seeing the world in a day (Geppert 2010p. 170). Dissent came from Africans. In May 1924 the Union of Students of African Descent protested against the West African walled city, and the African village was closed for a time (Geppert 2010p. 167). In 1929 the West African Students' Union argued that the government had already admitted exploitation at Wembley. Hughes records that the Colonial Office and Home Office had affirmed in principle that they opposed misrepresentation of British subjects at such events (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 64; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 66).

Arthur Mason, a butcher's son, called the exhibition 'a big thing in our life' and recalled paying ninepence to enter (Geppert 2010p. 173; Geppert 2010p. 173). Ibrahim Ismaa'il, a young Somali living in a socialist community near Cardiff, felt that the world seemed "made for Europeans" (Geppert 2010p. 173). Eric Pasold, an 18-year-old from a family of Bohemian industrialists, wrote that "The more exotic the pavilions the more they thrilled me" (Geppert 2010p. 173; Geppert 2010p. 173). Sir Henry Cole, chairman of the Committee for Government Participation, put provincial visitors at 70 per cent against 30 per cent Londoners (Geppert 2010p. 174; Geppert 2010p. 174).

Two German government observers, Sommer and Haug, criticised the site's lack of a central tower and the "modern department store style" of many displays, yet concluded that the exhibition was "imperialism in its purest form" (Geppert 2010p. 175; Geppert 2010p. 175; Geppert 2010p. 175).

The Government Pavilion

The government paid for its space like any other exhibitor. Geppert says it had to pay the organisers a substantial rent (Geppert 2010p. 159). Its Admiralty Theatre re-enacted the 1918 raid on Zeebrugge with model ships, and Geppert counts this among the very few places on the site where the First World War appeared (Geppert 2010p. 159; Geppert 2010p. 159). He says the re-enactment was popular with all ages and classes (Geppert 2010p. 159). Grosvenor says schoolchildren saw the raid as a reminder of the part their accompanying adult had played in the war (Lawn 2009p. 121). The pavilion also held a painting by H. C. Selous of the opening of the 1851 exhibition, lent by the Victoria and Albert Museum (Geppert 2010p. 159). Morning performances for schoolchildren brought, by official estimate, 120,000 children (Geppert 2010p. 160).

Schools were a major audience

Schools were discouraged from visiting on Saturdays, but on Empire Day, 24 May, the stadium held a thousand military bandsmen and a procession of 2,000 representatives from across the Empire (Lawn 2009p. 122; Lawn 2009p. 122; Lawn 2009p. 122). Eight hundred young cadets, scouts and guides paraded (Lawn 2009p. 122). A Scouts Imperial Jamboree in the stadium from 1 to 8 August was attended by 100,000 boy scouts (Lawn 2009p. 122; Lawn 2009p. 122). Geppert says the Royal Colonial Institute set essay topics for the more than two million schoolchildren who saw the exhibition in its first year, and repeated the competition the next year (Geppert 2010p. 169; Geppert 2010p. 169).

Spectacle and amusements

Grosvenor says the Pageant of Empire was scheduled nightly between 21 July and 30 August (Lawn 2009p. 121). It had three parts, Westward Ho, Eastward Ho and Southward Ho (Lawn 2009p. 122). Eastward Ho re-enacted the state procession of the Mogul emperor Jehangir, with snake-charmers, dancing girls, elephants and Indian bands (Lawn 2009p. 122; Lawn 2009p. 122). Bad weather restricted some performances, but 60,000 people saw the last one (Lawn 2009p. 122; Lawn 2009p. 122). Roche calls the pageant daily (Roche 2000p. 63). Benedict says the pageant, called the Bridge of Empire, with words by Kipling and music by Elgar, took three days to perform, each episode forming a block of stone in a bridge (Benedict 1983p. 47; Benedict 1983p. 47). MacKenzie puts audiences at 50,000 to 60,000 in late July and August (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236).

Allwood describes a rodeo run by C. B. Cochran, whose 'genuine cowgirls' caused comment in the papers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 98). James Paine and Son provided firework displays, and the Navy, Army and Air Force Torchlight Tattoos were held in the stadium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 98; Allwood et al. 2001p. 98). The amusement park had a coal-mine reproduction, the tomb of Tutankhamen and the Water Chute (Allwood et al. 2001p. 100; Allwood et al. 2001p. 100; Allwood et al. 2001p. 100). MacKenzie gives the park 40 acres and five bandstands, one holding 10,000 people (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236), and a Searchlight Tattoo that proved so popular its run was extended (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236).

How historians have interpreted it

Most readings agree that the fair was imperial propaganda, but they differ on what it achieved. MacKenzie argues that Wembley renewed and perpetuated the importance of empire to the British between the wars. It presented empire as a cure for economic ills, with imperial preference as an aim, and that message carried through the Empire Marketing Board, the Ottawa agreements of 1932 and the Glasgow exhibition of 1938 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 237). Geppert holds both readings at once. The fair renewed imperial belief, but its display of the empire as the whole world was a tautology, and so it also marked the beginning of the empire's end (Geppert 2010p. 177). In the encyclopedia entry, Geppert adds that Wembley foreshadowed the empire's political disintegration (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 235). Greenhalgh reads the pompous acres of plaster and stone as the mark of a nation in decline (Greenhalgh 1988p. 158). Roche treats the fair as an ideological vehicle, with the imperial message filtered through its fairground entertainment (Roche 2000). Roche also calls Rydell's view that Wembley failed to appeal to the modern public strained (Roche 2000p. 64). Rydell argues that the Wembley affair lacked a novel design form capable of capturing public attention, which the Crystal Palace had (Rydell 1993p. 67). Grosvenor reads the school programme as a training school for empire that taught a racial hierarchy (Lawn 2009p. 122). Hughes argues that by 1924-25 traditional ethnological displays of empire already seemed out of step with an emerging anti-colonial consciousness (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 63).

The texts give Asia a real but uneven place at Wembley. Several Asian territories had their own palaces or pavilions, but none of our texts records a Japanese or Chinese state display at the fair.

India

The Indian section was the first organised by the government of India, in cooperation with the provincial governments, rather than by the India Office (Geppert 2010p. 163). Dewan Bahadur T. Vijayaraghavacharya, the official Indian exhibition commissioner, described India as having grown from a dependency into a partner in the Empire, and reminded British audiences of India's military contribution in the First World War (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 164). Greenhalgh describes the Indian palace as the most impressive Indian palace built in Britain, on a five-acre site, with a palace of three acres and an expenditure of £180,000. Its chief function, Greenhalgh argues, was to show what Britain had done for India (Greenhalgh 1988p. 61; Greenhalgh 1988p. 61). The texts do not say who met that cost. Hoffenberg reads the pavilion as a neo-Victorian reworking of Ruskin's contrast between Scottish and Indian architecture, made without reference to the Sepoy Mutiny. Hoffenberg also notes that an Australian architect designed the Australian pavilion, whereas an English artist crafted the Indian one (Hoffenberg 2001p. 276; Hoffenberg 2001p. 276).

Geppert notes that references to change in India and its growing independence could be read as the direct outcome of progress (Geppert 2010p. 165). Officials estimated that more than 5,000 visitors came from India alone, many of them leaving their native land for the first time (Geppert 2010p. 174). India declined to take part in 1925 for financial reasons, and was the only major colony absent that year (Geppert 2010p. 165). Burma was also absent in 1925 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234).

Burma, Ceylon, Malaya and Hong Kong

Malaya, Burma, Ceylon, Hong Kong and Sarawak each had a palace (Greenhalgh 1988p. 57). The Burma and Ceylon pavilions had a more localised style than the classical majority of the site (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 236). Malaya, Burma, Ceylon and Hong Kong were described as representing the characteristic architecture of their countries, but their architects were more often British than indigenous (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233). The texts say little about who organised these pavilions. The Burmese pavilion was later shipped to Australia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234). The official guide promised that visitors could walk from Hong Kong to New Zealand or Malaya in a few minutes (Rydell 1993p. 65).

Peoples living in the exhibition

The guide's list of residents included 20 Malays, 30 Burmans and 160 Hong Kong Chinese. Indians and Singhalese lived outside the exhibition and attended their pavilions daily (Greenhalgh 1988p. 95; Greenhalgh 1988p. 95). Grosvenor notes that people from Nigeria, India and China, described as Christian natives, were looked after in the bungalows of the United Evangelistic mission (Lawn 2009p. 121). The Bulletin of Empire Study taught schoolchildren that the yellow or Mongolic type belonged to the Empire, with the white type the most progressive (Lawn 2009p. 112). Greenhalgh's reading of the living displays is that they were guarded rather than less racist (Greenhalgh 1988p. 95).

India: the pavilion and its entertainments

India held a rehearsal exhibition at Calcutta's Eden Gardens in 1923. It met local political opposition, and some firms invited to it refused to take part (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). The Indian Legislative Assembly agreed on 25 March 1922 to a pavilion, on condition that the Calcutta show went ahead (Geppert 2010p. 163). The pavilion covered two hectares (Geppert 2010p. 165). Vijayaraghavacharya warned that an undue preponderance of arts and crafts would suggest India had little large industry, so the organisers limited them (Geppert 2010p. 165; Geppert 2010p. 165). Visitors could eat an all-Indian menu served by Indian waitresses, see a jungle section, and watch a theatre with magicians, jugglers, 'Tibetan devil's dancers' and a live cobra (Geppert 2010p. 165; Geppert 2010p. 165; Geppert 2010p. 165). Geppert concludes that the usual exoticism was supplied only as far as the section's general appeal required (Geppert 2010p. 165).

Burma on the route to the Government Pavilion

Between the Burmese Pagoda and the Indian Pavilion stood the Old London Bridge, which led straight to the Government Pavilion (Geppert 2010p. 161; Geppert 2010p. 161). A souvenir guide described the walk from the Burma pavilion to the bridge as a step from the East to medieval London (Geppert 2010p. 161; Geppert 2010p. 161).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — Every colony and dominion able to pay built its own palace, so the imperial theme filled the site (Greenhalgh 1988p. 57). Benedict argues that the colonial pavilions displayed power rather than reality (Benedict 1983).
  • London and Paris — The fair is read as an attempt to lessen London's inferiority complex about Paris by imperialising the capital (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 232; Geppert 2010p. 143).
  • Trade and economy — The stated emphasis was on trade, and MacKenzie reads the fair's message as empire being the cure for economic ills (Benedict 1983p. 47; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 237).
  • Schooling and race — The Bulletin of Empire Study reached schools each week with a racial hierarchy of empire (Lawn 2009p. 110; Lawn 2009p. 112).
  • Finance and failure — The two-year loss of £1,581,905 appears in several texts, although others say nearly £2 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 234; Allwood et al. 2001p. 107).

Questions for study

  1. Benedict warns that admissions are not the same as separate visitors. How should historians use a total of 27 million visitors when judging the fair's reach? (Benedict 1983p. 61)
  2. Geppert and Roche give different accounts of the government's role in financing the fair. What evidence would settle the question? (Geppert 2010p. 142; Roche 2000p. 61)
  3. Grosvenor reads the school programme as teaching racial hierarchy, while Greenhalgh reads the displays of living people as more guarded. Which reading fits the evidence better? (Lawn 2009p. 122; Greenhalgh 1988p. 95)
  4. India refused to fund a second season in 1925. What does that suggest about how colonies took part in these exhibitions? (Geppert 2010p. 165)
  5. Hoffenberg argues that exhibition architecture expressed national character. Does that hold for Wembley, where many pavilions had British architects? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 276; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 233)
  6. Was the imperial message at Wembley a sign of renewed strength or of decline? (Geppert 2010p. 177; Greenhalgh 1988p. 158)
  7. Geppert's two million schoolchildren (Geppert 2010p. 169) and the 120,000 children at the government pavilion's morning shows (Geppert 2010p. 160) count different things. What would a historian need to know about each count before using either figure for the schools' role?
  8. Benedict's Gold Coast reviewer found the people shown in Western clothes disappointing (Benedict 1983p. 48). The Daily News guide said that a compound inside the Walled City offered a faithful copy of native life (Geppert 2010p. 167). What did the organisers think visitors were being shown?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
9Between the Warsp. 96

Allwood surveys the exhibitions of the 1920s and 1930s. It begins with the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where the stadium, the Neverstop Railway and the amusement park are described, and where advertising designers, more than architects, gave the event its character. The chapter moves to Paris 1925, whose Art Deco name it traces, and to Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau, which was fenced off and nearly denied its prize. Barcelona 1929 and Mies van der Rohe's German pavilion, with its Barcelona chair, follow, along with the 1928 convention that tried to limit how often exhibitions were held. The argument is that these events were increasingly designed for commerce and display rather than education, and that their losses mounted. Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, with its science pavilions and the Sky Ride, is treated at length. The chapter ends with Paris 1937, where the German and Soviet pavilions faced one another across the Champ de Mars, and where the approach of war ended the exhibition's run.

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
1Introduction: How to Read an Expositionp. 1

Geppert opens with a 1896 review by Georg Simmel of the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung, which he uses as a model for reading an exposition as a medium rather than a mirror of national identity. The book studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931, among them the Exposition Universelle of 1900, the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 and the British Empire Exhibition of 1924-25. Geppert argues that these events formed overlapping transnational networks, since organizers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and 'old town' sections from one another, and that the 'identity' framework of earlier work is too simple. He sets out a method of space, time and personae, drawing on Lefebvre and Chartier, and surveys the rise of exposition studies since the 1980s. The introduction admits that its choice of cases is partial, and it closes by promising five virtual visits that compare London, Paris and the would-be world city of Berlin.

5Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolisp. 134Asia

Geppert examines the British Empire Exhibition of 1924 and 1925 at Wembley, and the stadium built for it, treating the site as a suburban metropolis rather than a simple national display. The chapter opens with the 1990s plan to demolish the stadium, then recovers a forgotten layer of the ground: Watkin's Folly, an unfinished imitation of the Eiffel Tower abandoned in 1895, which ties Wembley to the London–Paris rivalry. Using trademark labels registered in Paris and Marseilles from 1886 to 1940 and the colonial sections of the fairs in those cities, the book compares sub-Saharan Africans, North Africans and Indochinese. Part I (1886–1913) covers the empire, racial theory and the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900; Part II (1914–1940) covers the Marseilles and Paris expositions of 1922, 1931 and 1937. A final chapter treats France's self-image as parent and brother to its colonial children. Hale argues that the images stressed human difference and French superiority, and that this framework still shapes France's ties with its former colonies.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are central to the book, but almost entirely through Indochina, one of the three colonial groups the study is built around. Chapters 4 ('Indochinese: Gentle Subjects') and 7 ('Indochinese: Fils Doué') are devoted to it, covering trademarks and the 1900, 1922, 1931 and 1937 fairs. Chapter 1 covers Indochina's conquest and its pavilions at the 1889 Paris fair, and chapter 8 treats the Indochinese as part of France's self-presentation. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion.
