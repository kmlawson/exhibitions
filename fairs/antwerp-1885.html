Antwerp Universal Exposition

Exposition universelle d'Anvers
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Antwerp Universal Exposition (Exposition universelle d'Anvers) opened on 2 May 1885 on the south side of Antwerp and closed on 2 November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). A temporary company of Antwerp merchants ran it, with the city's backing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84). It was Belgium's first international exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 184) and drew 3.5 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85). It included a Congo pavilion and a village of Africans, which Stanard reads as part of Belgium's promotion of empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The idea came from Antwerp's bourgeoisie after visits to the 1883 Amsterdam fair, which had a colonial theme (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84). In January 1884 leading merchants founded the temporary company Naamloze Maatschappij der Wereldtentoonstelling van Antwerpen to run the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84). Hardy dates the committee to 21 January 1884 and says industrialists and shipbuilders urged it on (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Stanard gives three aims: civic self-promotion, promoting Belgium as an industrialised country, and presenting Antwerp as a historic shipping centre (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84). Hardy says the fair welcomed commerce and, above all, navigation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). Prina says the new quays, completed in 1885, provided an opportunity to hold a world's fair, and that the fair promoted the seaport, commerce and art (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56).

Organisers and finance

Prina names three organisers: the civil servant Eduard Colinet, the shipbroker and councillor Frans Gittens, and the architect Jan Laurent Hasse (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56). She says the fair was organised under the aegis of the State but realised through private initiative (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56).

Victor Lynen presided over the executive committee and Auguste Beernaert was director of the central committee (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419). Hardy says Beernaert, then president of the Council of Ministers, accepted the post of director of the committee (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Stanard puts the budget at about 4 million Belgian francs. He says the fair broke even, that the city's contribution was substantial, and that the province gave only a token sum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85). Hardy gives the cost as 4 million francs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Site and buildings

The texts do not agree on the site. Stanard places the fairgrounds in 't Zuid, a new and undeveloped quarter south of the centre near the southern railway station, covering 22 hectares (54 acres) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85). Hardy gives 54.3 acres along the Escaut near the Gare du Sud, and says some railway station buildings housed the machinery exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). Prina says the site was thirty hectares cleared by demolishing the old Citadel (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56).

The buildings were spread out. Stanard names the Hall of Machinery and the Hall of Industry as the main attractions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Hardy says the exhibits were housed in several pavilions rather than one central building, and that the grand facade rose 66 metres (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). Prina describes the monumental entrance gate by Gédéon Bourdiau, a temporary structure of stucco and wooden plaques over an iron frame (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56). A garden by Louis Fuchs had fountains and follies that held tourist pavilions and company displays (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56).

Exhibits and attractions

Stanard and Hardy both give 14,472 exhibitors, of whom 3,411 were Belgian (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). Stanard says thirty-two foreign nations took part, but only 25 as official participants (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85). Hardy's bibliography refers to twenty-six participating countries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 92).

Stanard describes telephone lines that carried live concerts from Brussels, some 50 km away (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Hardy adds that Queen Marie-Henriette had a telephone link set up to Ostend, where she heard Faust on 7 September 1885 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 92).

Italy's section, designed by Camillo Riccio, who had designed the Turin buildings, had 665 producers, each with a site 8 m wide and 4 m high (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 57). Prina says 570 of the 665 Italian exhibitors won awards (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 59). The Italian fine art was in a separate pavilion and drew less interest than the decorative arts (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 63).

Empire and colony

The Congo is the colonial element the texts describe in most detail. Stanard says the decision to show the Congo was partly influenced by the 1883 Amsterdam fair, and that the Berlin Conference had recognised Leopold II's authority in central Africa. In July 1885 Leopold declared himself sovereign of the Etat Indépendant du Congo at Boma (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Prina says the fair publicised the Congo Free State's recognition with a pavilion in the gardens, and it also held Congo Free State objects and French and Portuguese colonial pavilions (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56). Hardy says France and its colonies made up nearly a third of the exhibits, 4,361 in all (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Stanard describes the Congo pavilion as modest. The Société Royale Belge de Géographie organised its ethnographic section, and the Musée Commercial of Brussels also contributed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Hardy says the Congo "could not afford its own section" and provided ethnographic exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91). Stanard writes that Chief Massola of Vivi and twelve other Africans were displayed as a village and asked to pose for photographs, and that Leopold II visited them and invited Massola to the palace at Laeken (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). The texts say nothing about how the Africans themselves saw the display.

Stanard argues that the era of Belgian world's fairs (1885-1958) matches the period of Belgian rule in central Africa, so that exhibitions helped promote empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). He presents the 1885 Congo display as the first of a practice that continued to the end of Belgian imperialism: The display of the Congo in 1885 was the first instance of a practice that would continue (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). The goals that Stanard lists for the fair are civic, industrial and maritime, and the colonial reading is his argument (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84).

Visitors and reception

Stanard gives 3.5 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85), and Depelchin's note gives the same figure (Filipová 2015p. 197). Hardy prints 32 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91), which the other texts do not support. Prina says the Italian section was one of the most visited and most rewarded at the fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 58). She also reports that Belgian official publications praised it (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 59).

Its place in the series

Depelchin says the Antwerp exhibition was the first to be associated with the label 'universal' (Filipová 2015p. 184). He lists the later Belgian shows as Brussels in 1888, Antwerp in 1894, Brussels in 1897, Liège in 1905, Brussels in 1910 and Ghent in 1913 (Filipová 2015p. 184). Stanard says the 1958 exposition was the ninth and last of Belgium's universal and international expositions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Prina calls the Antwerp fair Belgium's first world's fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 55).

The 1894 Antwerp exposition was a separate event, although it used the same site (Findling & Pelle 2008); Allwood lists the two as separate fairs (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). In 1896 a German journalist cited the Antwerp fair of 1885, along with London 1886 and Chicago 1893, as a fair that had already staged native displays, so that such displays had lost their novelty (Geppert 2010p. 48). Depelchin compares the Ghent fair of 1913 with the earlier exhibitions at Antwerp, Brussels and Liège (Filipová 2015p. 186).

How historians have interpreted it

Prina presents the Italian displays as a benchmark that Belgians used for their own design reform (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 60), and reports that Fumière described the fair as a mirror of contemporary questions and social problems (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 60). She frames her study as a contribution to understanding Italian identity after unification (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 10). The editors say her chapter concentrates on the official views of fairs and on government-sponsored design education (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 10).

Stanard reads the Congo display as part of Belgian imperial promotion (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86). Hardy's entry offers little interpretation and treats the fair mainly as a commercial and maritime event (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Empire and the Congo — The Congo is the colonial element the texts describe in detail; Stanard reads it as imperial promotion, while Hardy says the Congo "could not afford its own section" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86).
  • Port and commerce — The texts stress shipping, trade and the new quays, and the fair's aim of promoting the seaport (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56) (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).
  • Design reform and national identity — Prina reads the Italian section as a model for Belgian design reform and as a means of forging national identity (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 60) (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 57).
  • Civic self-promotion — Stanard describes the fair as an effort at municipal self-promotion by Antwerp's bourgeoisie (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84).
  • Displayed people — Africans from the Congo were displayed as a village and asked to pose for photographs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 86).
  • Disputed facts — The texts disagree on the site, the Congo pavilion and the visitor count (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91).

Questions for study

  1. Stanard's reading makes the fair part of empire, but the goals he lists are civic, industrial and maritime. What evidence in the texts would show that the organisers meant the Congo display to serve empire? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 84)
  2. The texts place the fairground differently: on the old Citadel, in 't Zuid, and near the Gare du Sud. How might the choice of site have shaped what the fair could show? (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 56; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91)
  3. Hardy's figure of 32 million visitors differs from 3.5 million elsewhere. How would you test which figure is right? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 85)
  4. The Italian section was praised and well awarded, while the Congo's own section was not affordable. What does this suggest about who could afford to display at Antwerp? (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 58; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 91)

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Introduction: communities real and imagined: world’s fairs and political meaningsDavid Raizman and Ethan Robeyp. 1

Raizman and Robey introduce the volume by asking how the world's fairs of 1851 to 1915 used manufactured goods to express national identity. They argue that earlier design history treated fairs as a foil to modernism, and that the fairs were networks of exchange rather than single, unified displays. Pevsner's dismissal of the Crystal Palace is challenged by later critics, from Banham and Sparke to Greenhalgh, Bennett and Mitchell, the last three reading the fairs as imperial spectacle. The editors use Anderson's 'imagined communities' to tie national identity to material culture. Their evidence is the Albert Memorial, Owen Jones's interior and Grammar of Ornament, and the contributors' cases: the 1888 Copenhagen Nordic Exhibition, the Nanjing and South American centenaries of 1910, Italy at Brussels and Antwerp, and the Finnish pavilion at Paris in 1900. They end with the outbreak of World War I, when the ideal of 'peaceful competition' broke down.

In the contents of this work

3The Belgian reception of Italy at the 1885 Antwerp World Exhibition: converging artistic, economic, and political strategies on displayDaniela N. Prinap. 50

Prina examines how Italy and Belgium, two young nation-states, each tried to link art, industry and education, and how the Italian government used foreign models to reform its own system. The starting point is a mission of 1880 by Baldassarre Odescalchi and Raffaele Erculei, who inspected the Brussels Jubilee Exhibition and the art-industry institutions of Belgium. The chapter then follows Italy's display at the Turin exhibition of 1884, notably the Borgo Medievale that Alfredo d'Andrade built as a recreation of medieval Piedmont, and its large showing at the Antwerp world's fair of 1885, where 570 of the 665 Italian exhibitors received awards. A Belgian architect, Théophile Fumière, wrote a book on the Italian section that praised Italian ceramics and the regional school system as a model for Belgian design reform. Prina ends with the Brussels decorative arts school and museum of the 1880s and with Italian interest in Belgian religious art and Art Nouveau, leading towards the Turin decorative arts exhibition of 1902.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
8The Ghent Universal and International Exhibition of 1913: Reconciling Historicism, Modernity and ExoticismDavy Depelchinp. 183

Depelchin asks why the Ghent exhibition of 1913, which was formally universal and international, gave so little prominence to the exotic, which usually featured so strongly in universal exhibitions. He places Ghent within Belgium's run of international exhibitions from Antwerp in 1885 to Ghent itself, and within the city's need to define itself through local history. The chapter then describes the fairground: the white Beaux-Arts Court of Honour, the 'Old Flanders' and 'Modern Village' sections that promoted architectural regionalism, and the Belgian city pavilions in historical styles. Its central case is the colonial and non-Western pavilions. Persia had the only prominent orientalist building on the Avenue of Nations, the Congo pavilion took a hybrid style, and the Moroccan, Tunisian and Algerian pavilions were pushed into a wooded area and presented as trading showcases. The French colonial displays were more commercial than picturesque. Depelchin concludes that the organizers' emphasis on local history suppressed exoticism, and that Ghent reshaped the universal exhibition to fit local ambitions.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

Wikipedia

The Antwerp International Exposition (Dutch: Wereldtentoonstelling van Antwerpen; French: Exposition internationale d'Anvers) of 1885 was a world's fair held in Antwerp, Belgium, between 2 May and 2 November 1885. It covered 54.3 acres (220,000 m2), attracted 3.5 million visits and broke even after spending 4 million Belgian francs There were 25 official participating nations including: Austria, Canada, France, Germany, Great Britain, the Ottoman Empire, Portugal, Serbia, Spain, Romania, the United States and some South American states. Australian wool growers sent exhibitions and won prizes. Taking place 20 years after the accession of King Leopold II, and the same year as the creation of the Congo Free State, the fair was the first in which a Congolese village was displayed, a feature that also appeared in the later 1897 Brussels fair.
