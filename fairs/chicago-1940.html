American Negro Exposition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The American Negro Exposition was held in Chicago in 1940, at the Chicago Coliseum, as a display of African American achievement. James Washington, a real-estate developer, first planned it in 1937, and a group of African American leaders took it over in early 1940 (Rydell 1993p. 187). Federal agencies and Secretary of Agriculture Henry Wallace helped stage it, and it drew nearly 250,000 visitors (Rydell 1993p. 188p. 190). The texts give neither its opening nor its closing dates (Rydell 1993p. 188).

Background and aims

The exposition grew out of a grievance. Chicago's African American community resented the lack of African American exhibits at the 1933-34 Century of Progress fair, and in 1937 Washington began planning a diamond jubilee for the seventy-fifth anniversary of the Emancipation Proclamation (Rydell 1993p. 187). Illinois appropriated $75,000 for it (Rydell 1993p. 188). Rydell says the organisers wanted to show African American cultural and economic achievements and to underscore African American patriotism (Rydell 1993p. 189). The emphasis, he writes, was on achievement and on the chance for African Americans to share in the nation's future prosperity (Rydell 1993p. 190).

Organisers and finance

Washington's Afra-Merican Exposition Authority faced problems over the site and charges that the fair was being organised for private gain (Rydell 1993p. 188). In early 1940 prominent African Americans replaced Washington with Truman K. Gibson, a young attorney, renamed the corporation the Diamond Jubilee Exposition Authority, and arranged to hold the fair at the Chicago Coliseum (Rydell 1993p. 188). With less than four months to the opening, the board turned to the federal government. A delegation that included Claude Barnett met Wallace, whose endorsement brought in other departments (Rydell 1993p. 188). Federal funds came through a congressional appropriation (Rydell 1993p. 188). The texts do not give the budget or the total cost.

Empire and colony

The texts show no imperial or colonial dimension to this exhibition (Rydell 1993p. 187p. 188). Rydell says the organisers sought fuller inclusion as citizens in the nation's culture of abundance, in exchange for African American support for the Roosevelt administration's war effort (Rydell 1993p. 191).

Exhibits and attractions

Federal agencies supplied displays, guides and staff. The Department of Labor showed charts on African American employment, and the Postal Service booth featured a stamp commemorating Booker T. Washington (Rydell 1993p. 188). The City of Chicago showed a model of the Ida B. Wells Housing Project, and Illinois showed the number of African Americans who had held state office (Rydell 1993p. 189). Organisers installed a replica of Lincoln's tomb with dioramas of African American history, and mounted church and school exhibits, art, and literature (Rydell 1993p. 189). Corporate exhibits included a Firestone miniature rubber plant within the Liberian national exhibit, and an Edison model kitchen staffed by African American women (Rydell 1993p. 190). E. Franklin Frazier arranged dioramas that illustrated his book The Negro Family in the United States (Rydell 1993p. 190). The Du Sable replica cabin, built for the 1933 fair, was among the exhibits (Ganz 2008).

Visitors and reception

Rydell says the exposition drew "250,000 visitors—far below the expectations of its sponsors" (Rydell 1993p. 190). He compares it with a Chicago exposition of 1915 that marked the fiftieth anniversary of emancipation (Rydell 1993p. 190). Critics objected that an ice-cream concession went to a Chicago firm that refused to hire African Americans, that a white-run firm printed the programmes, and that the organisers removed a birth-control exhibit after the Catholic Church threatened to withdraw (Rydell 1993p. 190). Rydell's note cites a New York Times report of 8 July 1940 on the cancelled exhibit (Rydell 1993p. 257).

Politics and controversy

The Communist Fighting Worker went further: "The Exposition was arranged and financed by the New Deal and its Negro henchmen" (Rydell 1993p. 191). A note cites a 1 October 1940 article in the same paper that called the exposition a front for New Deal war plans (Rydell 1993p. 257). Rydell reads the exposition as a failure of radical nerve: "Clearly, the American Negro Exposition failed to mount a radical critique of the dominant culture" (Rydell 1993p. 191). At the closing ceremonies Wallace declared, "There is no better way to rearm democracy" (Rydell 1993p. 191). Rydell calls his guarantee against discrimination manipulative but also remarkable (Rydell 1993p. 191).

Aftermath and legacy

After the 1933-34 fair, an unsuccessful campaign for the Chicago Park District to run the Du Sable replica cabin as a permanent memorial ended, and the cabin reappeared at the 1940 exposition (Ganz 2008). Ganz spells the name Du Sable, while the society's booklet spells it De Saible (Ganz 2008). Ganz's note cites a Chicago Tribune article of 18 August 1940 on the Du Sable Society showing the cabin at the Negro fair (Ganz 2008). Rydell ends by asking whether the federal government could be trusted to end discrimination if African Americans joined another war (Rydell 1993p. 192).

Its place in the series

Rydell connects the Chicago exposition with the 1933-34 fair and with the Dallas fair of five years earlier, where the organisers also turned to the federal government (Rydell 1993p. 187p. 188). He also ties it to the anger over the Negro Week at the American Common in the 1939-40 New York fair, where some African American editors condemned the Negro Week and noted that African American employment at the fair had stayed insignificant; he says this anger pushed federal officials to act in Chicago (Rydell 1993p. 186p. 187).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Federal sponsorship — Rydell describes federal help as decisive in 1940, with Wallace's endorsement and federal agency displays, and reads the same help as a bid for African American support for the war (Rydell 1993p. 188p. 191)
  • Accommodation or protest — Rydell finds the exposition accommodationist, while critics on the left called it an instrument of the New Deal (Rydell 1993p. 191)
  • Representing achievement — the exhibits showed African American history, art, literature and labour, with African American women as demonstrators in the Edison kitchen (Rydell 1993p. 189)
  • Attendance and reception — nearly 250,000 visitors, below the organisers' expectations, and critics' objections over concessions and the birth-control exhibit (Rydell 1993p. 190)
  • Afterlife of objects — the Du Sable replica cabin passed from the 1933 fair to the 1940 exposition (Ganz 2008)

Questions for study

  1. Rydell says in his introduction that the Roosevelt administration sponsored the exposition, yet he also describes a Black-led project that federal agencies later joined (Rydell 1993p. 158p. 188). How far can one fair be both?
  2. The texts do not state what the sponsors expected to draw, only that attendance fell far below it (Rydell 1993p. 190). What evidence would show the expectation, and who would have set it?
  3. Wallace promised to fight discrimination, and Rydell calls the promise both manipulative and remarkable (Rydell 1993p. 191). What would a historian need to judge whether it changed anything?
  4. The Du Sable cabin was refused as a permanent memorial and then shown at a fair of African American achievement (Ganz 2008). What does its path suggest about where African American history was displayed in Chicago?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
SixAfrican Americans in the World of Tomorrowp. 157

Rydell examines how African Americans tried to turn the white supremacist world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s into arenas for social change. He argues that each struggle over representation reflected shifting class and political alignments within the African-American community, as older Republican elites gave way to labour organisers and radical intellectuals. The chapter moves through five fairs: Philadelphia's 1926 Sesquicentennial, where A. Philip Randolph gave an opening-day address amid protests over a planned Ku Klux Klan presence; Chicago's 1933-34 Century of Progress, where the NAACP and a state anti-discrimination law confronted concessionaires; Dallas's 1936 Texas Centennial, where a federally funded Hall of Negro Life embodied an accommodationist compromise and was later demolished; New York's 1939-40 World's Fair, where picket lines met official denials of discrimination; and the 1940 Chicago American Negro Exposition, which federal agencies helped stage. Evidence comes from newspapers, official correspondence and interviews. Rydell concludes that the fairs were battlegrounds rather than mere showcases, though sponsors repeatedly steered reform towards accommodation.

Brown, Kendall H. “Fair Japan: Japanese Gardens at American World’s Fairs, 1876–1940.” Journal article.
Performing on the World Stagep. 14Asia

Brown argues that Japanese gardens at world's fairs were staged performances, not neutral displays of beauty. Japan's official image split into a modern, industrial pavilion and a timeless, feminine garden. Brown ties this to fairs timed around Japan's wars, from Chicago in 1893 to San Francisco and New York in 1939 and 1940. The gardens, he concludes, naturalized a politically charged message, presenting Japan's imperial present as the natural expression of an unchanging culture.

Cotter, Bill, and Bill Young. The 1964–1965 New York World’s Fair: Creation and Legacy. Charleston, SC: Arcadia, 2008.
1Why a Fair?p. 9

Cotter and Young trace the fair to a 1959 proposal by New York businessmen led by Thomas J. Deegan to mark the city's 300th anniversary. They argue that New York won federal backing over Seattle, Los Angeles, Chicago and Washington largely through politics, and reproduce a 1959 New York Times report. The chapter ends with Robert Moses being chosen to head the corporation, which the authors present as his chance to finish the park left unfinished after the 1939-1940 fair.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
EAppendix E: Fairs That Never Werep. 428

This appendix to the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions lists expositions that were planned but never held, from New York 1913 and Baltimore 1914 to Calgary 2005, each as a short history. It does not argue a thesis; it records how each proposal was made, which sites and themes were chosen, and where it stopped. The causes recur: world wars and the Great Depression, economic slumps, doubts that a city could afford the cost, governments and mayors withdrawing support, and rival bids that won the BIE vote. Several entries are detailed, notably Philadelphia 1976, Los Angeles 1981, Paris 1989, Chicago 1992, Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, which trace the bidding, financing and political fights. Some entries show a fair's afterlife, such as Tokyo 1940 tickets honored at Osaka 1970. The appendix ends with Calgary's defeat by Aichi for 2005.

In the contents of this work

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Introductionp. 1

Maloney introduces the book's subject: the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs between 1850 and 1940, and their lasting influence on public parks and gardens. He argues that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, from Jackson Park in Chicago to Flushing Meadows in New York, and that the need to create mature landscapes in a few months drove advances in tree transplantation, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The chapter traces the spread of the fair from London in 1851 to the United States, where cities with room to grow offered campus-like sites, and notes the designers, nurserymen and horticultural societies who shaped these fairs. It then explains the criteria for choosing the nine featured fairs, from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and sets out a table of their sites, attendance and finances. Maloney concludes that understanding the original design intent of these landscapes may help people appreciate and preserve the surviving remnants.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
Two“Fitter Families for Future Firesides”: Eugenics Exhibitions between the Warsp. 38

Rydell shows how American eugenicists used exhibitions between the wars to turn race betterment into a popular cause. The chapter begins with the Race Betterment Foundation's booth and pageant at the 1915 San Francisco fair, then moves to the Second International Congress of Eugenics, held at the American Museum of Natural History in New York in 1921. Henry Laughlin's congress exhibits, including racial casts, pedigree charts and anthropometric instruments, went after the congress to a House committee on immigration, which Rydell treats as a direct route from display to policy. The central case is the 'Fitter Families' contest that Mary Watts and Florence Brown Sherbon began at the 1920 Kansas Free Fair, where families were examined and scored and the scorecards went to Charles Davenport. The contest spread to state and county fairs, and a mobile tent exhibit carried it further. The chapter closes with the 1932 Third Congress, the four small eugenics panels at Chicago in 1933, and the 'Typical American Families' display at the 1940 New York fair. Rydell concludes that eugenics was a subterranean strand of the exhibition culture rather than a fringe doctrine.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.

The sentence uses the cabin's failed bid to become a permanent memorial to show what became of the fair's objects, and Ganz gives no account of the 1940 exposition itself.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Ganz's book is a history of A Century of Progress, the 1933-34 Chicago fair, which was privately financed during the Depression to revive the city's economy and reputation. Its argument is that the organisers used cooperative science exhibits and the idea of progress to generate optimism, presenting technology rather than people as the driver of progress, while Midway entertainment, women, African Americans, ethnic communities and aviation pressed their own meanings on the fair. Chapters move from the Sally Rand shows and the Chicago boosters who financed the fair to its architecture and corporate exhibits, then to women's spaces, the Du Sable cabin, ethnic pavilions and the Graf Zeppelin and stratosphere balloon, and end with an epilogue on the fair's objects and afterlife.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia have a slight place in this book, which is concerned with Chicago, its Midway and its organisers rather than with foreign participation. The only Asian national presence named is a Japanese Pavilion, mentioned in the epilogue as the source of items bid for in the fair's sale of surplus (a phonograph, a rug and lights). Tokyo enters the introduction only as one of the cities that surpassed Chicago in size. Chapter 7, Ethnic Identity and Nationalistic Representations of Progress, concerns European immigrant communities, Mexico and Italy, and has no Asian national section. Chapter 1, Sally Rand and the Midway, holds nearly all the remaining material, and there the Asian reference is mostly a concession name. The Oriental Village was one of the Midway's attractions, where Little Egypt recreated her Streets of Cairo show, where Rand moved her production in 1934, and where the mayor judged Hot Cha San's dance acceptable in 1933. The book does not say that these performances were Asian in origin. The 'Siamese' twins listed among the Living Wonders freak show are named as one of the displayed bodies, with no discussion of where they came from. Southeast Asia is otherwise absent, and the book does not discuss Asian visitors or exhibitions held in Asia.

The American Negro Exposition, also known as the Black World's Fair and the Diamond Jubilee Exposition, was a world's fair held in Chicago from July until September in 1940, to celebrate the 75th anniversary (also known as a diamond jubilee) of the end of slavery in the United States at the conclusion of the Civil War in 1865.
