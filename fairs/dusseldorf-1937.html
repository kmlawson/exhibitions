Reich Exhibition 'Schaffendes Volk'

Reichsausstellung 'Schaffendes Volk'
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

Schaffendes Volk was the Reichsausstellung held at Düsseldorf in Germany in 1937, and it opened in May that year (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33; Kargon et al. 2015p. 34). It began as an art and industry exhibition planned by the German Werkbund, and the Nazi Party took it over in 1935 under Hermann Göring (Kargon et al. 2015p. 35). It was billed as international, yet no foreign nation had its own pavilion (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31; Kargon et al. 2015p. 33). Students should study it because it sold consumer goods, synthetics and colonial claims as parts of the Four-Year Plan (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31; Kargon et al. 2015p. 47).

Background and aims

The Werkbund conceived the exhibition before Hitler came to power in 1933, as a showcase for German design and for partnership between manufacturers and designers (Kargon et al. 2015p. 35). Its first form was a housing exhibition, and the organisers had only seven months to turn it into a showpiece for the regime's economic agenda (Kargon et al. 2015p. 36). In 1935 the Nazi Party placed it under the patronage of Hermann Göring, who was also in charge of the Four-Year Plan, and it took the name Schaffendes Volk after a public competition (Kargon et al. 2015p. 35). The plan aimed to halve Germany's import bill so that foreign currency could buy raw materials for rearmament (Kargon et al. 2015p. 43). The fair presented the plan as a promise of consumer plenty drawn from German materials (Kargon et al. 2015p. 44). The chapter notes that the title was rendered at the time as A People at Work, which it says understates the sense of productive and creative labour in the German verb schaffen (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31).

The timing was aimed at Paris. The fair opened in the same month as the Paris Exposition Internationale, and the chapter says the timing was deliberate despite the organisers' denials (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33). The New York Times reported before the opening that "a vast German fair was being built in secret" as a rival to the Paris exposition (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33). To secure its success, the Werberat, which controlled exhibitions and trade fairs, banned all other competing German industrial exhibitions from 1935 to 1939, including a planned Dortmund exhibition for 1937 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33).

Organisers and finance

The commissioner was E. W. Maiwald, who wrote the official illustrated book and the final report (Kargon et al. 2015p. 34; Kargon et al. 2015p. 41; Kargon et al. 2015p. 154). The chapter names the Werberat der Deutschen Wirtschaft as the body that regulated every advertisement's copy and image (Kargon et al. 2015p. 54). Göring spoke at the opening and said the Four-Year Plan began a new technological era (Kargon et al. 2015p. 44). The passages read say nothing about the cost of the fair or how it was financed.

Site and buildings

The New York Times writer, as the chapter reports, counted over forty exhibition halls, thirty pavilions, twenty restaurants and cafes and a huge amusement park, and the grounds stretched more than a mile along the Rhine (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33). Individual halls included the steel and iron pavilion, with an open span of eighty-five metres (Kargon et al. 2015p. 38), and the Cement Association building by Fritz Faulenbach, with a cantilevered roof ten metres high (Kargon et al. 2015p. 38). The Mannesmann pavilion was by Emil Fahrenkamp (Kargon et al. 2015p. 38). The Ehrenhalle des werktätigen Volkes, the honorary hall at the entrance, was designed by the Association of German Engineers with the architect Gerhard Graubner and the artists M. Janssen and W. Geißler (Kargon et al. 2015p. 43; Kargon et al. 2015p. 44). Beside the grounds a housing estate of 84 single-family houses was built, eight of them as show homes, but most houses were not finished when the fair opened (Kargon et al. 2015p. 50; Kargon et al. 2015p. 51).

Exhibits and attractions

The Ehrenhalle presented four pillars filled with coal, wood, iron, and stone and earth, under the heading Wunder der Technik (Kargon et al. 2015p. 44). The synthetic rubber Buna of IG Farben was shown as a wonder of German ingenuity (Kargon et al. 2015p. 47). The textile section was the largest on synthetics, with rayon, acetate and synthetic silk (Kargon et al. 2015p. 52). The Demag pavilion had a façade fresco by Fritz Buchholz showing workers and an engineer (Kargon et al. 2015p. 46). A Coca-Cola bottling plant stood near the centre, where visitors watched four thousand bottles an hour being filled through a glass wall (Kargon et al. 2015p. 53). The chapter notes that the Italian designer Sironi is said by another scholar to have worked on an Italian exhibition at the fair, but the author found no evidence it took place (Kargon et al. 2015p. 154).

Empire and colony

The texts show a clear colonial strand. The Colonial Exhibition opened on the grounds of the Düsseldorf zoo one week after the fair, organised to coincide with a meeting of the Reichskolonialbund (Reich Colonial League). The league also had an exhibition hall on the main grounds (Kargon et al. 2015p. 47). The author reads the placement at a zoo as showing "the dehumanizing and racist logic of colonialism thrived under the Third Reich" (Kargon et al. 2015p. 48). At the opening, Admiral Rüman, the league's chief executive, said Germany was the rightful owner of its former colonies, which he said the Treaty of Versailles had taken, and said both exhibitions showed the importance of colonies for raw materials (Kargon et al. 2015p. 48). The Deutscher Lebensraum pavilion argued for German expansion to the east. Lebensraum was described as a plan to "redistribute people and work" by occupying land where German settlements could be founded (Kargon et al. 2015p. 48). Its three-dimensional map of Germany, with nine further maps and photomurals covering four hundred metres of wall, served as the centrepiece (Kargon et al. 2015p. 49). The chapter does not name the colonies or the colonised peoples shown at the colonial exhibition, and it describes no colonised or Indigenous people taking part. The introduction notes that fairs from the late nineteenth century showed conquered peoples, but it does not discuss this fair in those terms (Kargon et al. 2015p. 4).

Women, consumers and synthetics

The fair addressed women as the key consumers. Its displays, décor and wall texts spoke to them, and the German Women's Bureau (Deutsches Frauenwerk) ran a mothers' school there (Kargon et al. 2015p. 51). The chapter notes that the state offered newly married couples interest-free loans, in the form of vouchers for household goods, if the bride gave up her job (Kargon et al. 2015p. 37). Advertising for synthetic fabric promised better clothing, yet shortages continued and the quality of synthetic clothes fell, and labels with washing instructions were banned (Kargon et al. 2015p. 52).

Design and modernism

The chapter argues that the fair used modern design and photography. Steel-tube chairs, simple tableware and other Werkbund-style goods appeared in the displays (Kargon et al. 2015p. 36). Albert Renger-Patzsch and Hugo Schmölz, who had been linked to New Objectivity, photographed the machines as aesthetic objects (Kargon et al. 2015p. 39). The chapter calls this reactionary modernism, in which modern aesthetics joined völkisch iconography and racial aims (Kargon et al. 2015p. 41; Kargon et al. 2015p. 42; Kargon et al. 2015p. 56).

Visitors and reception

The official records gave almost seven million visitors (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31), and Maiwald's final report gave 6.9 million (Kargon et al. 2015p. 44). A note gives the figures of Stefanie Schäfers, who believes the total was much lower: 4,344,028 tickets sold, about 50,000 free tickets, and 1.2 million for the amusement and garden sections (Kargon et al. 2015p. 154). The chapter gives an estimate of 250,000 to 300,000 foreign tourists and business people, and over three hundred foreign journalists (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33). The final report claimed over eight thousand guided tours in twenty-two languages (Kargon et al. 2015p. 34). The Coca-Cola plant sold half a million bottles (Kargon et al. 2015p. 54).

Its place in the series

The chapter's author says the German pavilion at the Paris exposition was retrograde. Albert Speer built it (Kargon et al. 2015p. 35), and its interior suggested a nineteenth-century parlour (Kargon et al. 2015p. 42). The chapter sets this against Maiwald's final report, which called Paris the outdated model of the world's fair (Kargon et al. 2015p. 41). The introduction lists Düsseldorf 1937 among the three built fairs of the decade that the book compares (Kargon et al. 2015p. 1). Geppert's appendix list includes the fair among European interwar expositions (Geppert 2010p. 253; Geppert 2010p. 316). The Findling and Pelle encyclopedia excludes it from its main body, placing it in an appendix of fairs that fell short of the qualifications for inclusion, with no reason given (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Consumption and sacrifice — The fair sold the German people on consumer enticement and wartime sacrifice at the same time, promising a future abundance that was never realised (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31; Kargon et al. 2015p. 55).
  • Reactionary modernism — The chapter reads modern design and photography as serving a regime that also pursued racial and anti-liberal aims (Kargon et al. 2015p. 41; Kargon et al. 2015p. 42).
  • Colonial claims and Lebensraum — Colonial and Lebensraum displays made German expansion part of the same message as raw materials and synthetics (Kargon et al. 2015p. 47; Kargon et al. 2015p. 48).
  • Women and synthetics — Women were addressed as household managers whose buying could serve autarky, and the synthetics sold to them were of poor quality (Kargon et al. 2015p. 51; Kargon et al. 2015p. 52).
  • Rivalry with Paris 1937 — The fair was timed against the Paris exposition, and its organisers claimed it set a new standard for future world's fairs (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33; Kargon et al. 2015p. 41).

Questions for study

  1. The chapter says the fair was international in name only. What does a fair need in order to count as international, and who decides? (Kargon et al. 2015p. 33)
  2. The official visitor figure is much higher than the estimate in the note. How would you test which figure is more reliable, and what evidence would you need? (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31; Kargon et al. 2015p. 154)
  3. Why did the Nazi regime give the Colonial Exhibition a zoo setting and a hall in the main grounds? What does this show about how it presented empire? (Kargon et al. 2015p. 47)
  4. How did a fair about industry and synthetics address consumers, and what did it ask them to give up? (Kargon et al. 2015p. 31; Kargon et al. 2015p. 37)

Kargon, Robert H., Karen Fiss, Morris Low and Arthur P. Molella. World’s Fairs on the Eve of War: Science, Technology, and Modernity, 1937–1942. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2015.
1Introduction: World’s Fairs, Modernity, and the Demand for Authenticityp. 1Asia

The introduction sets out a comparative study of five fairs planned in the 1930s, three built (Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939) and two planned but never opened (Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942). Its argument is that these fairs became arenas of ideological struggle over modernity, as liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define the future. It traces how world's fairs since the Crystal Palace of 1851 taught populations to accept industrial change as progress, and how after the First World War, with the older empires gone, nations sought a unifying authenticity to animate modern technology. It then sketches the international crises of 1931 to 1936, from Manchuria to the Rhineland, which made the fairs' messages more confrontational. The national messages are outlined in turn: imperial Rome, racial unity in Germany, Japan's pan-Asian mastery, and the democratic displays of France and the United States. The introduction ends by describing the fairs as propaganda contests that preceded a war of armies, and by noting that the war of ideas proved significant for its outcome.

In the contents of this work

3Fantasies of Consumption at Schaffendes Volk: National Socialism and the Four-Year Planp. 30

The chapter examines Schaffendes Volk, the 1937 'international' exhibition in Düsseldorf, as a means of steering German consumers towards the goals of the Four-Year Plan. Its argument is that the fair sold consumer pleasure and wartime sacrifice together, promising abundance from synthetics while asking Germans to accept shortages. The author works through the Ehrenhalle's raw-materials displays, the Lebensraum hall with its large relief map, and a model housing estate that was unfinished at the opening. Women are treated as the key audience, addressed through the Women's Bureau and through advertising for synthetic textiles. The Coca-Cola bottling plant and the modernist industrial design on show illustrate how commercial modernism was absorbed into Nazi propaganda. The chapter then sets the fair against the German pavilion in Paris, which it reads as retrograde, and concludes that the regime reconciled private desire with collective duty by making consumption a form of civic mobilisation.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Kargon and colleagues examine five international expositions planned in the years before the Second World War: Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939, which were built, and Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942, which were planned but never opened. The volume argues that these fairs were arenas of ideological struggle in which liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define modernity and to prepare their peoples for war. The introduction sets them against the history of fairs from the Crystal Palace of 1851 and the crises of the 1930s, and each chapter treats one fair through its planning documents and press coverage. The book closes with the first postwar universal exposition, Brussels 1958, which carried the Cold War rivalry into a fair devoted to peaceful atomic energy.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure as one case rather than as a theme. Japan is the only Asian nation with a substantial role, and its treatment is concentrated in chapter 5, "Modernity on Display: The 1940 Grand International Exposition of Japan", on the Tokyo and Yokohama exposition cancelled in 1938: the Commemoration Hall competition, the Nippon Kōbō studio, Bruno Taut's writings on Japanese architecture and the 1942 "Overcoming Modernity" symposium. Japan's participation in fairs run by others is briefer: the Paris 1937 pavilion by Sakakura Junzō (chapter 2), the New York 1939 displays (chapters 4 and 5), and the low-profile exhibit at Brussels 1958 (chapter 7). Chapter 3 mentions a visit to Düsseldorf by Prince Chichibu. Elsewhere Asia is context or comparison. Chapter 5 links the exposition to the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, the Manchukuo express "Asia", Kenzō Tange's 1942 remark that admiring Angkor Wat was "the mark of an amateur", and a Thai proposal for a Japan-Thailand cultural centre in Bangkok. China appears as the war that cancelled the Tokyo fair and, in chapter 1, as the Manchurian crisis of 1931. Apart from Japan, the passages show no East or Southeast Asian nation exhibiting at these fairs and no Asian colonial display. The only exhibition held in Asia is Osaka 1970, mentioned in passing in chapter 7.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Wikipedia

The Reichsausstellung Schaffendes Volk (The Reich's Exhibition of a Productive People) of 1937 was held in today's North Park district of Düsseldorf, Germany, along one mile of the Rhine shoreline. It was opened on May 8, 1937 by Hermann Göring. Through October of the same year it attracted about six million visitors.
