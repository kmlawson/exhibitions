New York · 1853–1854

Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations opened in a Crystal Palace on Reservoir Square in New York in July 1853 and closed in November 1854. It was the first world's fair held in the United States, an imitation of London's Great Exhibition of 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its promoters lost money, and the building burned in 1858 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). Historians use the fair to test how exhibitions copied London's model, how commerce and civic pride shaped American fairs, and how the fair displayed nations and peoples (Greenhalgh 1988; Badger 1979; Burris 2001; Finnegan & Wright 2015).

Origins and aims

Badger calls the fair an obvious attempt to copy London's success (Badger 1979). Steen's entries describe it as America's first world's fair, inspired by London's Crystal Palace and promoted mainly by New Yorkers (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Horace Greeley proposed a similar structure in a Tribune article of May 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Burris places a Greeley letter to the same paper in the first month of the London exhibition (Burris 2001).

Salt places the impetus with the exhibitors and promoters of London 1851, who wanted more outlets for their goods (Finnegan & Wright 2015). Salt also records that Edward Riddle, the United States commissioner to the London Crystal Palace, obtained permission from the New York Board of Aldermen, for a small fee, to use Reservoir Park. Investors, among them William Cullen Bryant, later took the project over (Finnegan & Wright 2015). Mandell calls the New York venture a small affair, really an expansion of a planned national industrial exhibit, though invitations went out worldwide (Mandell 1967).

Organisation and finance

Burris describes the fair as a profit-seeking enterprise run by a group of New York businessmen (Burris 2001). On 3 January 1852 the city granted the promoters a five-year lease on Reservoir Square for one dollar a year. The conditions were that the building be of iron and glass, and that no single entrance charge exceed fifty cents (Allwood et al. 2001). The United States Secretary of State had the building classed as a bonded warehouse, a store where imported goods wait without duty paid, and approached foreign nations on its behalf (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the site was almost free, but the fair suffered from rivalry between states and had no official government backing (Allwood et al. 2001). The British government's direct outlay on the fair, under the Royal Commission, was £2,432 (Hoffenberg 2001).

The venture lost money from the start and was more than $100,000 in debt by the end of 1853 (Allwood et al. 2001). Theodore Sedgwick resigned as president in January 1854. P. T. Barnum took over, announced that the exhibition would become permanent, and resigned on 10 July 1854 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Burris 2001).

The texts give different sums. Steen gives a closing debt of about $300,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990), as do Burris (Burris 2001) and Allwood, Allan and Reid, who say "over $300,000" (Allwood et al. 2001). Badger gives receipts of $340,000 against costs of $640,000 (Badger 1979). Rydell, Findling and Pelle give losses of some $340,000 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and the statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives a loss of $340,000, or £70,103 (Allwood et al. 2001). Our texts do not explain the gap.

Edward Riddle, an auctioneer from Massachusetts, came back from London and approached P. T. Barnum, who was not interested at the time (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell, Findling and Pelle explain the lack of public money: the federal government, caught in the sectional crisis, would not sponsor the fair as the royal commission had done in London (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Burris adds that when Theodore Sedgwick resigned, the original stockholders sold their interests and left the losses to a second tier of investors (Burris 2001p. 92).

Barnum's remedies failed in a particular way. Allwood, Allan and Reid say that for four months he tried everything in his repertoire of attractions to clear the loss (Allwood et al. 2001p. 24). Burris says that his musical performances took place in a hall so large and so poor in acoustics that patrons at the back could hear nothing (Burris 2001p. 92).

Admission was costly for working families. Burris says that many working-class New Yorkers paid fifty cents when they might earn ten dollars in a week, so that a family of four entering twice spent half a week's wages (Burris 2001p. 91). Hackney carriages charged a dollar and a half and more, against the twenty-five cents that Londoners paid two years earlier (Burris 2001p. 91).

Site and architecture

Madison Square was the first choice, but local residents and businesses halted the plan (Burris 2001). The fair took Reservoir Square instead. It lay far from the city centre, and the reservoir made a poor backdrop (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001).

Several architects submitted designs (Allwood et al. 2001). Steen names Leopold Eidlitz, Joseph Paxton and James Bogardus (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say Paxton's design did not suit the site, and they name Hamilton Hoppin and James Bogardus, who proposed a roof hung from a central cast-iron tower (Allwood et al. 2001). The chosen plans were those of Georg J. B. Carstensen and Charles Gildemeister (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001).

The building was an iron-and-glass Greek cross, a plan with four equal arms. It stood 149 feet high, with arms 365 feet long, and a dome of about 100 feet rose at the crossing (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001; Findling & Pelle 1990). The exhibition area covered about four acres. A two-storey annex held a machine arcade and a picture gallery (Findling & Pelle 2008). The glass walls were enamelled to cut heat and glare, unlike the London building, which had to be lined with canvas (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid say its structure was almost identical to London's, and its roof leaked at the start (Allwood et al. 2001). Greenhalgh calls the winning scheme "an unfortunate pastiche of the London prototype" (Greenhalgh 1988). Mandell says the main building looked like a greenhouse (Mandell 1967), and Maloney calls it an overgrown greenhouse like its London namesake (Maloney 2012). Its footprint took virtually all the ground, which left little room for outdoor landscaping (Maloney 2012).

Exhibits and attractions

About four thousand exhibitors took part (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001; Findling & Pelle 1990). Machinery was the largest American category (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). The prize exhibits were McCormick's reaper, Colt's revolvers and Morse's telegraph (Burris 2001). Hinsley says the fair took "Machinery" as its motto and publicly introduced the sewing machine (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Maloney describes the agricultural displays as utilitarian: reapers, corn shellers, churns and threshing machines, with no horticultural or landscape exhibits (Maloney 2012). Greeley found no raw American farm produce, only roots he was told were California potatoes (Maloney 2012).

The building held what was probably the largest sculpture display in the United States until then, together with a picture gallery (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Burris and Steen both record that the fair included the first exhibit of paintings at an international exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its centrepiece was a bronze statue of Washington by Baron Marochetti (Allwood et al. 2001; Ganz 2008).

Elisha Graves Otis gave personal demonstrations of his safety elevator in the building (Allwood et al. 2001). Burris describes enterprising sideshows and shanties around the site, including cockfights, and says that Barnum ran a museum and a play of Uncle Tom's Cabin (Burris 2001). Hinsley describes Shantyville, more than a mile of amusements that included an encampment of three hundred Indians from fifty tribes (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Nations and peoples on show

The fair had four national divisions of twenty-nine courts each, with classification modelled on London (Findling & Pelle 1990). The texts disagree about the share of American exhibitors. Steen says more exhibitors came from the United States than from any other country (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Burris says about one-third were American and two-thirds foreign (Burris 2001). Salt gives a total of about 2,700 foreign and domestic exhibitors (Finnegan & Wright 2015).

Haiti's display is the best documented foreign one. Salt says Haiti, called "Hayti" in Fair documents, was placed in the north-west corner beside Italy, Austria and Holland (Finnegan & Wright 2015). Salt argues that its place among presumably modern, cultured and civilised nations presented Haiti as an equal Atlantic nation-state, not a primitive exhibit (Finnegan & Wright 2015). Salt suggests that Faustin I was personally involved, possibly in response to a circular from the US Secretary of State urging consular help, but that how Haiti used that help is "not fully clear" (Finnegan & Wright 2015). One Fair document lists samples of mahogany among the Haitian objects (Finnegan & Wright 2015).

Some states stayed away. China declined to take part (Hur). The Ottoman Empire stayed away because transport costs were too high (Çelik 1992). Our texts say little about colonies at this fair.

Hinsley argues that displays of this kind presented "primitive" peoples from peripheral territories as though they were raw materials (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Salt lists the nations whose exhibits were in the building as Italy, Great Britain, Germany, France, Austria and Holland (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 193). She says that nearly 2,700 exhibitors provided these foreign contributions (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 193). That figure is for foreign exhibitors only, and it is not the total of about 2,700 foreign and domestic exhibitors given above. Badger says that only a few foreign exhibitors were shown when the fair opened (Badger 1979p. 16).

Salt identifies B. C. Clark as Faustin I's commercial agent to the United States. Clark published two pamphlets in Boston in 1853 (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 195p. 196). Salt says that the question at the centre of the pamphlets was whether Haiti was a real nation (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 195). Clark translated passages from the French of the Haitian historian Thomas Madiou for American readers (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 196).

Salt describes an album of twelve daguerreotypes and lithographs of the imperial court (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 200). One image is attributed to A. Hartmann, a daguerreotypist based in New York (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 204). Salt does not know whether the album was shown at the Fair (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 206). She suggests that an illustrator for the Illustrated London News, who copied two of the album's images for a hostile article in 1856 (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 206), may have seen the album at the Fair (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 206).

Visitors, reception and politics

Steen says nearly 700,000 people were admitted between 15 July and 30 November 1853, paying more than $300,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Attendance was poor until the autumn and fell away in December and January (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Badger gives a total attendance of 1,250,000 (Badger 1979), and Benedict gives 1.3 million (Benedict 1983).

Steen points to the lack of an integrated rail network, the cost of omnibus and street-railway fares of five or six cents, a 50-cent admission and Sunday closing (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle say President Pierce hoped the fair would help reunite a divided nation, and they say it did not (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Allwood, Allan and Reid say Pierce saw the fair as a means of welding the States together (Allwood et al. 2001).

Greeley's opening speech accused Britain of aggrandising its own manufacturing at the expense of the rest of mankind, and claimed that American industry had democratised progress (Ganz 2008). Della Coletta says the fair celebrated Yankee industry against the agrarian South and promoted Northern values as the national image (Della Coletta 2006). Burris argues that the fair exposed the nation's subordination to Britain in cultural and religious matters (Burris 2001).

Aftermath and legacy

The fair closed on 1 November 1854 with a debt (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Burris 2001). The building was used for fairs for two years, then reverted to the City of New York (Allwood et al. 2001). On 5 October 1858, during the American Institute's annual exhibit, a fire destroyed it in less than half an hour (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Burris 2001). Badger notes that this happened although the adjacent reservoir held twenty-one million gallons of water (Badger 1979).

The special police at the fair wore uniforms, and before the end of 1853 the city police were required to dress in the same way (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Steen calls the fair a proving ground for new ideas (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Roche says the Crystal Palace, the Eiffel Tower and the elevator showed American architects in New York and Chicago what monumental public buildings on assembled metal frames could achieve (Roche 2000).

William Blake, speaking to the Centennial Commission in 1872, criticised the New York organisers for profit motives and poor planning, which left the palace unready and displays installed late (Giberti 2002). Giberti says the Centennial organisers learned from this, and so chose to hold their own architectural competition (Giberti 2002). Blake had organised the Mineralogy Department at New York before classifying the Centennial's exhibits (Rydell 1984). Greenhalgh places the real beginning of the American fair tradition at Philadelphia in 1876 (Greenhalgh 1988).

Twenty-three years passed before another world's fair was held in America (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Burris says that the disappointment of New York, with the onset of the Civil War, put grand plans for international exhibitions on hold (Burris 2001p. 92). Allwood, Allan and Reid say that because of the Civil War, Americans were badly represented at London in 1862, and no official American presence was possible (Allwood et al. 2001p. 29).

Opening day and the first months

Burris dates the opening to 12 July, two months behind schedule, when President Franklin Pierce and Secretary of War Jefferson Davis led an opening-day parade that drew some twenty thousand spectators (Burris 2001p. 90). Badger also gives 12 July (Badger 1979p. 16). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 14 July and a delay of three months (Allwood et al. 2001p. 24), and Rydell, Findling and Pelle give the same three-month delay (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The texts do not explain the difference between these dates.

Inside the building, Burris says that no concessions were open for the first two months, that there were no seats, and that the varnished banisters stayed sticky all summer in the heat (Burris 2001p. 90). Burris also records that Horace Greeley, then editor of the Tribune, noticed that none of the architects stood on the platform with the President and officials, and that six British commissioners rode in the parade in a conspicuous place (Burris 2001p. 91). The same account says the proceedings were tightly controlled, because foreign visitors were negligible in number (Burris 2001p. 91).

Inside the building

Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the dome as the set piece of the interior. Monte Lilla designed it, facing it with tempera-painted canvas pierced by thirty-two coloured stained-glass windows showing the arms of the Union and the several States (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23). Henry Greenough's interior decoration used pastel tones, with the structural detail picked out in primary and secondary colours and gold (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23). The roof was clad in white pine boards and tinned (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say that the architects put wood in the roof instead of glass, because glass let in too much light, and that the wooden roof leaked (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Gas lighting served policing and cleaning, and on some evenings the building was opened so that the lit dome could be seen (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23). Carstensen, one of the designers, had laid out the Tivoli Gardens in Copenhagen (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23).

Sideshows and the crowd outside

Burris describes the sideshows that sprang up while the building was going up. They included a French bearded lady and dancing bears (Burris 2001p. 89). A boardinghouse pasted advertisements for cheap rooms over the tombstones of a nearby cemetery (Burris 2001p. 90). Allwood, Allan and Reid describe Latting's Observatory and Ice-cream Parlour, built by a New York businessman opposite the palace, with a viewing platform reached by a steam elevator that the authors describe as unreliable (Allwood et al. 2001p. 23).

The Shantyville described above belongs to Philadelphia in 1876, not to New York. Badger describes more than a mile of saloons, amusement booths and side shows outside the gates of the Centennial, with variety shows and attractions such as "learned pigs" (Badger 1979p. 20). Any account of New York's surroundings should be checked against that fact.

Foreign observers and the press

George Wallis, one of the British commissioners at New York with Joseph Whitworth, summed up the fair in a report presented to the House of Commons (Filipová 2015p. 1). He observed of the furniture section that "elaborate carving is the rule rather than the exception", and he judged that the best pieces were the work of Europeans (Filipová 2015p. 1). The purpose of his report was to map the competition that the United States represented (Filipová 2015p. 1).

The Illustrated Magazine of Art in 1853 called the Fair "the Normal School of Art for the nation", and said that Americans in particular needed its lessons (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 192).

How historians have interpreted it

The texts agree on two points. The fair copied London's Crystal Palace (Greenhalgh 1988; Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001), and it lost money (Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001; Greenhalgh 1988). They disagree about what the failure meant.

Steen argues that the fair's importance was local and practical. It brought business to hotels, shops, construction trades and printers (Findling & Pelle 2008). Steen's entries conclude: "Although a financial failure, the Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations was of considerable significance" (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Allwood, Allan and Reid read the fair as a speculative bubble, and they judge the 1858 fire the end of an apparatus that had already burst (Allwood et al. 2001). Greenhalgh denies that it began an American tradition, placing that beginning at Philadelphia in 1876 (Greenhalgh 1988).

Badger reads the fair as a product of civic pride rather than national strength. The fair reflected, to a degree, the pride of a great city becoming aware of itself as a dynamic metropolis (Badger 1979). Badger argues that once Barnum took over, American fairs became popular "as much show as tell" (Badger 1979).

Burris goes furthest in linking the fair to later exhibitions. Burris argues that the fair revealed a gap between its national and humanistic claims and the free rein given to commerce, and that this gap marked every later American exposition (Burris 2001). Rydell, Findling and Pelle take a different view. They judge the fair a failure of nationalism, overwhelmed by the sectional crisis that led to the Civil War (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Della Coletta reads the fair as an effort to promote Northern values as the national image (Della Coletta 2006).

Hinsley places the fair among the carnivals of the industrial age, in which the display of "primitive" peoples treated them as raw materials (Karp & Lavine 1991). Salt reads Haiti's display as a diplomatic effort to win equal standing among nations (Finnegan & Wright 2015). Ganz reads the Washington statue as a claim that white American men had become self-made activists in a nation of progress (Ganz 2008). Maloney reads the fair as the start of a shift from agrarian to industrial display (Maloney 2012).

The authors thus divide on scale and consequence. Steen sees a financial failure of local significance (Findling & Pelle 2008). Rydell, Findling and Pelle see a national failure (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh sees no lasting tradition (Greenhalgh 1988), while Burris sees a template for later fairs (Burris 2001).

China

The texts say little about any Asian presence at this fair. Hur records that China declined to take part in the Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations in New York in 1853, as it did for the Exposition Universelle in Paris in 1855 (Hurp. 12). The passage gives no reason for the refusal. A few Chinese and foreign merchants from mainland China later submitted silk, vases, ivory carvings, silkworms and raw silk to the London exhibition of 1862 (Hurp. 12).

The passages found for this fair name no Japanese, South Asian or Southeast Asian exhibitor, visitor or observer.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Copying London — the texts treat the fair as an imitation of the 1851 Crystal Palace, with differing views on how far it was a copy (Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Money and management — the fair ran as a speculative enterprise, with conflicting debt and loss figures and a turbulent presidency under Sedgwick and then Barnum (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Burris 2001; Allwood et al. 2001; Badger 1979; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Architecture and site — the Reservoir Square building was a near copy of London's, and the texts see it as derivative and poorly suited to its site (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001; Allwood et al. 2001; Maloney 2012).
  • Machinery and display — machinery, reapers, the Otis elevator and the sculpture gallery were the fair's showpieces (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001; Roche 2000; Maloney 2012; Karp & Lavine 1991).
  • Nations and peoples — Haiti's place among the nations, China's absence and the Shantyville attractions show how the fair framed foreign states and peoples (Finnegan & Wright 2015; Hur; Çelik 1992; Karp & Lavine 1991).
  • Attendance and admission — attendance was hurt by travel costs, the 50-cent admission and Sunday closing, and the texts give conflicting totals (Findling & Pelle 2008; Badger 1979; Benedict 1983; Burris 2001).
  • Afterlife — the 1858 fire, the police uniforms and the Centennial lesson show the fair's legacy in the city and in later American fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Giberti 2002; Roche 2000; Greenhalgh 1988).

Questions for study

  1. Was the New York fair a copy of London, a local speculation, or the start of an American tradition? Greenhalgh and Steen answer this differently, so what evidence would settle the question? (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008)
  2. Burris and Badger both link American fairs to commerce and show. How far does the evidence about this fair support the idea that the fairs that followed were built on the same model? (Burris 2001; Badger 1979)
  3. Salt reads Haiti's display as a claim to equal standing. What would a visitor have seen in the north-west corner, and what do the texts leave unknown? (Finnegan & Wright 2015)
  4. The texts disagree on attendance, exhibitor numbers and losses. Which kinds of sources would a historian need to choose between them? (Benedict 1983; Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001)
  5. Giberti argues that Blake's 1872 criticism shaped the Centennial's planning. What does this suggest about how exhibition organisers learned from one another? (Giberti 2002)
  6. Hinsley treats the fair's sideshows as part of its industrial character. How should students weigh that reading against the emphasis on machinery and sculpture in the same texts? (Karp & Lavine 1991; Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001)
  7. Burris gives about 4,000 exhibitors (Burris 2001p. 90), Salt gives nearly 2,700 foreign exhibitors (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 193), and Badger says only a few foreign exhibitors were shown (Badger 1979p. 16). Which records, such as the official catalogues or the awards lists, would let a historian settle how many foreign exhibitors there were?
  8. Burris gives the opening as 12 July (Burris 2001p. 90), while Allwood, Allan and Reid give 14 July (Allwood et al. 2001p. 24). Which kinds of evidence, such as press reports from the opening week, would a student need to choose between them?
  9. Rydell, Findling and Pelle blame the sectional crisis for the lack of federal sponsorship (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), while Burris describes a fair run for profit by New York businessmen from the start (Burris 2001p. 89). Which reading better explains why Barnum could not revive the fair?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
3The Copyists, the First Paris ‘Exposition Universelle’ and London Againp. 19

The chapter follows the copies of the 1851 exhibition staged in other cities, and asks why most of them failed. Allwood, Allan and Reid cover the Dublin Exhibition of 1853, financed largely by William Dargan, whose southern hall roof was destroyed by a storm and whose five-shilling entry charge kept most people away. New York's Crystal Palace of 1853 ran up debts despite a large site, lost the backing of P T Barnum and burned down a few years later. The Paris Exposition Universelle of 1855 had a government-financed palace with poor ventilation, made a loss of over eight million francs, and included the Exposition Economique, an early thematic display. The final section covers London's 1862 International Exhibition, condemned as an ugly shed and rescued from a loss by Sir John Kelk. The authors conclude that repetition without royal patronage or a central site could not ensure success.

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
3America and the Great World’s Fairsp. 13

Badger follows American participation in world's fairs from London in 1851 to the Philadelphia centennial of 1876. He argues that the United States was hesitant and privately organised, so that its first display in London, rescued by a private banker's money, was mocked by Punch and only later won respect for its machinery and agriculture. Later fairs at Paris and Vienna brought more official backing but also mismanagement, while American design and the fine arts still looked weak to European critics. The New York World's Fair of 1853, a failed Crystal Palace imitation, leads into a discussion of features peculiar to American fairs: little government control, rivalry between cities and a strong appetite for popular entertainment, illustrated by Philadelphia's Shantyville. The centennial, the chapter's main case, looked back to the American past, and the chapter closes with the late 1880s plans for a Columbian fair that would have to outdo Paris.

Burris, John P. Exhibiting Religion: Colonialism and Spectacle at International Expositions, 1851–1893. Charlottesville: University Press of Virginia, 2001.
4Exhibitionism, American Stylep. 86

Burris asks why religion became a theme at the Chicago fair of 1893, and answers by tracing the three nineteenth-century American international expositions that came before it. American fairs differed from European ones because the United States had no colonies to display, so the peoples classed as primitive lived inside the country, and the hierarchies shown at the fairs carried direct political meaning for American social order even though they were staged as carnival entertainment. The chapter works through the New York Crystal Palace of 1853, with its commercial shanties and collapse, the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876, with its racially divided displays and Native American sections, and Chicago's White City, Midway Plaisance and anthropological Department M. Its evidence includes the Kwakiutl performances, the Dahomean village, the exclusion of African Americans, and the protests of Frederick Douglass and Ida B. Wells. The chapter concludes that the midway placed peoples deemed primitive in a carnival setting that implied their religion was not serious, which shaped who could speak at the World's Parliament of Religions.

Finnegan, Diarmid A., and Jonathan Jeffrey Wright, eds. Spaces of Global Knowledge: Exhibition, Encounter and Exchange in an Age of Empire. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
9Brokering Knowledge in an Age of Mis-Recognition and Ignorance; or Displaying Haiti to the MassesKaren N. Saltp. 187

Salt examines how Faustin I, the last emperor of Haiti, used objects and images in the 1850s to counter pejorative views of Haiti in the Atlantic world. She argues that Faustin I deliberately circulated knowledge about Haiti, through Haiti's presence at the 1853 Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations in New York, through pamphlets by his commercial agent B.C. Clark, and through an album of daguerreotypes and lithographs of the imperial court. The chapter works through Clark's two 1853 pamphlets, which pressed for Haiti's recognition, and through an 1856 Illustrated London News article whose text mocked the emperor while its engravings copied the album's portraits of him and Empress Adelina. Salt set these items against the caricatures of Daumier and Cham and against the racialised American debates over recognition. She concludes that the material traces are fragmentary and that the reach and purpose of the album remain open questions, though a positive 1852 Gleason's article suggests that other objects circulated too.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
1Fairs in the Age of Industrialism’s Advance

Rydell, Findling and Pelle trace the world's fair from London's Crystal Palace of 1851, which drew over six million visitors and set the model for later fairs, to the American fairs of the industrial era. Their main cases are the failed New York Crystal Palace of 1853, the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial, where foreign exhibits were arranged by race and Smithsonian displays cast Native Americans as savage counterparts to civilised whites, and a run of southern fairs in Atlanta, New Orleans and Nashville that sought industrial and export markets. Wikipedia

The Exhibition of the Industry of All Nations was a World's Fair held in 1853 in what is now Bryant Park in New York City, in the wake of the highly successful 1851 Great Exhibition in London. It aimed to showcase the new industrial achievements of the world and also to demonstrate the nationalistic pride of a relatively young nation and all that she stood for. Jacob Aaron Westervelt, at that time Mayor of New York, was the President of the exhibition-committee. The general superintendent was Admiral Samuel Francis Du Pont. Opening on July 14, 1853 with newly sworn President Franklin Pierce in attendance, the fair was seen by over 1.1 million visitors before it closed on November 14, 1854. The fair featured its own glass and iron exhibition building – the New York Crystal Palace – directly inspired by London's. The Palace was destroyed by fire on October 5, 1858.
