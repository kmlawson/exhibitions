Paris Universal Exposition of 1855

Exposition universelle des produits de l'agriculture, de l'industrie et des beaux-arts
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle des produits de l'agriculture, de l'industrie et des beaux-arts opened in Paris on 15 May 1855 and closed on 15 November, two weeks late (Findling & Pelle 2008). It was the first French universal exposition and the first European world's fair to combine industrial products with fine art (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Roche 2000). Its organisers set it against London 1851, and it became a model for later fairs: a dominant central building, a large fine-arts section and the display of colonial possessions (Benedict 1983; Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008). Historians use it to study the rivalry between art and industry, the use of fairs by Napoleon III's regime, and the start of the Paris series (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Kaiser; Roche 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair was a French reply to London 1851. The texts agree that France wanted to take back the initiative from Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Ratcliffe lists aims that did not sit easily together: to mark forty years of peace since Waterloo, to advertise the Second Empire of Napoleon III, installed after the coup of December 1851, and to encourage a Franco-British alliance (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The plan changed along the way. It was first scheduled as a national show for 1854. Napoleon III made it international, and the Crimean War, in which Britain and France were fighting Russia, forced a postponement of about a year (Mandell 1967; Kaiser). Kaiser argues that the fair symbolised the Franco-British alliance during the war, and that Queen Victoria's visit helped the new emperor gain respectability (Kaiser). Della Coletta notes that the exposition called itself "a temple of peace, bringing all nations together in concord" while fighting went on in the Crimea (Della Coletta 2006). Badger reads the fair as an attempt to silence London's claim, made in 1851, to be the capital of Europe (Badger 1979).

Organisation and finance

A private company, approved by the government on 27 March 1852, built and operated the main hall. The state gave the land and guaranteed the company a minimum annual return of 4 per cent. The exposition itself lost money, but the company earned a profit (Findling & Pelle 1990). A commission of thirty-seven, including Ingres and Delacroix, organised the event (Findling & Pelle 1990). Prince Napoleon, the emperor's cousin, was its chief (Allwood et al. 2001). Frédéric Le Play organised the classification of exhibits, and saw the fair as a display of progress made possible by cooperation between classes (Mandell 1967; Roche 2000).

The sources differ on the loss. Ratcliffe and Allwood, Allan and Reid give 8.3 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001). Mandell gives 8 million francs in the text and 9 million in a note (Mandell 1967). Badger's appendix gives a cost of £1,000,000 and receipts of £128,099, and says the French government covered the deficit (Badger 1979). Admission was kept low. Mandell says it was the first French exposition to charge admission, at one franc on most days and five francs on Sundays, later reduced to two (Mandell 1967). Badger reports that Napoleon III ordered the price kept to an absolute minimum, on some days only four cents (Badger 1979). Ratcliffe gives a Sunday charge of 20 centimes (Findling & Pelle 2008). These two accounts of the Sunday price cannot both be right.

Prince Napoleon, as chief administrator, wrote the official report. It warned that universal expositions were too complex to run well, and suggested limiting them to machinery or art for specialists (Mandell 1967). Giberti says the report urged future fairs to be serious educational institutions, partial rather than universal, and proposed a dual system of classification, with objects arranged by nation across the hall and by type along it. The report also recommended abolishing juries and awards, a proposal that was ignored (Giberti 2002).

Site and architecture

The centre of the fair was the Palais de l'Industrie, an iron frame 250 metres long, 108 metres wide and 35 metres high, with a glass roof, designed by Barrault and Viel (Findling & Pelle 2008). Benedict argues that such a building encompassed the whole fair and symbolised it, as London's Crystal Palace did (Benedict 1983). Greenhalgh says the difficult relationship between the architect Viel and the engineers compromised the building (Greenhalgh 1988).

The hall was too small and poorly ventilated. Two temporary structures were added: a gallery on the quai de Billy and a French Renaissance building by Hector Lefuel for the fine arts (Findling & Pelle 1990). Mandell says the show spilled into other buildings (Mandell 1967). Giberti says the exhibits were spread across several buildings, which split the national displays, and gives six buildings in one place and seven in another (Giberti 2002). The site is given as 117,000 square metres, with a cost of 11 million francs excluding the main building (Findling & Pelle 1990). Ratcliffe gives 29 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008), while Benedict and Allwood, Allan and Reid give 34 acres (Benedict 1983; Allwood et al. 2001).

The Palais des Beaux-Arts was temporary and was to be pulled down, while the Palais de l'Industrie was meant to be a lasting monument. Hornstein argues that this reversed the architectural fortunes of art and industry (Hollengreen et al. 2014). The Palais de l'Industrie stayed on its site until 1897, when it was demolished for later exhibition buildings (Allwood et al. 2001; Geppert 2010). Mandell gives 1898 (Mandell 1967).

Exhibits and attractions

The fair was an attempt to outshine London, which had admitted only sculpture connected with mechanical processes (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Napoleon III said the arts were as important as industry, and the Palais des Beaux-Arts became in many ways the most important section (Allwood et al. 2001). The fine-arts show was large. Findling and Pelle's dictionary gives 5,000 works by 2,054 artists from 29 states, 3,634 of them French (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh gives 1,041 French artists showing 2,726 works, and 71 French women showing 162 works (Greenhalgh 1988). Greenhalgh argues that the real contest was not romanticism against classicism: Courbet showed eleven controversial canvases that announced a realist idea of art's role (Greenhalgh 1988). Hornstein gives the most detailed account of the rivalry between art and industry. Industrial exhibitors could put prices on their goods for the first time at a world's fair, and about 1,500 sale booths in the Comptoirs de Vente let visitors buy them. Artists could not show prices, and a petition by foreign artists to do so was refused (Hollengreen et al. 2014). The Palais de l'Industrie drew about 3.6 million paying visitors, against about 900,000 at the Palais des Beaux-Arts, and L'Illustration complained that visitors paid twice to see the two halls (Hollengreen et al. 2014). A cheaper scheme, the Exposition Economique of low-priced domestic goods for working-class homes, opened almost two months after the rest of the fair and proved popular (Allwood et al. 2001).

Nations and colonies on show

Thirty-four countries took part, against 28 in London (Findling & Pelle 2008). Imperial Russia had no official representation because of the war, though individual Russian businessmen were invited (Findling & Pelle 1990). The German states sent no joint display; the individual states collected central funds and contributed separately (Lawn 2009). Hungarian goods appeared among the Austrian exhibits in a minor and fragmented way (Filipová 2015). The Ottoman Empire took part (Çelik 1992). Chinese authorities passed over the Paris show, after declining the New York exhibition of 1853 (Hur).

France proudly showed its colonial empire, but Ratcliffe argues that, like the British, it exoticised the colonial peoples and invented Orientalism (Findling & Pelle 2008). Coté says colonial exhibits were included in the 1851 and 1855 fairs but became prominent only from Paris 1878 (Coté). Hale agrees that the colonies took part in a limited way, and that their sections grew in stature only under the Third Republic (Hale 2008). Hoffenberg shows the Australian colonies, slow to exhibit in London in 1851, built full courts for Paris, and British Guiana sent woods, oils and fibrous substances (Hoffenberg 2001). A Canadian commissioner described the Indian court as an Indian village or bazaar (Hoffenberg 2001). Kaiser reports that two American commissioners and jury members called the fair "a great school where all industrialists could learn lessons and find models" (Kaiser).

Visitors and reception

The sources give different attendance figures. Ratcliffe gives 5,162,230 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008). Findling and Pelle's dictionary gives 5,162,330 (Findling & Pelle 1990), as does Badger's appendix (Badger 1979). Hale gives 5,163,000 paying visitors, citing Ory (Hale 2008). Benedict gives 5.2 million (Benedict 1983), Della Coletta gives more than five million (Della Coletta 2006), and Greenhalgh gives about four million (Greenhalgh 1988). Hale says the fair drew about as many paying visitors as London 1851 (Hale 2008), while Mandell says attendance was slightly lower than London's (Mandell 1967).

Queen Victoria made a state visit in August 1855, the first by a reigning English monarch since Henry V in 1422 (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the visit improved relations between the two countries (Allwood et al. 2001). The Pre-Raphaelite painters had their first large public display in Paris (Mandell 1967). Some contemporaries were less impressed. One English critic concluded that the exposition had been reduced to a vast bazaar, because exhibitors could state their prices (Giberti 2002). Mandell reports that many Frenchmen remembered the fair as a political and intellectual victory (Mandell 1967).

Aftermath and legacy

After 1855, all the Salon exhibitions were held in the Palais de l'Industrie until 1897. A critic wrote in 1857 that art had entered the Palais de l'Industrie and would not leave it, and Hornstein reads this as a sign that industry had overshadowed art (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Greenhalgh argues that a heavy fine-arts section became standard at later fairs (Greenhalgh 1988).

The 1855 fair set the pattern for the Paris series of five fairs between 1855 and 1900, which Roche argues gave the genre its new form, content and cultural character (Roche 2000). Le Play designed the 1867 building to suit the classification he had organised in 1855 (Mandell 1967), and Prince Napoleon's dual system inspired the arrangement of the 1867 exhibits (Giberti 2002). In 1865 Proudhon proposed turning the Palais de l'Industrie into a permanent exhibition centre (Geppert 2010; Della Coletta 2006). Geppert describes the building as a national landmark, the Parisian counterpart to the Crystal Palace, and reads its demolition as a sign that the nineteenth-century exhibition tradition had ended (Geppert 2010). ## How historians have interpreted it Several texts treat the fair as a French rival to London, and as the first event of a series that later fairs followed (Badger 1979; Kaiser; Roche 2000). Ratcliffe reads the fair as a theme park, a celebration of Western progress, consumption and high culture, and judges it a success. Ratcliffe also quotes Walter Benjamin's view of expositions as "places of pilgrimage to the fetish commodity" (Findling & Pelle 2008). Mandell calls the fair a political and intellectual victory, and sees Napoleon III's aims as a mix of idealism, prestige and business (Mandell 1967). Della Coletta sees the fair as a benchmark for Turin and as part of an ideology that presented nations as harmonious and progressive, even when they were at war (Della Coletta 2006). Badger reads the fair as a stage in the Paris-London rivalry and a means of binding the capitalist class to the regime (Badger 1979).

The main disagreement is over how far art and industry were joined. Allwood, Allan and Reid argue that the French did not draw the line between fine art and industry as sharply as the British did (Allwood et al. 2001). Hornstein argues that the pairing revealed a growing divide, in which industry prevailed over art, and that the fair may have deepened the schism it was meant to close (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Giberti argues that the 1855 fair added the fine arts while keeping them separate from industry, in a building of their own with its own rules (Giberti 2002). Burris argues that the fair challenged the Crystal Palace model by stressing art and design, and was too politically unsettled to host the intellectual congresses that Paris first held in 1867 (Burris 2001).

The readings also differ on colonial display. Ratcliffe notes that France proudly showed its colonial empire (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hale and Coté regard the colonial sections as limited until later (Hale 2008; Coté), and Hoffenberg treats the colonies as a means of measuring progress and building commercial links (Hoffenberg 2001). Hanson, in Raizman and Robey's volume, argues that later fairs kept many of the precedents of progress, peace and prosperity set at London and at Paris 1855 (Raizman & Robey 2018). The texts agree that the fair was a turning point, but not on whether it succeeded. Mandell and Ratcliffe call it a success (Mandell 1967; Findling & Pelle 2008), while Hornstein and Badger stress the public's preference for industry and the financial loss (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Badger 1979).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Colonial displays

Asian possessions appear in the British display. Greenhalgh says the British took an enormous imperial display to Paris that included exhibits from the East India Company and Ceylon (Greenhalgh 1988p. 56). Greenhalgh also says the Dutch exhibits were generally noted to be as impressive, but the texts do not say whether these came from the East Indies (Greenhalgh 1988p. 56). The French built an Imperial Pavilion, a government-built structure that housed much imperial produce (Greenhalgh 1988p. 56). Greenhalgh calls Algeria the centrepiece of the French effort, effectively the equivalent of India for the English (Greenhalgh 1988p. 64). Ratcliffe says the organisers proudly displayed the colonial empire, but, as the British did with their possessions, only by exoticising and inventing Orientalism and the colonial other (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 22). Hoffenberg adds that the exhibitions of this period let commissioners measure and represent progress (Hoffenberg 2001p. 7).

India

Royle was executive for the Indian department at the fair (Hoffenberg 2001p. 284). Indian producers were encouraged to use overseas shows, including the Paris exposition, and the Board of Trade asked Royle to report on the Indian and colonial products shown there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104). The Canadian commissioner was struck by the Indian court, which he described as an Indian village or bazaar with women, men, horses, cattle, elephants and monkeys (Hoffenberg 2001p. 150). Hoffenberg says the court invited visitors to imagine a timeless India of fantasy and tradition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 150). The same commissioner praised the originality of design of the Indian art (Hoffenberg 2001p. 161). A Bombay newspaper editor wrote that the 1855 exposition would have the advantage of experience over 1851 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 7). Hoffenberg also says Bombay museum officers built a machinery collection from technology prepared for the Paris exposition of 1855 and then returned from it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 194).

China

Hur says Chinese authorities declined the New York exhibition of 1853 and the Paris exhibition of 1855, and that a few Chinese and foreign merchants later sent products to London in 1862 (Hurp. 12). Kaiser says in general terms that China was not officially represented at world exhibitions until 1904, and that its unofficial displays seemed weak by comparison with Japan (Kaiserp. 48). Kaiser does not single out 1855 in this passage.

Japan

The texts do not mention a Japanese display at Paris in 1855.

Interpretation

Greenhalgh reads the Imperial Pavilion as a French gain in the rivalry over imperial display (Greenhalgh 1988p. 64). Ratcliffe reads the colonial displays as exoticising, and Hoffenberg reads the Indian courts as presenting a timeless India (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 22; Hoffenberg 2001p. 150). The texts give no account of Asian visitors or of how Asian displays were received at this fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Art and industry — The texts treat the fine arts as the centre of the fair, and some see the pairing with industry as a rivalry that industry won (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Greenhalgh 1988; Allwood et al. 2001; Benedict 1983; Giberti 2002).
  • Rivalry with London 1851 — Most texts treat Paris as a reply to the Crystal Palace and to British claims of leadership (Badger 1979; Kaiser; Della Coletta 2006; Roche 2000; Mandell 1967; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Peace, progress and ideology — The fair claimed to be a temple of peace during the Crimean War and used industry to show a progressive nation (Della Coletta 2006; Findling & Pelle 2008; Badger 1979).
  • Colonial and imperial display — The texts agree that France showed its empire, but differ on how central this was in 1855 compared with later fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008; Hoffenberg 2001; Coté; Hale 2008; Hur; Çelik 1992).
  • Admission and the public — Low prices, the sale booths and the crowds are treated as signs of a new relation between fair and visitors (Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967; Hollengreen et al. 2014; Giberti 2002).
  • Classification and the Palais de l'Industrie — The arrangement of exhibits and the iron hall are treated as models for later fairs (Benedict 1983; Giberti 2002; Mandell 1967; Geppert 2010; Roche 2000).

Questions for study

  1. Why did contemporaries count the 1855 fair a success when it lost money? Compare the accounts in (Mandell 1967), (Findling & Pelle 2008) and (Badger 1979).
  2. How far did the fair separate art from industry? Does Hornstein's account of a deepening divide (Hollengreen et al. 2014) fit better than the account of a blurred line in (Allwood et al. 2001)?
  3. Did low admission prices make the fair more democratic, or was the Exposition Economique a way of managing the working-class public (Allwood et al. 2001; Findling & Pelle 2008; Badger 1979)?
  4. Colonial display is central in some accounts and minor in others. Which texts support each view, and does the difference depend on the period they study (Findling & Pelle 2008; Coté; Hale 2008; Hoffenberg 2001)?
  5. How should historians treat sources that give different attendance and exhibitor figures, such as (Findling & Pelle 2008), (Findling & Pelle 1990), (Greenhalgh 1988) and (Lawn 2009)?
  6. Should the fine-arts section be judged as a showcase for women artists and realist painters, as Greenhalgh argues (Greenhalgh 1988), or as a national display of the French school?

Katie Hornstein's chapter is devoted to the 1855 Exposition Universelle. She argues that the fair's pairing of fine art and industrial products, which the French government presented as mutually dependent, revealed instead a growing divide: industrial goods could be priced and sold in the Comptoirs de Vente while art could not, and the permanent Palais de l'Industrie outshone the temporary Palais des Beaux-Arts. Using the press, including L'Illustration, and the attendance figures, she shows that contemporaries read the fair as a rivalry in which industry prevailed over art. She concludes that the fair may have deepened the perceived schism between art and industry, despite its aim of unifying them.

Wikipedia

The Exposition Universelle of 1855 (French pronunciation: [ɛkspozisjɔ̃ ynivɛʁsɛl]), better known in English as the 1855 Paris Exposition, was a world's fair held on the Champs-Élysées in Paris, France, from 15 May to 15 November 1855. Its full official title was the Exposition Universelle des produits de l'Agriculture, de l'Industrie et des Beaux-Arts de Paris 1855. It was the first of ten major expositions held in the city between 1855 and 1937. Nowadays, the exposition's sole physical remnant is the Théâtre du Rond-Point des Champs-Élysées, designed by architect Gabriel Davioud, which originally housed the Panorama National.
