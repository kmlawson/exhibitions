Semarang International Colonial Exhibition

Koloniale Tentoonstelling Semarang
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition (Koloniale Tentoonstelling Semarang) ran in Semarang, one of the colonial cities on Java, from 20 August to 22 November 1914 (Cotép. 30p. 23). It was a private initiative of Semarang's citizens, chiefly the chamber of commerce, not of the colonial government (Cotép. 4). It was first set for 1913 and opened in 1914 (Cotép. 4). Coté calls it the first large exhibition in the Netherlands Indies, and argues that its colonial meanings were ambiguous and that 1914 marked the beginning of the end of the imperial future it celebrated (Cotép. 3p. 34p. 9).

Background and aims

The exhibition was first planned in 1912 and intended to open in 1913, to mark the centenary of Dutch independence from France (Cotép. 4). A committee of city notables postponed the opening to 1914 (Cotép. 4). The new date was explained as the anniversary of the return of the archipelago to the Netherlands by Great Britain after the Napoleonic period. Coté adds that it also marked the completion of the conquest and incorporation of the archipelago, a process begun in 1892 (Cotép. 4).

Coté places the exhibition within the colonial exhibitions of his period. These aimed to celebrate white settler achievement, describe the civilising role of the colonial mission and demonstrate a future for the colonised native (Cotép. 2). The Semarang exhibition was conceived in European terms but was more directly modelled on colonial exhibitions in British India and Australia (Cotép. 3). Its organisers meant it to affirm settler society and to show urban Javanese a harmonious future. For some planners it was a blueprint for a possible future Indies society (Cotép. 6p. 11).

Organisers and finance

The exhibition was a private undertaking of the city's business and community leaders, not of the colonial government (Cotép. 4p. 26). Coté calls it, literally, a colonial exhibition: it represented the interests of a small group of private individuals and a local settler identity rather than a Dutch national one (Cotép. 5). Neither the colonial government nor the Dutch ministry of colonies supported it, and officials on the committee attended in a private capacity (Cotép. 27). The organisers resented the government in Batavia for its niggardly support (Cotép. 31). The Semarang council did not take part officially. According to a note, its refusal had more to do with money than with government direction, and council members sat on the planning committee as private citizens [17, note 19, l. 1366-1371].

Oei Tiong Ham, described as Semarang's most famous son and as a member of one of the most prominent and wealthy Chinese communities in colonial Asia, provided the land and was the major financial guarantor (Cotép. 25). According to Maclaine Pont, Oei received in return a new road to his land holdings, which raised their value [17, note 31, l. 1431-1433]. Financial exigencies had already reduced the organisers' plans to the bare bones of their instrumental aims (Cotép. 5). The exhibition lost money. Coté links the loss to low native attendance and to the absence of foreign tourists during the war (Cotép. 30).

Site and buildings

The grounds covered 26 hectares, with 600 metres of roadway, 1,067 metres of railway and 105 specially built buildings of 39,260 square metres (Cotép. 3). Most of the architecture was by Henry Maclaine Pont, assisted by Thomas Karsten. The temporary buildings used asbestos sheets, galvanised iron roofing and steel frames (Cotép. 3). Electric and gas lighting gave artificial daylight until midnight on each opening day, and tram and automobile services moved visitors around the grounds (Cotép. 3). The site lay in the foothills outside the city (Cotép. 25). The spectacular entrance arch, the public buildings and the reconstructed regional houses set European modernity against the traditional (Cotép. 11).

Maclaine Pont's own plan differed. He wanted the European and Indies sections kept sharply distinct but organically connected, so that neither dominated the other (Cotép. 14). Western buildings were to stand in plain view. Eastern forms were to lie along a winding path, with a temple above the grounds giving a view over the whole site (Cotép. 15). Coté calls this a mystical vision, with orientalist nostalgia in it (Cotép. 15p. 17). The plan was not carried out, largely for financial reasons, and the businessmen on the committee did not share its utopian view. The native buildings became exotic colour (Cotép. 17). Coté argues that the buildings, set in a tropical landscape, could not convey the experience Maclaine Pont described (Cotép. 17).

Exhibits and attractions

The grounds were divided into seven thematic departments, and nine purpose-built pavilions housed government and European industrial and agricultural displays (Cotép. 13p. 19p. 38). Government and industrial displays and European agriculture were the most significant (Cotép. 19). The sugar display, with a model of an old factory from the forties and fifties and a modern factory with a capacity of 4,000 picols of cane at a time, was the most impressive (Cotép. 19). A transport hall showed the move from native prau and grobak to the European automobile, which the journalist JFHA Later praised as a crass display of evolutionary progress (Cotép. 20p. 39).

The native section included traditional architecture, regional crafts, working craftsmen, dance and music, demonstration fields and indigenous agriculture (Cotép. 13). The native agriculture display catalogued traditional species and recommended improved varieties (Cotép. 22). Scale models of ideal native housing showed cheap domestic designs, with specimens of house pests beside them (Cotép. 23). A kampung tukang, or village of tradesmen, gave live craft demonstrations (Cotép. 24). Luna Park, native entertainments and warung were meant to draw the Javanese crowd (Cotép. 3p. 30).

Who took part

Some prominent Javanese residents sat on committees, and wealthy Chinese businessmen were key sponsors (Cotép. 13). Javanese involvement in the design was limited. All native exhibits, except those of the Surakarta and Yogyakarta royal courts, were organised by local colonial bureaucrats (Cotép. 14). Classical temple carvings from Yogyakarta, first planned for the native display, were replaced by a photographic exhibition (Cotép. 14).

The women's pavilion was the one place where colonial and indigenous interests seemed to meet (Cotép. 28). Its European section reused exhibits from De Vrouw 1813-1913, the Amsterdam exhibition of the previous year (Cotép. 29). The display included a holy corner for Kartini, a display on the teacher Dewi Sartika (spelled Raden Dewi Sartica in note 32) and the Chinese Lie Tak Tong girls' school (Cotép. 29p. 40). The souvenir commentary by Maria Kooy-van Zeggelen praised joint work across races but treated indigenous women as needing awakening by Western ideas (Cotép. 29).

Visitors and reception

Between 20 August and 22 November, 306,795 native visitors and 370,472 Europeans attended (Cotép. 30). The Luna Park and native attractions had been meant to bring in the Javanese, and the exhibition was a financial failure (Cotép. 30). Coté sets out four explanations for low native attendance (Cotép. 30).

The first held that the exhibition was too European for ordinary Javanese, who felt uncomfortable in unfamiliar surroundings (Cotép. 30). The second blamed rumours of danger, which spread before the opening. Organisers held a selamatan in the exhibition mosque the day before the public opening, apparently without effect (Cotép. 30). The third held that officials had warned native communities to be thrifty before war-related price rises, and that the warning had worked too well (Cotép. 31). The fourth, which commentators did not state and which Coté thinks most likely, was growing opposition to the colonial regime. Sarekat Islam had been founded in 1912, and the 1913 mandatory celebration of Dutch independence added to resentment (Cotép. 31). The Indisch Partij issued a nationalist manifesto demanding independence (Cotép. 32). A later explanation, from one of the original designers, was that there was nothing of interest for natives to see (Cotép. 32).

The visitors were mainly better-off and educated Javanese, the group the colonial regime had already educated and employed (Cotép. 32). Semarang had about 150,000 inhabitants, most of them Inlander (Cotép. 33). Coté infers that much of the over 300,000 native attendance came from other north coast cities, such as Surabaya, Yogyakarta and Surakarta (Cotép. 33).

The colonial setting

Coté defines colonial modernity in two strands: a reformist faith in progress, expressed in technology and education, and a tighter surveillance of racial boundaries (Cotép. 6). In 1914 the Dutch had only just ended a forty-year war against Aceh, had only begun to build an administrative bureaucracy, and had set up an education system for the indigenous population only by 1910 (Cotép. 8). A contemporary observer, Gonggrijp, judged that more had changed in the Netherlands Indies between 1905 and 1914 than in the previous three centuries (Cotép. 9).

Politics and controversy

The Eurasian settler community, largely of low economic status, had pressed for political representation for decades. The Indische Bond was formed in 1898 to demand better conditions for the settlers (Cotép. 27). Coté dates the legislation that created municipal government to 1903 in one place (Cotép. 27) and to 1906 in another (Cotép. 13). Coté argues that the exhibition could stand for settler national pride as readily as for colonial success (Cotép. 28). The European audience was divided. For the Eurasian working class the exhibition offered technical education and a reminder of the value of being European. Coté concludes that it played on ethnic rivalry (Cotép. 28).

Aftermath and legacy

Coté reads 1914 as the beginning of the end of imperial dreams rather than a glorious beginning. Retrospectively, the year came to be linked with the Indië Weerbaar (defence of the Indies) campaign and the first signs of Indonesian nationalist politics (Cotép. 4). Maclaine Pont regretted that the indigenous architectural exhibition was not kept on as a permanent display after the closure [17, note 15, l. 1347-1349]. Semarang today has a permanent exhibition of central Javanese architecture, an imitation of the far grander Taman Mini Indah established in Jakarta in 1975 [17, note 15, l. 1340-1343]. Coté doubts that Semarang provided the specific model for Taman Mini Indah, though the two are decidedly similar in their purpose [17, note 40, l. 1477-1480].

Its place in the series

Coté sets the exhibition against earlier Dutch displays of colonial life. He names Dutch participation in world exhibitions from London in 1851 to Paris in 1931 (Cotép. 2). Native arts and crafts exhibitions had been held in Batavia, Surabaya and Semarang, and Semarang had held its first arts and crafts exhibition in 1908 (Cotép. 3p. 36). Before Semarang, nothing on the scale of the Calcutta exhibition of 1883 had been attempted in the Dutch colony (Cotép. 3). The note gives the Allahabad exhibition as 1911, where the text gives 1910 (Cotép. 3p. 36). Coté places the native village of the Paris exhibition of 1889 in the same European tradition (Cotép. 12).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Japan and China

Japan and China were the main Asian participants. The Japanese exhibition buildings were impressive and reflected the politics of Asia during the European war. Japan, a British ally, was believed to have serious political and economic interests in the Netherlands Indies (Cotép. 5). The Chinese display expressed the new Nationalist image of China and its concern not to be outdone by Japan (Cotép. 5). The Chinese consul to Batavia also made a formal visit to the exhibition [17, note 10, l. 1319-1321]. Coté argues that the displays of the independent Asian governments of China and Japan offered provocative models for an autonomous Asian future (Cotép. 34).

The Semarang Chinese community

The Chinese delegation had been invited by wealthy Semarang Chinese businessmen who supported Chinese national identity in the colony (Cotép. 5). Oei Tiong Ham provided the land and was the main financial guarantor. The Chinese pavilion was designed by a Chinese architect and built by tradesmen brought from China (Cotép. 25). Coté reads it as an opportunity to display independent economic power, claim political autonomy and protest against colonial discrimination (Cotép. 25). Chinese demands for legal equality were the background to this claim [17, note 30, l. 1423-1425].

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial modernity — Coté reads the exhibition as a show of reformist progress tied to tighter control of racial boundaries, with urban reform giving officials a neutral scientific language (Cotép. 6p. 8).
  • Pedagogy of display — the official account never mentioned pedagogy, yet the layout, organised by theme and labelled throughout, worked like a school textbook (Cotép. 17p. 18).
  • Native audience and attendance — natives were both object and participant, and attendance was low for four reasons Coté weighs against one another (Cotép. 34p. 30).
  • Settler and Eurasian politics — the exhibition could stand for settler national pride, and it played on ethnic rivalry among Europeans (Cotép. 26p. 28).
  • Nationalism and tradition — the exhibition gave the native a view of the extent of the colonised population and defined native tradition, both of which could feed an anti-colonial movement (Cotép. 34).

Questions for study

  1. If the exhibition was meant to win over urban Javanese, why were its native displays arranged by colonial bureaucrats and not by the people shown (Cotép. 14)?
  2. How far can the four explanations for low native attendance be trusted, when the article says the financial loss, not the failed pedagogy, prompted the post-exhibition analysis (Cotép. 30)?
  3. Did an exhibition run by Semarang's private leaders serve colonial order, settler nationalism, or both (Cotép. 26p. 28)?
  4. The article gives 1903 and 1906 as the dates of municipal government. What difference would it make to Coté's argument if one date were wrong (Cotép. 13p. 27)?
  5. Coté says the educated Javanese visitor could take in the knowledge without accepting the colonial vision. How would a study of the exhibition's visitors test that claim (Cotép. 36)?

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

Untitled opening section (no heading in text)p. 1Asia

Coté opens by placing the Semarang International Colonial Exhibition of 1914 within the history of world's fairs, which he presents as stages for a discourse of progress and a pedagogy of mass education. Colonial exhibitions, he argues, carried the same didactic aims as European ones but faced a particular problem: could native audiences read them correctly? Drawing on Prakash's account of British India and on Dutch participation in world exhibitions from London in 1851 to Paris in 1931, he shows how the Netherlands used such displays to present itself abroad. The Semarang exhibition, planned in 1912, postponed from 1913 and staged by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, is described through its scale, its architecture, its electric lighting and the foreign pavilions of Japan, China and two Australian states. Coté concludes that the exhibition, opening as war began in Europe, was a settler-led showcase whose meanings could not be fixed, and that it marked the beginning of the end of the imperial future it celebrated.

In the contents of this work

The colonial exhibition of Semarang: visible and invisible meaningsp. 5Asia

Coté argues that the Semarang exhibition, though framed as a display of colonial achievement, was also meant to model a harmonious future society that would win over both the settler community and the Javanese urban population. He frames this through the idea of colonial modernity, which combined a reformist faith in progress with tighter surveillance of racial boundaries. Drawing on Stoler and Mrazek, he shows how the discourse of urban reform gave colonial officials a neutral-seeming, scientific language for regulating native life in towns. He then sets the exhibition within the politics of 1914, when the Aceh war had only just ended, the colonial bureaucracy was still being built and nationalist demands were beginning to appear. Coté concludes that the organisers saw the exhibition as a new colonial beginning and a blueprint for a future Indies society, but that it was also, unknown to them, the start of the end of the future it celebrated, and that gaps between plan, layout and visitors already revealed disagreement.

Defining a colonial utopiap. 11

Coté turns to the architecture and layout of the Semarang exhibition, using the 1920 account of its designer, Henry Maclaine Pont, as his main evidence. The entrance arch, the electric lighting and the tram and road links announced a colonial modernity, while reconstructed regional houses set beside them gave European-ness its contrast. Coté places these native displays within a long European habit of exhibiting villages and live craftsmen, but argues that in a colony the native was also meant to be a member of the audience. Maclaine Pont's plan, with Western buildings in plain view, eastern forms hidden along a winding path and a temple overlooking all, was a utopian vision of separate but connected communities. The plan was never fully carried out, for financial reasons, which left the native section to read as orientalist nostalgia. He concludes that a utopia laid out in a tropical landscape could not be felt through its buildings alone, however carefully reconstructed.

In the contents of this work

Establishing proper orderp. 17Asia

Coté examines how the Semarang exhibition was ordered to educate visitors, though official accounts never named pedagogy. Organised by theme and labelled throughout, its layout worked like a school textbook. The strongest displays were government departments and European agriculture, including a pair of sugar factory models, one from the 1840s and one modern. In the native section, a journalist's guide read regional buildings and crafts as an evolutionary sequence, while native agriculture and model housing drew on the health reformer Tillema's warnings about disease. The live kampung tukang is read as an economic display, and the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control. Coté concludes that the exhibition's order expressed a settler vision of progress in which each community had a designated place.

The spectatorp. 26

Coté asks whether the native spectator would have recognised the invitation to take part in colonial modernity, and argues that the exhibition's evolutionary message was hard to read against. He shows that the exhibition was a private Semarang initiative without government backing, and that its European audience, especially the Eurasian working class, was divided by economic and political grievances. The chapter concludes that settler progressives on municipal councils could make the exhibition stand for settler nationalism as readily as for colonial success.

Women and the Semarang International Colonial Exhibitionp. 28Asia

Coté treats the women's pavilion as the one place where colonial and indigenous interests seemed to meet. The European section reused exhibits from the Amsterdam exhibition De Vrouw 1813-1913, and the pavilion also honoured Kartini and the teacher Dewi Sartika and included a Chinese girls' school. Coté shows, however, that the souvenir commentary by Maria Kooy-van Zeggelen treated indigenous women as needing awakening by Western ideas, so that progressivism shaped even this display.

In the contents of this work

Outcomesp. 30Asia

Coté weighs why the Semarang exhibition failed: 306,795 Javanese visitors against 370,472 Europeans, and a financial loss. He sets out four explanations: that it was too modern, that rumours of danger scared people away, that the native was too thrifty after official warnings, and, most likely, that growing anti-colonial opposition kept the masses out. He concludes that visitors were mainly the better off and educated, the group the object lesson was meant for.

In the contents of this work

Conclusionp. 34Asia

Coté concludes that the colonial exhibition, meant to reinforce colonial order, introduced ambiguities unlike those of European exhibitions, because the native was a participant as well as an object. He identifies two ways it could feed nationalism: by showing the extent and diversity of the colonised population, and by defining native tradition in ways that anti-colonial movements could use. He argues that the pedagogic project depended on the viewer's involvement, so the educated Javanese visitor could consume the knowledge without accepting the colonial vision.

Wikipedia

The Colonial Exhibition, Dutch: Koloniale Tentoonstelling, took place in Semarang, Dutch East Indies (now Indonesia) on 20 August through 22 November 1914. Colonial exhibitions were trade expositions. It was designed to "give a comprehensive picture of the Dutch Indies in their present prosperous condition." It was the first large scale exposition in the Dutch East Indies, and financed by the participating corporations with a subsidy from the Dutch East Indies government. The architect was Maclaine Pont.
