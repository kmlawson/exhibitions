Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion was planned for Keijo- (Seoul) during the Japanese protectorate (Henry 2014p. 95) (Henry 2014p. 96). In summer 1908, officials and local leaders planned it with a budget of 500,000 yen, nearly ten times that of the 1907 Keijo- Exhibition (Henry 2014p. 96) (Henry 2014p. 96). The Korean government could not raise the money, and the organisers postponed the event indefinitely (Henry 2014p. 97) (Henry 2014p. 97).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Henry places the plan before the annexation of 1910 (Henry 2014p. 96). He describes the 1907 Keijo- Exhibition as a small-scale precursor to the expositions of 1915 and 1929 (Henry 2014p. 93). Protectorate and business officials wanted to build on that event, using the palace grounds to show Japan's material prowess and to promote the commercial interests of powerful settlers (Henry 2014p. 96).

Organisers and finance

The director of the 1907 exhibition met the Korean Minister of Agriculture, the mayor, the Japanese settlement association and local business leaders to plan a large-scale event (Henry 2014p. 96). They called it the Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion (Henry 2014p. 96). Officials allocated a budget of 500,000 yen (Henry 2014p. 96).

Site

The organisers wanted a more symbolically potent location than the Meiji-machi site used in 1907 (Henry 2014p. 96). They first discussed Kyŏngbok Palace and later decided to survey Kyŏnghŭi Palace, which Henry calls the focus of modernising reforms during the Great Han Empire (Henry 2014p. 96) (Henry 2014p. 97). Holding the display there would also have highlighted the alleged ineptitude of Kojong, whom the protectorate had forced to abdicate (Henry 2014p. 97) (Henry 2014p. 97). Henry's note for the Kyŏnghŭi sentence cites the Cho-sen shimpo- of July 1908 (Henry 2014p. 242). He notes that Kyŏngbok Palace opened to public viewing in 1908 but does not link that opening to the plan (Henry 2014p. 29).

Why it was not held

The Korean government was already compromised by the aggressive tactics of the Residency-General (Henry 2014p. 97). It "failed to muster the necessary funds" (Henry 2014p. 97), and the Japanese organisers postponed the event indefinitely (Henry 2014p. 97). Henry's note for this sentence cites the Cho-sen shimbun of 1 May 1910 (Henry 2014p. 242). The text says postponed, not cancelled, and gives no later date for the event.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Protectorate exhibitions — the plan followed the 1907 event and was meant to extend it, before annexation (Henry 2014p. 93) (Henry 2014p. 96).
  • Palace sites — the choice moved from Kyŏngbok to Kyŏnghŭi Palace, with its links to Kojong (Henry 2014p. 96) (Henry 2014p. 97).
  • Finance — a budget near ten times the 1907 event, and a Korean government unable to raise the money (Henry 2014p. 96) (Henry 2014p. 97).

Questions for study

  1. Why did organisers think a palace site was more "symbolically potent" than the settler district used in 1907, and what did that choice ask visitors to believe?
  2. What does the failure to raise funds suggest about the limits of protectorate power over Korean institutions?
  3. How did planning a display at a palace associated with reform relate to the protectorate's forced abdication of Kojong?

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
1Background of the Time (1905-10)p. 9Asia

Hotta-Lister sets out the diplomatic and economic setting for the exhibition in the years 1905 to 1910. Japan had become a great power after the Russo-Japanese War, and its legations in London, Washington and other capitals were upgraded to embassies between 1906 and 1908. The chapter then traces strains in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which British newspapers and officials increasingly questioned over Manchuria, the 'open door' pledge and Japanese immigration. It goes on to tariff revision, which Komura pursued against British objections, and to Japan's trade deficit and reliance on foreign loans. Its last sections cover the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the rivalry between Katsura and Saionji. It also surveys the Japanese and English-language newspapers on which much of the study relies, including The Times, the Japan Weekly Mail and the Chuo Shimbun. The chapter concludes that these pressures explain why the government was so committed to the exhibition.

In the contents of this work

3Exhibition Objectivesp. 74Asia

This chapter asks why the Japanese government sponsored the Japan-British Exhibition and what it hoped to gain. Hotta-Lister divide the aims into political and diplomatic, economic and educational groups. The political case is tied to three issues then pending with Britain: revision of Japan's commercial tariffs, renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and the annexation of Korea, which took place in August 1910 while the Exhibition was open. Working through speeches, newspapers and diplomatic correspondence, the authors argue that Komura and Katsura saw the Exhibition as a platform for Japan's imperial role, and they set the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial exhibits of the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908. The economic section covers City loans, exports to Britain and the trade imbalance. The educational section shows Japanese leaders trying to correct British views of Japan as warlike or backward, through the book Japan Today and The Times Japanese Edition. The chapter ends with these publications.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
