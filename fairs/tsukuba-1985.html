Tsukuba Expo ’85 was a Special Exposition, registered with the BIE, held at Tsukuba Science City in Ibaraki Prefecture from 17 March to 16 September 1985 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365; Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). The Japan Association for the International Exposition organised it, and the national government and large corporations promoted it heavily (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). It drew 20,334,727 visitors, more than any other special exposition of its time (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The texts present it as a display of computers, robots and high technology, and as an attempt to turn a farming district into a national science city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366; Allwood et al. 2001p. 163).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The Japanese government chose Tsukuba, about 33 miles north-east of Tokyo, as a satellite capital under its policy of dispersing population from the capital (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 364). The Science and Technology Agency, a branch of MITI, announced a concept for a science exposition in 1978, but the BIE did not endorse so narrow a theme (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). A task force led by Jiro Ushio devised a theme on dwellings, surroundings, and science and technology for man at home, which the BIE approved in 1980 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). Narita dates the registration as a Special Exposition to 1981 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). Allwood, Allan and Reid give a different sequence. After the BIE-registered American fairs of 1982 and 1984 had weakened sponsors' confidence, Japan planned an event to put Tsukuba on the map (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). They say the BIE, pressed by Japan, which had meant to ask for 1986, accepted a legal loophole and registered Tsukuba for 1985 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 154). The texts do not explain how the 1981 registration in Narita's account relates to the 1985 date in Allwood's.

Organisers and finance

The Japan Association for the International Exposition prepared, organised and operated the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). Narita says the fair was "known as Dentsu Expo"; the advertising agency Dentsu produced 18 of the 28 private pavilions, and Hakuhodo four more (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366). Allwood reports that the cost was 117 billion yen, and that the fair made a profit, as he says all Japanese expos seem to (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). The total spent in Japan by public and private sectors was 1,157.9 billion yen, which Allwood says stimulated 2,316.3 billion yen of production in the national economy (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165). The American pavilion was funded in part by four major corporations, which contributed $6 million, some 40 percent of the congressional appropriation for American participation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365).

Site and buildings

The site covered about 100 hectares, or 246 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). Allwood's events table gives 250 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210). Narita describes colourful, geometric pavilions, several designed by Kisho Kurokawa, including the Toshiba and Mitsui pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The entry in the 1990 dictionary says the master plan called for eight self-contained blocks, so that the fair became a set of fairs within a fair, without the vistas of the Paris, New York, Brussels and Osaka fairs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). It adds that no innovative architectural symbol, such as the Eiffel Tower or the Trylon and Perisphere, was unveiled (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). Allwood says the internationals were slotted into four pavilions, the best of them a white tent-like structure by Fumihiko Maki (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). The 1990 entry spells the architect Fumihiko Mako (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). The entry describes the transit systems, the Sky-Ride cable car, the Vista-Liner minirail and the Mini-Bus, as well designed but poorly employed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360).

Exhibits and attractions

The Japanese corporate pavilions drew the largest queues. Allwood lists Fujitsu's robot, TDK's animal and insect views, Fuyo's stage musical performed by robots, a Sony screen 25 by 40 metres, and a Japan Air Lines magnetic levitation train (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). Fujitsu's pavilion used the Canadian Omnimax system to show DNA, and NEC's theatre took the audience on a journey to the cosmos (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). Narita names the huge intelligent robot in the Fujitsu pavilion as Fanuc-man, part of the same computerised display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The Japanese government pavilions showed robotics, hydroponics, space and housing (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). Narita says the most popular exhibit was hydroponically grown tomatoes, and that the most popular amusement area was the Children's Plaza, which taught the principles of science through play (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The US pavilion, devoted to artificial intelligence, used robots under a heading that calls them robots that think, and showed computers that could solve the Rubik's Cube (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). Narita describes Masanori Hata's TDK presentation, the Elephinsect Bird, which combined an elephant's nose, an insect's eyes and a bird's flight (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366).

Who took part

Narita gives 47 countries and 37 international organisations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). Allwood's text speaks of 41 nations (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164), Massidda's table gives 48 countries (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192), and Allwood's events table gives 50 international participants (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210). The texts do not reconcile these figures. Narita counts 28 Japanese corporate participants (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The colour-plate caption in Allwood, Allan and Reid says the foreign exhibitors found it hard to compete with Japanese scientific skill (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81).

Visitors and reception

The official figure of 20,334,727 visitors is given by Narita (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365), and Allwood gives over 20 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). Allwood describes the Japanese audience as having taken the medium to heart, and enjoying the fairground atmosphere as well as the technical information (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). He also describes an elderly couple queuing for a pavilion who declined an invitation to enter, saying they valued the conversation in the waiting lines (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). He reports that 30 per cent of Japanese middle-school students visited, as did 76 per cent of the nation's scientific and corporate research practitioners (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). Allwood judges the theme, Science and Technology for Man at Home, to have evaded the harsh realities of science and the environment, and calls the expo "a world viewed through rose-coloured spectacles" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). Narita argues that most pavilions followed similar patterns, so that alert visitors gave in to monotony (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366). Allwood quotes a Toronto newspaper which read the fair's message as bigness, richness, power, leisure and speed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164).

Its place in the series

Japan planned its next official expo for 1985 as soon as the BIE timetable allowed, after Okinawa 1975, which Allwood describes as a success in political terms (Allwood et al. 2001p. 162). Kobe Portopia 81 followed. It ran for six months outside the BIE rules and drew over 16 million visitors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Allwood says its Japanese know-how was to be used in 1985 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). At New Orleans in 1984, the Japanese pavilion promoted a visit to Expo 85 the following year (Allwood et al. 2001p. 153). Vancouver's planners changed their date to 1986 when Tsukuba had 1985 locked up (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 368). Massidda writes that, until Tsukuba was appointed for 1985, specialised and international exhibitions had substantially lost a credible global dimension (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 191). He spells the name Tsukaba (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 191). Brisbane's Fujitsu pavilion reprised a 3-D film first shown three years earlier at Tsukuba (Peterson 2020p. 165). Narita's Brisbane entry says its novel transport system was bought from the Mitsubishi corporation after it had been used at Expo 85 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 371). Japan then used the BIE's garden-expo category for Osaka 1990, which Allwood says became a hybrid expo (Allwood et al. 2001p. 165).

Aftermath and legacy

Allwood says Tsukuba Science City was put on the map, and that it now holds over 150 research establishments (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164). The Expo Science Center, a permanent legacy, stood in downtown Tsukuba City (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360). The Tsukuba Expo Center was the one permanent facility of the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365). The fair's records are held by the Ibaraki Prefectural Library and by the Tsukuba Expo Memorial Foundation, based at the Expo Center, which became a permanent memorial site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 367).

How historians have interpreted it

Three authors interpret the fair at length. Allwood, Allan and Reid read Tsukuba as a state project to make a science city, and judge its message to have evaded the environmental and social issues of its time (Allwood et al. 2001p. 163). Narita reads the pavilions as a concentration on high technology, shaped by public relations experts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366). Massidda places the fair at the end of a period, from 1970 onward, in which specialised and international exhibitions had substantially lost a credible global dimension (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 191).

Themes in the scholarship

  • High technology and robotics — Narita finds the pavilions concentrated on high technology, especially robotics and biotechnology (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366); Allwood lists the robot shows of Fujitsu and Fuyo (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164); the 1990 entry names the Fanuc robot (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360).
  • Corporate sponsorship — Dentsu produced 18 of the 28 private pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 366); four corporations funded much of the American participation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 365).
  • Science city and national policy — the government chose a satellite-city site for science (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 364); Allwood says the fair put Tsukuba Science City on the map (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164).
  • Economic return — Allwood reports a cost of 117 billion yen and a profit (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164); his events table lists a profit (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210); the 1990 entry gives no figure for profit or loss (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360).
  • Architecture and site — eight self-contained blocks and no landmark symbol (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 360); the best foreign pavilion was by Fumihiko Maki (Allwood et al. 2001p. 164).

Allwood's chapter covers the exhibitions held in Japan, Australia and Korea between 1975 and 1999, and argues that Japan turned the medium into a state-led tool of regional and economic development, which its neighbours then copied. It opens with Okinawa 1975, where an ocean theme and the Aquapolis floating platform were set on a former battlefield, and moves through Tsukuba 1985, which used computers, robots and audio-visual display to promote a science city. Its main case is Osaka 1990, a garden exposition that the organisers turned into a hybrid expo within the BIE's rules, with 82 nations and a record attendance. The chapter then surveys Brisbane 1988, whose shaded riverside site and state pavilions are described, Taejon 1993, where Korean corporate pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors, and the Kunming 1999 garden expo. It ends with the Philippines' withdrawal from a recognised exposition for Manila in 1992, and concludes that developing nations were still denied a realistic route to hosting.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. Each case is a chapter, organised by fair, using archival and ethnographic methods. The conclusion argues that state-directed national representation has declined as the form became costly for host cities, and that Asia has become the leading host of expositions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are the whole subject of this work, and their coverage is central. Japan is the main case in chapters 2 and 4 (the government site, 'Japan Beautiful' concession and fine arts galleries at San Francisco 1915; the pavilion and Takarazuka Revue at New York 1939-40). China fills chapter 3 (the official site and the Chinese Village at San Francisco 1915, with Chinatown's role) and chapter 5 (Expo '88 in Brisbane). The Philippines (chapter 6) and Indonesia (chapter 7) are studied through their New York 1964-65 pavilions, South Korea (chapter 8) and Thailand (chapter 9) through Milan 2015, and chapter 10 treats the Chinese Milan pavilion and Asia's rise as host. Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Wikipedia

Expo '85, officially called the International Exhibition, Tsukuba Japan 1985 (国際科学技術博覧会, Kokusai Kagaku Gijutsu Hakurankai; "The International Science Technology Exposition"), was a world's fair held in Tsukuba Science City, Ibaraki, Japan between Sunday, March 17 and Monday, September 16, 1985. The main venue was located in town of Yatabe, and the sub venue was in village of Sakura. The theme of the fair was "Dwellings and surroundings – Science and Technology for Man at Home". Attendance was over 20 million and 48 countries participated, along with several companies.
