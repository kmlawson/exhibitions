Omaha · 1899

Greater America Exposition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Greater America Exposition was held at Omaha, Nebraska, in 1899 (Rydell 1984). In the July after the 1898 Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition closed, Omaha businessmen reopened that fair's grounds and buildings under the new name (Rydell 1984). It stayed open for three months and failed (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Rydell links it to the federal turn to the exposition as a display of colonialism, which he says culminated at St Louis in 1904 (Rydell 1984).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Rydell says the 1899 event was the 1898 fair reopened under a new name (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia gives a different link: the remaining holdings of the 1898 fair were sold to the Greater American Exposition for $17,500, and its organisers hoped to repeat the 1898 financial success (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Rydell adds that the 1899 fair used many of the same structures built for 1898 (Rydell 1984). Rydell reproduces its 1899 prospectus as an illustration, and the book mentions the fair nowhere else except in an index entry (Rydell 1993p. 17; Rydell 1993p. 266).

Empire and colony

The sponsors stated that the purpose was "to exploit the wonders of the colonial possessions" (Rydell 1984). Their advertising promised exhibits of the material resources, products and industries of islands that the United States had recently acquired, and of their native peoples (Rydell 1984). The promoters brought in a second Indian encampment, placed under P. E. Iller, owner of the Willow Springs Distillery (Rydell 1984). Rydell argues that federal funding after 1898 went to administering the newly acquired territories rather than to the Omaha fair, and that the government never objected to a colonial exhibition in principle (Rydell 1984). He then argues that federal authorities turned to the exposition to show that colonialism was central to continued American progress, and that this culminated at St Louis in 1904, where nearly one thousand Filipinos were put on exhibit in the Philippine Reservation (Rydell 1984).

Who took part

Rydell names Frederick Cummins as manager of the Indian Congress at the Greater America Exposition of 1899 (Rydell 1984). The passages do not say how Cummins's Indian Congress related to Iller's encampment, which the text describes as another encampment (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

Visitors and reception

The passages read give no attendance figure for the 1899 fair (Rydell 1984). The text calls its end "a huge joke and failure" and gives the federal government's non-participation as the main problem, apparently (Rydell 1984).

Aftermath and legacy

According to the encyclopedia, the grounds were cleared of the remaining structures, the lagoon was filled in, and Kountze Park was established as a public park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148).

Its place in the series

Rydell argues that the Buffalo Pan-American Exposition set the stage for the St Louis spectacle of 1904 (Rydell 1984). Cummins, the 1899 manager, later received the Indian Congress concession at Buffalo because of stronger financial backing from the Citizens Bank of Buffalo (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial possessions as subject — the sponsors' stated purpose and the advertised exhibits of newly acquired islands and their peoples (Rydell 1984)
  • Reuse of the 1898 fair — the same structures were reused, and the encyclopedia says the 1898 holdings were sold to the 1899 organisers (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148)
  • Indigenous display — a second Indian encampment under P. E. Iller, and Cummins as manager of an Indian Congress (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984)
  • Federal government and annexation — federal money after 1898 went to the new territories, and Rydell reads the government's absence as the main problem (Rydell 1984)
  • Finance and failure — the organisers hoped to repeat the 1898 success; the fair failed after three months (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148)
  • Series of fairs — Rydell links it to the federal use of expositions for colonial display, which culminated at St Louis in 1904 (Rydell 1984)

Questions for study

  1. What evidence would show why the federal government stayed out of the 1899 fair, and how far Rydell's account of its absence rests on argument rather than documents? (Rydell 1984)
  2. Rydell calls the 1899 event a reopening of the 1898 fair, while the encyclopedia describes a sale of the 1898 holdings. Which account better explains the fair's finances? (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148)
  3. Why did the texts name different men for the Indian displays, and what would the Indian Congress and the encampment have been for visitors? (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984)
  4. Why would the sponsors advertise islands recently acquired by the United States at an Omaha fair, and what did they expect visitors to learn from it? (Rydell 1984)

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
4The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition, Omaha, 1898: “Concomitant to Empire”Asia

Rydell reads the Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition of 1898 as an ideological event staged as the Spanish-American War began. He traces how Omaha's businessmen and the Knights of Ak-Sar-Ben backed the fair, and how Edward Rosewater and the Bureau of American Ethnology's James Mooney shaped its Indian Congress, a display first imagined as a vast encampment of all tribes. The main evidence is the Congress itself: its staged sham battle, the parade before President McKinley, and Mooney's complaints that the show had become a Wild West show. Midway villages of African Americans, Chinese, and Filipinos, and the Buffalo Bill day, are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and empire. The chapter closes with the fair's record attendance and the Greater America Exposition that followed the next year, which turned the exposition movement toward the colonies.

Rydell ends his chapter on the 1898 Trans-Mississippi fair by describing its reopening the next July as the Greater America Exposition, which its sponsors aimed at the colonial possessions. He reads the fair as one step in the federal government's turn to the exposition form to show colonialism as central to American progress, a turn that he says culminated at St Louis in 1904. He reports that it stayed open three months and fizzled out, apparently mainly because the federal government did not take part. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.
