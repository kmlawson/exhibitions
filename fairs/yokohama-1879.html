The First Japanese Agricultural Products Exhibition was held in Yokohama in 1879 (Parkp. 7). Park, writing on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition, says that the term kyōshinkai ('gathering for mutual progress') originated in this show (Parkp. 7). It was a competitive exhibition for a single kind of farm product (Parkp. 7). Park's note credits Matsukata Masayoshi with organising the first kyōshinkai, but the text names no organiser for the Yokohama show itself (Parkp. 7).

Origins of the term

Park traces the word kyōshinkai to a Japanese agricultural product exhibition held in Yokohama in 1879 (Parkp. 7). At first the kyōshinkai was a small competitive exhibition for one kind of agricultural product (Parkp. 7). Later the word referred to a large-scale exposition that displayed products and staged spectacular, entertaining events in Japan (Parkp. 7). The text gives no venue, exhibit list or visitor figures for the Yokohama show (Parkp. 7).

Organisers and models

Park's note 21 says that Matsukata Masayoshi, then head of the Agricultural Promotion Bureau in the Ministry of the Interior, organised the first kyōshinkai (Parkp. 7). He was impressed by French agricultural fairs that improved farm products through competition and awards (Parkp. 7). The note cites Reischauer's Samurai and Silk, pp. 221–22, for this (Parkp. 7). Park does not state directly that Matsukata organised the Yokohama show; the link rests on the term and on this note (Parkp. 7).

Its place in the series

Park presents the term as the source of later uses. After industrial exhibitions were introduced into Korea, local governments there began to plan kongjinhoe (Parkp. 7). The word kongjinhoe, rather than pangnamhoe, became the official title of the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Parkp. 6p. 7). Korean newspapers covered later Japanese kyōshinkai, among them the Fukuoka Kyōshinkai of 1909 and the Nagoya Kyōshinkai of 1910 (Parkp. 7).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Origin of the term — Park treats the Yokohama show as the source of the word that Korea later used for its industrial exhibitions (Parkp. 7p. 6).
  • Competition in farm produce — the first kyōshinkai is described as a contest for one product, later widened into a spectacle with entertainments (Parkp. 7).
  • French models — Matsukata is said to have been impressed by French agricultural fairs that used competition and awards (Parkp. 7).
  • Colonial expositions — Park reads the industrial exhibitions that followed in Korea as the start, in earnest, of colonial expositions tied to Japan's colonial project after 1910 (Parkp. 7).

Questions for study

  1. What did a contest for one farm product offer its organisers that a large spectacle did not, and what changed the word's meaning? (Parkp. 7)
  2. Note 21 names Matsukata as organiser of the first kyōshinkai. What other evidence would show that he also organised the Yokohama show? (Parkp. 7)
  3. The Governor General asked that the 1915 event not be called an exposition, because it would show too few exhibits. What does that objection suggest about how the colonial government wanted the event read? (Parkp. 7)
  4. Park links the Yokohama show to the vocabulary of later exhibitions, not to their content. How far does that connection support an account of colonial expositions that starts in 1879? (Parkp. 7)

Park's article on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition mentions this exhibition only in passing, in its opening section and in footnote 21. It names the Yokohama agricultural products exhibition of 1879 as the origin of the term kyōshinkai ('gathering for mutual progress'), which Korea later used as kongjinhoe. Park credits Matsukata Masayoshi with organising the first kyōshinkai, but does not state that he organised the Yokohama show itself. The article gives no venue, exhibits or visitor figures for it, and uses the exhibition to show how a Japanese word for a competitive agricultural fair became a term for large colonial-era expositions.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
