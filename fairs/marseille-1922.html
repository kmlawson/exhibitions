National Colonial Exhibition

Exposition nationale coloniale
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition nationale coloniale, or National Colonial Exhibition, was held at Marseille from April to October 1922 (Hale 2008p. 99). Adrien Artaud was its commissioner general, after the death of Jules Charles-Roux in 1918 (Hale 2008p. 88). It was the second colonial exposition in Marseille after 1906, and Greenhalgh calls it state-funded (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119). It showed colonial soldiers, villages and an Angkor Wat replica, and Hale reads it as a display that kept racial hierarchy while speaking of brotherhood (Hale 2008p. 99; Hale 2008p. 147).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and organisers

The Marseille colonial shows grew from proposals of the 1890s and 1900s. In 1894 L. Mirinny, an engineer, first proposed a Marseille colonial annex to the 1900 Paris fair (Hale 2008p. 86). Dr Edouard Heckel proposed a national colonial exposition in 1902 (Hale 2008p. 87), and Charles-Roux was commissioner general of the 1906 show (Hale 2008p. 87). A 1916 show was planned, but war put the plans on hold in August 1914 (Hale 2008p. 87). After Charles-Roux died in September 1918, Artaud was named commissioner general (Hale 2008p. 88). Our timeline names Charles-Roux as the organiser of 1922; the texts give that role to Artaud.

Paris contested the event. Its municipal council had approved a colonial exposition for 1921 or 1922 (Hale 2008p. 88). A compromise in spring 1919 gave Marseille 1922 and Paris 1925 (Hale 2008p. 88). Other texts date the earlier plans differently. Geppert records a 1913 government decision for Marseille (Geppert 2010p. 181), and Rydell a plan for Marseille in 1916 and Paris in 1920 (Rydell 1993p. 63).

Greenhalgh calls the 1906 and 1922 shows state-funded and says they were almost certainly the result of agitation from the South (Greenhalgh 1988p. 119). Hale's endnotes name a 13-member committee for the 1922 exposition (Hale 2008p. 199). Roche's table of expo attendances and profits has no row for it (Roche 2000p. 43).

Site and display

Hale calls the 1922 exposition the most extensive showcase yet of the peoples and resources of the overseas empire (Hale 2008p. 99). Its Angkor Wat reconstruction, about seventy metres on each side, was larger than the West African and Algerian pavilions (Hale 2008p. 147). Greenhalgh says villages from most African territories were built (Greenhalgh 1988p. 90). A West African village held nearly 100 Africans, more than twice the number at the West African section of Paris 1900 (Hale 2008p. 101).

Empire and colony

Marseille was a French port, not a colony, and the exhibition presented the colonies as France's partners (Hale 2008p. 110). Geppert calls Marseille the proverbial port of the empire (Geppert 2010p. 180). Hale lists the sections: West Africa, Equatorial Africa, Madagascar, Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco and Indochina (Hale 2008p. 100p. 104p. 106p. 124p. 126p. 129p. 147). Organisers stressed the native soldier as indispensable to France (Hale 2008p. 88). Equatorial Africa gave its buildings to a Pas-de-Calais village damaged in the war, to show that colonial exports could help rebuild France (Hale 2008p. 104).

Colonised people took part as performers, guards and exhibitors. Governor General Martial Merlin lectured invited African chiefs on serving France during African Week (Hale 2008p. 99). Tirailleurs sénégalais stood guard at the pavilions and performed in festivals (Hale 2008p. 100). Hale says the images "perpetuated the idea of black inferiority at the same time that organizers’ discourse expressed republican values such as brotherhood and equality" (Hale 2008p. 100). Greenhalgh quotes a Marseille source saying that the city "thinks colonially" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 69). The exposition's health director, Dr Charles Platon, planned strict medical control of the colonised participants, and in April 1922 the commissioner general announced that all of them, especially the Indochinese, had to be vaccinated (Hale 2008p. 202).

Visitors and reception

President Millerand visited in May, and at the official reception he said France had never considered colonial peoples to be inferior races (Hale 2008p. 132p. 154). Geppert records one critic, the official Camille Fidel, who found the 1922 show less picturesque and more expensive than Wembley (Geppert 2010p. 174).

Place in the series

Hale says the 1906 success led the city to plan a show for 1916 and every ten years after (Hale 2008p. 87). Roche lists the interwar French series as Marseille 1922, Paris 1925, 1931 and 1937, and sets Marseille beside the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley (Roche 2000p. 42; Roche 2000p. 59). Rydell says both fairs were conceived before 1914 (Rydell 1993p. 63). Geppert says Paris planners watched the Marseille and Wembley shows closely (Geppert 2010p. 181).

How historians have interpreted it

Hale reads the interwar expositions as part of a shift to a cooperative view of empire, in which colonies were partners (Hale 2008p. 110). Rydell argues that Marseille revived the national colonial exhibition tradition without making it seem modern (Rydell 1993p. 67). Geppert treats the Marseille shows as side-routes that left little lasting mark on the city (Geppert 2010p. 182).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Indochina at the 1922 fair

Hale says Indochina made an especially memorable showing at Marseille in 1922, thanks to the local committees and to Albert Sarraut, a former governor general of the region (Hale 2008p. 159). For some visitors the Angkor Wat temple was the central attraction of the whole fair (Hale 2008p. 147). Sarraut's description, as Hale quotes it, runs: "It can’t be described. It is sublime and superhuman." (Hale 2008p. 147). The temple combined a pagoda from the 1889 Paris fair with a tower from the Bayon, built for the 1906 Marseille Cambodian section (Hale 2008p. 147).

Performers and living displays

The local committees and the army sent 300 Indochinese to demonstrate trades, populate villages and perform (Hale 2008p. 150). A Laotian village was recreated near the temple (Hale 2008p. 150). Contemporary writer Charles Regismanset called it Cambodian, though the maps said Laotian (Hale 2008p. 202). A Vietnamese theatre company and a puppet company performed, and Laotian singers and musicians appeared (Hale 2008p. 152). King Sisowath's Cambodian ballet was the most acclaimed attraction (Hale 2008p. 152). Two Cambodian businessmen objected to the planned engagement, one on musical grounds and one on cost, but by mid-April 1921 officials had decided to hire the dancers (Hale 2008p. 152). On 21 July Governor General Maurice Long watched a show on the temple steps (Hale 2008p. 155).

Reading the image

Hale argues that the image of Indochina in these expositions leaned on Cambodia, although Vietnam had the larger population, and presented Indochina as a feminised land that France would protect (Hale 2008p. 159).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial service — Hale shows the African soldiers and the message of service to France as the exposition's central theme (Hale 2008p. 99p. 100)
  • Villages and living displays — villages from most African territories and a West African village of nearly 100 people (Greenhalgh 1988p. 90; Hale 2008p. 101p. 150)
  • Angkor Wat and Indochina — the temple as the centre of the Indochinese section, and Cambodia as the emblem of Indochina (Hale 2008p. 147p. 159)
  • Paris rivalry — Marseille's claim to a colonial show against Paris's, settled in 1919 (Hale 2008p. 88; Geppert 2010p. 181)
  • Reception and critics — Millerand's visit and Fidel's unfavourable comparison with Wembley (Hale 2008p. 132; Geppert 2010p. 174)

Questions for study

  1. Hale says the images of Africans kept black inferiority while speeches spoke of brotherhood. How far can an exhibition's stated aims be read from its images? (Hale 2008p. 100)
  2. Why did Marseille compete with Paris for a colonial show, and what did each city hope to gain? (Hale 2008p. 88; Geppert 2010p. 181)
  3. Two Cambodian businessmen objected to hiring the royal dancers. What does the record show of colonised people's own view of their performances? (Hale 2008p. 152)
  4. Hale argues that Indochina's image rested on Cambodia rather than Vietnam. What does that choice suggest about French ideas of protection? (Hale 2008p. 159)

Hale, Dana S. Races on Display: French Representations of Colonized Peoples, 1886–1940. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2008.
Introduction to Part 2p. 85Asia

Hale introduces Part II by noting that the First World War changed French views of the empire, since African, Malagasy and Indochinese labourers and soldiers kept French industry running and the army supplied. The text sketches the planning of colonial fairs, from the 1906 Marseilles exposition through the 1922 Marseilles event to the Paris exposition of 1931. It ends by previewing the trademark and exhibition chapters on 1914 to 1940.

In the contents of this work

5Sub-Saharan Africans: La Force Noirep. 91

Hale asks how French images of black Africans changed after the First World War, when the tirailleurs sénégalais were praised as defenders of France and the empire was described as a family. The chapter reads trademarks registered in 1914 to 1940, which show black soldiers as friendly, childlike figures, alongside comic children, banana caricatures and entertainers. It then turns to the colonial expositions at Marseilles in 1922 and Paris in 1931, where African troops, dancers and village artisans were displayed, and where Franco-African brotherhood was preached alongside images of primitive strength. Hale describes the parades, festivals and Paris performances built around African soldiers, and the ethnographic notices that graded groups by labour value. The last case is the 1937 Paris fair on the Ile des Cygnes, where craftsmen were housed on site and two Cameroonian artisans were overcome by carbon monoxide from an illegal stove. Hale concludes that the force noire image endured, and that the language of fraternity changed the rhetoric of colonial power without changing the status of Africans.

In the contents of this work

6North Africans: Fils Aînép. 118

Hale argues that French images of North Africa between the wars cast Algeria, Tunisia and Morocco as the firstborn son of the colonial family while keeping their Arab and Berber inhabitants inside Orientalist stereotypes. The first evidence is commercial trademarks registered in Paris and Marseilles from 1914 to 1940, which used veiled women, warriors, camels, Turkish pipes and Islamic motifs to sell products from coffee and tobacco to grain, wine and insecticide. The chapter then turns to the 1922 exposition in Marseilles, the 1931 exposition in Paris and the 1937 world's fair, examining Moorish palaces, reconstructed souks and cafés, staged performances such as the Fantasia and the Rifle Dance, and the rival claims of officials such as Lyautey and Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. Arab and Berber candidates were absent from the 1937 Meilleur Mariage Colonial contest, which Hale reads as a limit to the eugenic argument for colonial marriage. The chapter concludes that officials presented North Africans both as exotic entertainers and as children needing French training, a blindness that it ties to the bloody struggle for independence that followed.

In the contents of this work

7Indochinese: Fils Douép. 141Asia

Hale argues that French officials and entrepreneurs praised Indochinese as the most gifted of the colonial children, the fils doué, while treating them as subordinates who needed French guidance. Trademarks registered in Paris and Marseilles show the same pattern: tea, cloth, soap and paint labels used straw-hatted workers, servants and exotic women, and registrations barely rose after 1914. The chapter then follows the official display at the 1922 exposition in Marseilles, where a full-scale Angkor Wat, Cambodian royal dancers, a Rue Annamite and a dragon procession carried the message, and at the 1931 exposition in Paris, where ethnographic directives sorted peoples into barbarian and advanced groups and a plan to use Chinese rickshaw pullers was dropped after a protest by the Chinese consul. The 1937 Khmer-style pavilion and artisan workshops on the Ile des Cygnes are read as a claim to protect a declining civilisation. The chapter concludes that this gentle and talented image masked Vietnamese opposition to French rule, which the French public discovered only abruptly a decade after the 1937 fair.

In the contents of this work

8La Mère-Patrie and Her Colonial Children: France on Displayp. 161Asia

Hale examines how the colonial expositions and trademarks of the Third Republic presented France to its own public, portraying sub-Saharan Africans, North Africans and Indochinese as children of a parental mère-patrie. Trademark counts from Paris and Marseilles (656 for sub-Saharan Africa, 628 for North Africa and 259 for Indochina) open the argument, followed by a survey of the Paris exposition of 1900, the Marseilles exhibitions of 1906 and 1922, the Paris exposition of 1931 and the 1937 world's fair, showing how organisers such as Charles-Roux and Lyautey used colonial troops, living displays and performances. Hale distinguishes a parental, hierarchical France from a fraternal image of brothers that appeared after the war, and reads the second as a response to colonial military service and to anti-imperialist protest, including a surrealist tract against the 1931 exposition. The chapter concludes that colonial peoples were displayed as objects of protection and entertainment, and that the soldiers of the empire, ironically, were the ones who defended France in its hour of need.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
ThreeColoniale Modernep. 61Asia

Rydell argues that colonial expositions between the wars made empire look like part of modernity, a fusion he calls coloniale moderne, and that American fairs adopted it. He begins with the Marseilles colonial fair of 1922 and the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where living colonial subjects and a pageant of empire were shown, and then the 1925 Paris decorative arts fair, where French colonial pavilions were given modernistic designs. The central case is the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, with its Angkor Wat replica and the U.S. government's prefabricated Mount Vernon, stocked with artifacts from the overseas territories. Rydell shows how protests in Puerto Rico and the Philippines were erased from the American display. The chapter then turns to Chicago's 1933 fair, which borrowed the colonial villages idea, and to San Francisco's 1939 Golden Gate exposition, where Pacific Basin pavilions and a Latin American court presented the West as a market for American goods. Rydell concludes that these fairs tied American expansion to the imperial policies of European powers.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hale asks how the Third Republic represented its colonial subjects and, through them, its own identity. Using trademark labels registered in Paris and Marseilles from 1886 to 1940 and the colonial sections of the fairs in those cities, the book compares sub-Saharan Africans, North Africans and Indochinese. Part I (1886–1913) covers the empire, racial theory and the Paris fairs of 1889 and 1900; Part II (1914–1940) covers the Marseilles and Paris expositions of 1922, 1931 and 1937. A final chapter treats France's self-image as parent and brother to its colonial children. Hale argues that the images stressed human difference and French superiority, and that this framework still shapes France's ties with its former colonies.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are central to the book, but almost entirely through Indochina, one of the three colonial groups the study is built around. Chapters 4 ('Indochinese: Gentle Subjects') and 7 ('Indochinese: Fils Doué') are devoted to it, covering trademarks and the 1900, 1922, 1931 and 1937 fairs. Chapter 1 covers Indochina's conquest and its pavilions at the 1889 Paris fair, and chapter 8 treats the Indochinese as part of France's self-presentation. Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos appear as conquered territories and as exhibits: the Angkor Wat reconstructions of 1922 and 1931, the Cambodian royal ballet, the Théâtre Annamite, the dragon procession and the Rue Annamite, with artisans, soldiers and performers housed on the fairgrounds. Asian visitors include the prince of Annam in 1889. East Asia gets only passing attention. Chinese appear as neighbours seen as troublesome in the Tonkin affair that opens chapter 4, and as workers in pay and hours disputes (chapter 4), and as rickshaw pullers whose planned employment in Paris in 1931 was dropped after the Chinese consul protested (chapter 7). Japan and Korea get no discussion, though kimono-clad women recur on trademarks. In Southeast Asia, Thailand appears only as a threat to Cambodia, and the Javanese dancers of 1889 and the Malays of the 1931 ethnographic directives are passing mentions; the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma are absent.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
ch. 3 'Coloniale Moderne' (pp. 63-64, 69-70)

Rydell opens his chapter on colonial modernity with the 1922 Marseille fair, which he treats as a pre-1914 project revived after the war. He argues that Marseille and Wembley were cut from the same cloth, using colonial display to present empire as an economic necessity. He describes Marseille as dominated by an Angkor Wat model and North African pavilions, with thousands of living colonial subjects, and says the fair covered roughly five times the colonial display area of Paris 1900. He argues that the Marseille show revitalised a national colonial exhibition tradition without making it look modern, and that its Angkor Wat replica inspired the Indochina section of Paris 1931.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

In a chapter on Louise Faure-Favier's novel Blanche et Noir, Robles notes that ethnographic human displays were still common in the 1920s. She names the 1922 National Colonial Exhibition in Marseille as one example, alongside a 1925 Fulani exhibition at the Jardin d'Acclimatation.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

Roche places the 1922 Marseille exposition in a survey of imperial expos, as one of the interwar French series that followed the nineteenth-century Paris fairs. He notes that it was closely followed by the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley. His table of attendances and profits has no row for the Marseille show.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Çelik, Zeynep. Displaying the Orient: Architecture of Islam at Nineteenth-Century World’s Fairs. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1992.
Epilogue (p. 98 onward)

In his epilogue Celik lists the 1922 Marseille show among French colonial exhibitions that repeated the structures of the 1889 Paris fair. He argues that these pavilions combined exoticism with the civilising mission of expansionism. He uses the example to argue that twentieth-century specialised fairs kept the formats of nineteenth-century expositions.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Çelik examines how Islamic architecture was built, displayed and read at nineteenth-century world's fairs, centred on the Paris expositions of 1867, 1878, 1889 and 1900, Vienna in 1873 and Chicago in 1893. His argument is that the fairs claimed scientific accuracy while indulging fantasy, so that the Islamic quarters and national pavilions show both how Europeans constructed the Orient and how Ottoman and Egyptian rulers sought a modern cultural identity. The chapters move from the visitors and displayed peoples, to the street-level quarters, to the national pavilions of the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Iran, Tunisia, Morocco and Algeria, to the Ottoman and Egyptian exposition experience, and then to the influence of pavilion design on neo-Islamic building in Istanbul, Cairo and the West.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive slight attention, and the mentions are passing. In chapter 2 (Islamic Quarters) the account of Garnier's History of Habitation at the Paris 1889 exposition quotes a classification that places Chinese and Japanese dwellings among "contemporary versions of primitive civilizations", a display organised by a French architect for European visitors, and Drevet's 1878 Paris facades for the Siamese and Annamese pavilions are said to need a symbolic architecture alongside the Muslim nations. In chapter 3 (National Pavilions) Drevet's 1878 foreign section again includes Siam and Annam, and Çelik notes that Luxembourg, Greece, China, Japan and Russia also commissioned French architects, but no Chinese or Japanese building is described. Javanese figures in a parade description quoted in chapter 1, and a Javanese village appears in a Midway description at Chicago in 1893 in chapter 2. Asian participation, state commissioners, merchants and Asian visitors are not discussed at all, and the Introduction sets aside colonial discourse about occupied territories, so Indochina and the Dutch East Indies get no treatment as such.
