Berlin Trade Exhibition

Berliner Gewerbe-Ausstellung
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Berliner Gewerbe-Ausstellung (Berlin Trade Exhibition) was a privately organised trade fair held in Treptower Park, south-east Berlin, from 1 May to 15 October 1896 (Geppert 2010p. 1; Geppert 2010p. 40). It stood in for the international exposition that Wilhelm II had blocked, and its organisers called it a rehearsal for a later world exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 19; Geppert 2010p. 60). The texts treat it mainly as a test of Berlin's claim to be a world city, and they mention its German colonial section, a Cairo reconstruction and an old Berlin quarter (Geppert 2010p. 60; Geppert 2010p. 44; Geppert 2010p. 49; Geppert 2010p. 52).

Background and aims

Germany had never held an international exhibition comparable to those in London or Paris (Geppert 2010p. 16). The fair grew out of the German exhibition question, which was debated in three phases: 1878-82, 1891-92 and 1907-10 (Geppert 2010p. 16; Geppert 2010p. 20). In 1892 Berlin merchants and industrialists pressed for a world exhibition, but Wilhelm II ended the plan. A letter he wrote on 20 July 1892 gave his reasons, and an official notice of 13 August 1892 closed the question (Geppert 2010p. 34). Geppert attributes the final decision to the Kaiser alone, although Chancellor Caprivi had become less opposed (Geppert 2010p. 34).

The organisers then turned to a Berlin trade fair. At a public meeting on 10 November 1892, Ludwig Max Goldberger said that "Berlin has much to show to the world and that is precisely what we are aiming at!" (Geppert 2010p. 37). Geppert says the project was downgraded at each stage, from international to national to local (Geppert 2010p. 37). The 1879 fair at Moabit was a different event, with more than 2 million visitors (Geppert 2010p. 17). Sources name the 1896 fair differently: "Berliner Gewerbe-Ausstellung" in the Encyclopedia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425) and "Industrie-Ausstellung" in the Historical Dictionary (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 400).

Organisers and finance

The fair was realised without official state support (Geppert 2010p. 45). An Arbeitsausschuss chaired by Fritz Kühnemann, with Goldberger and Bernhard Felisch as vice-chairmen, ran it (Geppert 2010p. 41). Prinz Friedrich Leopold of Prussia took general patronage with the Kaiser's consent, and Hans Hermann von Berlepsch, the Prussian minister of commerce, became honorary president (Geppert 2010p. 41). The predominant architects were Karl Hoffacker, Hans Grisebach and Bruno Schmitz (Geppert 2010p. 41).

The city of Berlin gave a guarantee fund and a subsidy of 300,000 marks, while the state built only a new railway station (Geppert 2010p. 39). Geppert reports that Alfred Kerr, writing after the closure, strategically downplayed the financial deficit (Geppert 2010p. 61).

Site and buildings

Witzleben, in west Berlin, was first chosen as the site. An exhibitors' committee then campaigned, with anti-Semitic undertones, for Treptower Park, and the city agreed on 26 April 1894 (Geppert 2010p. 38; Geppert 2010p. 39). Treptower Park covered about 120 hectares. The contract required the park to be restored, so the buildings were removed and the Neuer See was filled in once the fair closed (Geppert 2010p. 40).

About 300 smaller structures stood inside a tall wooden fence (Geppert 2010p. 42; Geppert 2010p. 42). The Hauptgebäude, 400 by 200 metres, was the focal point, and its semicircular entrance recalled the Trocadéro of 1878 (Geppert 2010p. 42; Geppert 2010p. 42; Geppert 2010p. 44). A circular electric tram took 24 minutes to cover the site, which Geppert calls a direct precursor of the moving sidewalk at Paris 1900 (Geppert 2010p. 40). Geppert says the layout did not follow the latest international design, which used national pavilions, because no foreign nation took part (Geppert 2010p. 42; Geppert 2010p. 42).

Empire and colony

The colonial exhibition was larger and more comprehensive than any earlier German colonial show, and it formed Group XXIII of the fair (Geppert 2010p. 47; Geppert 2010p. 295). Germany had acquired its first colony only twelve years earlier (Geppert 2010p. 45). The organisers gave three official aims: to reduce public ignorance of the colonies, to answer political critics and to document German imperial efforts (Geppert 2010p. 45). Geppert says colonisation was argued as a national and cultural duty rather than a mission civilisatrice, and the slogan equated the major powers of Europe with the colonial powers (Geppert 2010p. 45).

The colonies shown were German possessions: Cameroon, Togo, New Guinea and East Africa (Geppert 2010p. 46; Geppert 2010p. 47). The section had its own committee from February 1895, chaired by Graf von Schweinitz, who had commanded the German troops that conquered the East African fortress Quikuru qua Sike in 1892; the section's reproduction of a fortress was modelled on it (Geppert 2010p. 45; Geppert 2010p. 47). The Foreign Office paid at least part of the cost, because the section could serve political and propaganda purposes (Geppert 2010p. 45).

The section had two parts. An ethnological section held native villages representing the German overseas possessions, and a second section of more than 300 firms and associations showed commerce and science (Geppert 2010p. 45). Geppert reads the two halves as a contrast between the colonies in their supposedly original state and the European institutions that aimed to change them (Geppert 2010p. 46). The official report said that the natives still lived in the Stone Age (Geppert 2010p. 46). The native houses bordered Alt-Berlin, and a critic noted the coincidence with the Brandenburg colonial venture of the Grand Elector (Geppert 2010p. 46).

Between 60 and 100 people were put on display over five and a half months, and sources disagree on the number (Geppert 2010p. 47). The official guide gave 90, with 24 from Cameroon, 20 from Togo, 6 from New Guinea and about 40 from East Africa; the section's report gave 103 (Geppert 2010p. 296). They lived and worked on the site, and some slept in a reproduction of the Quikuru fortress (Geppert 2010p. 47). Geppert records conflicts with the displayed people and staff, including fistfights, which led to dismissals and left some of them homeless, and notes that professional observers did not mention these problems (Geppert 2010p. 52).

Wilhelm II was welcomed with performances on the opening day and spent time in the colonial section (Geppert 2010p. 47). Geppert suggests that he took part in colonial events, despite opposing an international exhibition, presumably because they raised his own visibility (Geppert 2010p. 47). The final report claimed that the colonial section considerably exceeded the usual framework of a Berlin exhibition, since it made the fair more than local and national (Geppert 2010p. 49).

The section drew more than two million visitors, and the official report credited the people on display with much of the interest (Geppert 2010p. 46; Geppert 2010p. 47). Geppert places these displays in the history of the Völkerschau, a practice he links with the animal dealer Carl Hagenbeck (Geppert 2010p. 48; Geppert 2010p. 48). Two of the exhibited Swahili died of illness and were buried in a Berlin cemetery (Geppert 2010p. 48).

Exhibits and attractions

The Sonderausstellung Kairo was a privately run section of about 3.4 hectares (Geppert 2010p. 42). Geppert says it was modelled on the Rue du Caire at the Paris exposition of 1889, and that it offered a selection of Cairo's oldest quarters, with about 500 Egyptians and a 38-metre replica of the Cheops pyramid (Geppert 2010p. 49). The Egyptian government supported the section (Geppert 2010p. 51). A French journalist found that the Arab inhabitants came from Damascus and had studied French in Beirut (Geppert 2010p. 50). Max Eyth, in his diary for 10 July 1896, found Kairo quite good as a picture, if somewhat overloaded (Geppert 2010p. 52).

Alt-Berlin, a retrospective city of about 4.5 hectares, formed part of the main venue on the fair's central axis. A section of this size had not been foreseen in the original concept (Geppert 2010p. 52; Geppert 2010p. 53). It was set in the second half of the seventeenth century, and its more than 500 staff wore historical costume (Geppert 2010p. 54; Geppert 2010p. 54). Almost 1.8 million tickets were sold for it alone (Geppert 2010p. 54). Lease-holders covered its historical façades with modern advertising posters, which was widely criticised (Geppert 2010p. 54). A French journal judged the reconstructions of Old Berlin and Cairo to be adaptations or imitations of what had been done elsewhere (Geppert 2010p. 56).

Visitors and reception

The fair drew almost seven and a half million visitors over 165 days (Geppert 2010p. 40). On average this was 41,000 a day, not the 55,000 expected, and it rained on 120 of the 165 days (Geppert 2010p. 41). The Vossische Zeitung reported within the first week that anyone who had not visited would be left out of conversation in the pub (Geppert 2010p. 41).

Georg Simmel published a short review in the Viennese weekly Die Zeit on 25 July 1896 (Geppert 2010p. 1). He argued that expositions were momentary centres of world civilisation, and he presented the fair as the testing ground for Berlin's new position as a world city (Geppert 2010p. 1; Geppert 2010p. 57). Geppert quotes Simmel's description of the city as "single city to which the whole world sends its products and where all the important styles of the present cultural world are put on display" (Geppert 2010p. 1). Simmel held that Berlin could never equal Paris (Geppert 2010p. 58).

Other critics were less positive. Friedrich Naumann argued that the fair was business on a grand scale and a display of capitalist production, not a pleasure trip (Geppert 2010p. 59; Geppert 2010p. 59). Julius Lessing saw much mediocrity in the choice of exhibits (Geppert 2010p. 58). Foreign reporters were sceptical: the New York Times found the fair unimpressive, and a French critic doubted that foreigners would come, since Berlin was not a city of pleasure (Geppert 2010p. 59; Geppert 2010p. 60). The Figaro called the planning a fiasco (Geppert 2010p. 37).

Aftermath and legacy

The organisers called the fair a trial run and a final rehearsal before a German universal exposition (Geppert 2010p. 60). The press agreed that greater things were to come (Geppert 2010p. 60). Soon after the closure the structures were dismantled, leaving far fewer traces in the city than other expositions (Geppert 2010p. 61). The fair gave rise to no Berlin exhibition tradition and left no direct legacy (Geppert 2010p. 61). After 1946 a Soviet war memorial was built on the site (Geppert 2010p. 40). The Kaiser's negative verdict on a Berlin world exhibition returned in 1910, during a visit to the Brussels exposition (Geppert 2010p. 35). In his conclusion Geppert writes that a fair of this scale was never repeated (Geppert 2010p. 223).

Its place in the series

Geppert connects the fair with the Chicago exposition of 1893. Hoffacker and Grisebach had designed German sections there, and the water theme echoed Chicago's Lake Michigan (Geppert 2010p. 41; Geppert 2010p. 44). Geppert also traces the retrospective principle from Turin 1884 and London 1886 to Bremen 1890, Antwerp 1894, Amsterdam 1895 and Berlin 1896 (Geppert 2010p. 52). Wilson Smith's chapter in the Filipová volume lists Alt-Berlin in 1896 among the historic-city exhibits that followed Old Edinburgh (Filipová 2015p. 207). Massidda sets Eco's account of Montreal 1967 beside Simmel's account of the Berlin fair, and says that Eco used virtually the same words (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 186).

Themes in the scholarship

  • A would-be world city — The fair was read as a test of Berlin's status, and Simmel and Geppert both judged that Berlin fell short of Paris (Geppert 2010p. 16; Geppert 2010p. 58; Geppert 2010p. 60).
  • Colonial display as national duty — The colonial section was presented as a national obligation and a way for the fair to look international (Geppert 2010p. 45; Geppert 2010p. 49).
  • Human displays — The people on show drew large crowds, and their numbers are disputed (Geppert 2010p. 47; Geppert 2010p. 47; Geppert 2010p. 296).
  • Borrowed forms — Kairo and Alt-Berlin were adapted from Paris and elsewhere, and critics found them unoriginal (Geppert 2010p. 49; Geppert 2010p. 51; Geppert 2010p. 56).
  • Wilhelm II and the state — The Kaiser's opposition shaped the fair, and he took part in its colonial events (Geppert 2010p. 34; Geppert 2010p. 47).
  • Fleeting legacy — The fair was dismantled, and Geppert finds no lasting exhibition tradition in Berlin (Geppert 2010p. 61).

Questions for study

  1. Geppert asks whether the fair was a national success but an international failure (Geppert 2010p. 60). What evidence would you need to judge which description is fairer?
  2. The colonial section was presented as a national duty and a means of appearing international (Geppert 2010p. 45; Geppert 2010p. 49). How do the texts show visitors responding to the people on display (Geppert 2010p. 47)?
  3. Simmel and Wilhelm II both saw the exposition as globalising, but they valued the result differently (Geppert 2010p. 58). How would you explain the difference?
  4. The native houses stood beside Alt-Berlin (Geppert 2010p. 46). What story about German history did this placement tell visitors?
  5. The fair left few physical traces (Geppert 2010p. 40; Geppert 2010p. 61). Why might an exhibition of this size leave so little in a city?

Chapters that treat it at length 3 in 1 work

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
1Introduction: How to Read an Expositionp. 1

Geppert opens with a 1896 review by Georg Simmel of the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung, which he uses as a model for reading an exposition as a medium rather than a mirror of national identity. The book studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931, among them the Exposition Universelle of 1900, the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 and the British Empire Exhibition of 1924-25. Geppert argues that these events formed overlapping transnational networks, since organizers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and 'old town' sections from one another, and that the 'identity' framework of earlier work is too simple. He sets out a method of space, time and personae, drawing on Lefebvre and Chartier, and surveys the rise of exposition studies since the 1980s. The introduction admits that its choice of cases is partial, and it closes by promising five virtual visits that compare London, Paris and the would-be world city of Berlin.

In the contents of this work

2Berlin 1896: Wilhelm II, Georg Simmel and the Berliner Gewerbeausstellungp. 16

Geppert asks why Germany never hosted an international exposition like those in London, Paris or Vienna, and what the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung of 1896 was in their place. He traces three failed phases of the 'exhibition question' (1878-82, 1891-92, 1907-10), in which businessmen's associations and government debated and dropped plans, and attributes the final failure largely to Wilhelm II's personal opposition, with cost and site problems behind it. The chapter then turns to the privately financed fair: its layout, the colonial section with its 'native villages', the Egyptian quarter Kairo and Alt-Berlin. Reading Georg Simmel's 1896 review beside the Kaiser's letters and the press, Geppert argues that the fair tested Berlin's claim to be a world city and found it wanting next to Paris. It concludes that the fair left few urban traces but was publicised as a rehearsal for a later world exhibition.

In the contents of this work

7Conclusion: Exhibition Fatigue, or the Rise and Fall of a Mass Mediump. 201

Geppert's concluding chapter asks why world and imperial exhibitions, at their height around 1900, also drew sustained criticism. It traces the German term Ausstellungsmüdigkeit ('exhibition fatigue') from the diaries of the engineer Max Eyth, who worked at fairs in London, Vienna and Paris, through German debates from the 1880s to similar complaints in France and Britain. It then follows the institutional response: a German permanent exhibition commission, a British Board of Trade branch, and the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions. A proposed Anglo-German exhibition at the White City, which German officials blocked, shows how these fears worked in practice. The second half turns to sites. Geppert compares the Eiffel Tower, the Ferris wheels at Earl's Court and Paris, and the Berlin Funkturm, and treats the grounds in London, Paris and Berlin as 'cities within the city' that were absorbed into urban life in different ways. The chapter closes by proposing three elements of a theory of exposition practice: transience, the movement from space to place, and the chronotope of time and space.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Mandell, Richard D. Paris 1900: The Great World’s Fair. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1967.
TwoOrigins and Oppositionp. 25

Mandell examines the political opposition to the Exposition of 1900. Beginning with the Republic's 1889 triumph and its later scandals, economic stagnation and demographic anxiety, the chapter shows how French fears of German power produced a race to hold a fair first: a German plan for Berlin prompted the government to announce Paris 1900 in July 1892. It then describes the control of Commissioner General Alfred Picard, the foreign invitations and the American and German demands for space, with Chicago 1893 as a point of comparison. The main argument concerns the opposition of 1895 and 1896, led by the Nancy editor Goulette through the decentralist pamphlet Pas d'Exposition en 1900! and Barres's moral and provincial objections. Mandell concludes that the Chamber and Senate approved the project in 1896 because invitations had already been sent and national honour was engaged.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Wikipedia

The Great Industrial Exposition of Berlin 1896 (German: Große Berliner Gewerbeausstellung 1896) was a large exposition that has also been dubbed "the impeded world fair (Die verhinderte Weltausstellung)". Under the official name of a Berlin Industrial Exposition (German "Berliner Gewerbeausstellung") - borrowing the name from earlier exhibitions - the Universal Exposition of Berlin took place between 1 May and 15 October 1896 in the Treptow borough of Berlin. The exposition featured one of the largest and longest Great refractors up-to that time, and it was preserved after the exposition and survived to the 21st century.
