The Punjab Art and Industry Exhibition was an art and industry show. Filipová lists it among colonial fairs organised in India, giving Lahore and the years 1881–82 (Filipová 2015p. 14). Hoffenberg lists a Punjab Art and Industry Exhibition for 1881 without a city or any detail (Hoffenberg 2001p. 279). The texts do not name its organisers, exhibits or visitors. Both books mention it only in passing, so this page is short.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Empire and colony

Filipová places the Lahore fair among the fairs in India, Australia and other colonies that were organised "to promote settlement, investment, and development" in those countries (Filipová 2015p. 14). The introduction adds that such fairs often served as "displays of the British dominance and imperial authority" (Filipová 2015p. 14). The texts do not say which colonies or dominions took part, or which colonial products, peoples or displays the Lahore fair included (Filipová 2015p. 14; Hoffenberg 2001p. 279).

Hoffenberg's book on exhibitions in Australia and India argues that the shows linked the Empire's political units (Hoffenberg 2001). It does not describe the Punjab show.

Dates and sources

The texts agree on 1881 only. Filipová gives 1881–82 and Lahore (Filipová 2015p. 14). Hoffenberg's appendix lists the show for 1881 and also a Punjab Art and Industry Exhibition for 1864 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 279; Hoffenberg 2001p. 279). On page 2 Hoffenberg describes art and industry exhibitions that Governor Richard Temple organised in the Punjab during the 1860s, which drew over 20,000 visitors, but he does not name the 1881 show there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2).

Hoffenberg's note 29 cites John Lockwood Kipling's "General Reports of Results" in the 1881 Punjab report (Hoffenberg 2001p. 312). The sentence the note supports concerns art reproductions at expositions in general (Hoffenberg 2001p. 68).

Who was involved

The book describes John Lockwood Kipling, director of the Mayo School of Art in Lahore, as organising "a large collection" for the Calcutta International Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 35; Hoffenberg 2001p. 35). Appendix B lists the exhibitions he served, including Calcutta in 1883–84, and does not name the Punjab show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 283).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — Filipová treats colonial fairs in India, this one included, as displays of British dominance and imperial authority (Filipová 2015p. 14).
  • Settlement and development — the stated aim of these fairs was to "promote settlement, investment, and development" (Filipová 2015p. 14).
  • Art reproduction and expertise — Hoffenberg cites the 1881 Punjab report for a point about expert authority over reproductions at expositions generally (Hoffenberg 2001p. 68; Hoffenberg 2001p. 312).
  • Evidence and dates — the Punjab show appears in Hoffenberg only in an appendix, a note, the bibliography and the index, and the years 1864 and 1881 both appear (Hoffenberg 2001p. 279; Hoffenberg 2001p. 415).

Filipová's introduction names the Punjab fair at Lahore, 1881-82, only in a list of colonial fairs in India, grouped with fairs in Australia, New Zealand, Canada and other colonies. It argues that these fairs were organised to promote settlement, investment and development and often displayed British dominance and imperial authority, and that most work on colonial displays concentrates on the largest colonisers.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.
