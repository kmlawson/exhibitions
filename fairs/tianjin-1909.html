Zhili Provincial Preparatory Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The Zhili Provincial Preparatory Exhibition was held in Tianjin in December 1909 and showed goods made in Zhili province (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). The texts present it as Zhili’s preparatory engagement with the national exposition of 1910, to which the province then sent its own contingent (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). The texts name no organisers beyond Tianjin’s exposition officials (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). It matters as one province’s record of its part in national display (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179p. 181).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Susan R. Fernsebner describes Zhili as the one province that left a detailed record of its part in the exposition project (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). She links it to the aims of the directors Chen Qi and Xiang Ruikun, who hoped to mobilise a “new people” for a globally competitive Chinese economy (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 180). Zhili’s record, she writes, covers both its 1909 preparatory exhibition and the 1910 national event (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181).

Exhibits and displays

A published account of a visitor’s tour listed porcelain, lacquer ware, paper, seals, embroidery, silks and woven and dyed textiles (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). The text quotes the account on metalwares: “all kinds of brass, pewter, wood and iron wares, all made by this very province’s workers” (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). Medicines of all sorts and items of fine art were also shown (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). Displays showed the strengths of new industrial manufactures (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181). One textile exhibit came from a training centre for reformed criminals and showed improvements to both the product and its makers (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181). The Figure 9.6 caption calls the makers a training institute for criminal offenders (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 182). Figures 9.5 and 9.6 illustrate the fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181). Their captions credit a 1910 publication, Zhili xie zan hui, Nanyang quanye hui Zhili chupin lei zuan hebian, vol. 1; one caption spells the middle word chu pin and the other chupin (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181p. 236).

Foreign consulates and the national event

Tianjin’s exposition officials invited representatives of foreign consulates, which the text presents as an opportunity to promote trade (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). Reports recorded the items to which each consulate gave most attention (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). Zhili then sent its own contingent and goods to the 1910 national exposition, as did the other provinces (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179). The text says Zhili’s 1910 displays, including soap, echoed the same agenda (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181). The passages give no attendance figures and no opening or closing dates for the Tianjin fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Provincial self-representation — the text treats Zhili’s own record of its displays as a provincial contribution to national display (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179p. 181)
  • Local manufacture — goods described as made by the province’s workers, and new industrial manufactures on show (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179p. 181)
  • Reform and training — textiles from a training centre for reformed criminals, captioned as a training institute for criminal offenders (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 181p. 182)
  • Foreign consulates and trade — consulates invited and presented as a chance to promote trade (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 179)
  • New people and global competition — the directors’ aim to mobilise new people, which the Zhili displays are said to echo (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 180)

Questions for study

  1. What does a province’s own published record of its displays show about the 1909 fair, and what does it leave out?
  2. Why did Tianjin’s officials invite foreign consulates, and what did the consulates’ reports measure?
  3. How does the display of textiles from a training centre for reformed criminals fit the claim that it showed improvement?
  4. Which sources could supply the attendance, dates and outcome that these passages do not give?

FourThe Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, the Last Show of the Qing Dynastyp. 124Asia

Hur turns to the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, held in Nanjing from June to November 1910, the first national exposition hosted by China and the last of the Qing dynasty. The chapter traces the project to Duanfang, the Manchu governor-general who proposed it in 1908 and modeled its regulations on Japan's domestic exhibitions, and to his successor Zhang Renjun after Duanfang's transfer in 1909. Hur describes the preparations, including a site of about 165 acres, pavilions for twenty-two provinces, foreign samples buildings, and the security measures taken amid fears of unrest in Nanjing. The main argument concerns the Chinese press, which, unlike its coverage of Osaka and St. Louis, was mostly descriptive or positive, a shift Hur ties to the constitutional movement and to newspapers and organizers who backed it. The chapter also covers the large Japanese visiting groups and the overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia. It concludes that the exposition was a momentary display of an integrated, constitutional China that faded with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

Brief discussion 1

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Ch. 9 “When the local is the global” (Susan R. Fernsebner); printed pp. 179–183 (index entry l. 11091–11092); figures 9.5–9.8 at pp. 181–183

Fernsebner’s chapter on Chinese exposition projects includes a short account of Zhili province’s preparatory exhibition at Tianjin in December 1909, which preceded the 1910 national exposition. She describes the displays of local manufactures, textiles, medicines and fine art, and the invitations sent to foreign consulates, drawing on Zhili’s own published record of its displays. Her argument is that such displays were part of an agenda, associated with the directors Chen Qi and Xiang Ruikun, to present a “new people” who would serve a globally competitive Chinese economy. Most of the Zhili material in the passages read concerns the 1910 national event rather than the Tianjin fair, which gets about one paragraph and two figures.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.
