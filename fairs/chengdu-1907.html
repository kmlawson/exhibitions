Local-products exhibition, Chengdu

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources online

Exhibitions of local products were held at Chengdu, alongside Tianjin and Wuhan, from 1907. The only text that names the Chengdu show gives no date, venue, organiser or exhibit list for it. It can be recorded only as one of several small city-level shows of local goods in the years before the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which the same text treats at length (Hurp. 125; Hurp. 125; Hurp. 124).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Local shows before Nanyang

Hur places the Chengdu show within the early proposals for a national exhibition. In 1904 Zhang Zhenxun, an overseas Chinese merchant from Southeast Asia, had an audience with the Empress Dowager to propose a grand-scale exposition in China (Hurp. 125). Hur then notes that "small-scaled events occurred independently throughout the nation" (Hurp. 125). Among these were exhibitions of local products on the level of city and province in Tianjin, Wuhan and Chengdu, which the text dates only to "since 1907" (Hurp. 125; Hurp. 125).

The passage gives no venue, organiser, scale, exhibit list or visitor count for the Chengdu show (Hurp. 125). Hur does not describe these shows as precursors of the Nanyang exposition (Hurp. 125).

Local goods and the Nanyang screening

Hur dates Duanfang's first proposal for the Nanyang Industrial Exposition to 1908 (Hurp. 125). Duanfang was Governor-General of Liang-Jiang at the time (Hurp. 125). The text calls the exposition the first national exposition hosted by China, and the first and last of the Qing dynasty (Hurp. 124).

In March 1909 Duanfang wrote to each provincial governor to ask for exhibitions of local products from their districts, to screen exhibits for Nanyang (Hurp. 129). The Liang-Jiang shows in Jiangsu, Anhui and Jiangxi were followed by shows in nine further provinces, Sichuan among them (Hurp. 129; Hurp. 129). The passage does not name Chengdu or connect the Chengdu shows with this screening (Hurp. 129).

Overseas Chinese merchants, as a preliminary stage of their own participation, had held local-products exhibitions in areas including Surabaya, Semarang, Java, Batavia and Singapore (Hurp. 159).

Place in the series

The chapter approaches the Nanyang exposition as an extension of China's experience at earlier world exhibitions, notably Osaka and St Louis (Hurp. 125; Hurp. 125). The Chengdu shows are not discussed in those terms (Hurp. 125).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Local-products exhibitions — the text treats the Chengdu show as one of several small city and provincial events, grouped with Tianjin and Wuhan (Hurp. 125).
  • Provincial screening for Nanyang — the text describes provincial shows of local goods used to screen exhibits for Nanyang, with Sichuan named among them but not Chengdu (Hurp. 129; Hurp. 129).

Questions for study

  1. What goods did the Chengdu exhibitions show, and who organised them? The one text that names them does not say.
  2. Did the 1907 shows in Tianjin, Wuhan and Chengdu feed into the Nanyang exposition, or run alongside it? What evidence would settle this?
  3. Why did Duanfang need provincial exhibitions to screen exhibits for a national exposition, and what does this suggest about relations between the centre and the provinces?
  4. Hur treats Nanyang as an extension of world-exhibition experience. Do local-products shows such as Chengdu's fit that frame, or do they belong to a separate tradition?

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Kwon, Hyeokhui. “An Analysis of Korean Intellectual Responses to the Exhibition of Koreans at Japanese Expositions: Nationalism and the Discourse on Northeast Asian Solidarity at the Turn of the Century.” Journal article.
The Anthropological Pavilion and Korean Responsesp. 27Asia

Kwon's longest section follows the protests that brought about the withdrawal of the 1903 Korean display. Japanese officials, worried about relations with Korea and China, ordered the display removed after a protest by three Koreans living in Japan, who appealed to shared race and culture. Kwon notes that these protesters were pro-Japanese bureaucrats, and sets their stance beside the Chinese students' fierce objections to a Chinese display cancelled before it opened. Turning to 1907, Kwon argues that Korean newspapers and the T'aegŭk hakpo responded with far more emotion, condemning the display as racist and as national shame. The Japanese Yomiuri, by contrast, framed the affair as women's liberation and downplayed discrimination. The section ends by linking these reactions to the nationalism of the Patriotic Enlightenment movement after the 1905 Protectorate Treaty.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
