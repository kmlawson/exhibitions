Charleston · 1901–1902

South Carolina Interstate and West Indian Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The South Carolina Inter-State and West Indian Exposition was held at Charleston, South Carolina, from 1 December 1901 to 31 May or 1 June 1902 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Its promoters meant it to increase trade with the West Indies and the Caribbean after the Spanish-American War (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). Colonel John H. Averill proposed it in 1899 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). It drew about 675,000 visitors, much lower than expected, and one text reports a large deficit and bankruptcy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). Two reference entries treat it at length; the other texts mention it briefly (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 166; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The idea came from Colonel John H. Averill in 1899. The News & Courier and its editor, J.C. Hemphill, supported it, and local businessmen and civic leaders found the funds and site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

The encyclopedia gives the purpose as promoting the port of Charleston and trade with the West Indies and the Caribbean after the Spanish American War, and showing the South's cotton (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The dictionary adds that promoters wanted to boost sales of Sea Island cotton, rice, silk and tea (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Fair America gives the principal object as promoting the city as a port for trade to and from the West Indies (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Organisers and site

F. W. Wagener was president of the fair's board (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). The two entries disagree on the site. The encyclopedia gives a 250-acre site near the Ashley River, including an old racecourse and a plantation owned by Wagener (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The dictionary gives about 160 acres, which included the old Lowndes plantation, also Wagener's, whose mansion became the Women's Building (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Bradford L. Gilbert designed the fair, having designed the Atlanta exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The appendix of officials in each book gives the president's initial as G. rather than W. (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 387).

Buildings and exhibits

Fourteen exposition buildings stood on the site, in Colonial South, Spanish Renaissance and Mexican missionary styles, with a lake, a sunken garden and a plaza (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). The largest was the Cotton Palace. The encyclopedia gives it as 350 feet long with a 150-foot dome, and the dictionary as 360 feet long with a 130-foot dome (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). The encyclopedia says it celebrated the region's main crop and the machinery of the New South (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

The dictionary describes a cotton exhibit coordinated by D. A. Tompkins of Charlotte, North Carolina, with products from sixty mills (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). A silk exhibit in the Women's Building showed silkworms, cocoons and silk garments from colonial days, and local hostesses served tea grown in South Carolina (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). An art exhibit of works by colonial and early national artists was lent from local estates (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Five statuary groups in the plaza represented Huguenots, Aztecs, Indians, Negroes, and a mother and child (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174).

The Midway was the most popular section for visitors. The encyclopedia lists its ethnic attractions as Streets of Cairo, Fair Japan, a Cuban-American Theatre and a Wild West show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

Who took part and the Negro Building

The encyclopedia says the Negro Building was segregated from the rest of the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). Charleston's black leaders used it to show black industrial and agricultural advancement, in keeping with Booker T. Washington's philosophy of racial progress (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). Its exhibits were praised in the press (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). Greenhalgh names the Charleston fair among southern fairs that all included Negro sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99).

Visitors, money and reception

The encyclopedia gives approximately 675,000 visitors, much lower than expected, and says that more than 350,000 people received free passes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). It blames a cold, wet winter and railroads that did not cut fares substantially (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). It also says that Charleston's established wealth and the federal government gave no financial support. A large deficit followed, and the company sold buildings and exhibits at auction (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The fair brought some new business and reconnected city and state with the nation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

The dictionary states that the fair attracted visitors and earned a small profit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Its author concludes that the fair had little effect on the southern economy, but was "a pleasant and memorable experience for its visitors" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 175; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 175).

The Liberty Bell arrived by train in early January 1902 and was housed in the Philadelphia Building (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). President Theodore Roosevelt visited in April 1902, and more than 24,000 people attended on that day (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The dictionary calls this President's Day and reports a well-received visit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Roosevelt praised Civil War veterans and Charleston's efforts to increase trade with the West Indies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

Empire and colony

The texts tie this fair to empire through trade, not through colonial exhibits. The encyclopedia says the fair aimed at trade with the West Indies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The dictionary names Cuba and Puerto Rico, recently acquired in the Spanish-American War, as the trade the promoters had in mind (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Fair America says that the acquisition of Puerto Rico and a protectorate over Cuba signalled a new American interest in the economic potential of the Caribbean (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Neither entry names a colony or dependency as an exhibitor, or describes colonised people on display at this fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). The Midway's ethnic attractions are named but not described, and neither source says who performed in them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167).

Its place in the series

The encyclopedia calls it the third major fair in the South in six years, after Atlanta in 1895 and Nashville in 1897, and says it came a year after the Buffalo Pan-American Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 166; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 166). The dictionary describes it as following hard on the heels of the Buffalo fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174). Fair America pairs it with the Jamestown exposition of 1907 as the two southern fairs held between Nashville and World War I (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

How historians have interpreted it

In the encyclopedia, James M. Beeby argues that the planners tried to rise above southern regionalism and promote patriotism and national unity, and that Roosevelt's visit fitted the aim of easing sectional tensions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). In the dictionary, John E. Findling judges that the fair had little effect on the southern economy (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 175). Rydell, in All the World's a Fair, lists the fair among southern expositions that each presented an image of a New South imbued with the spirit of progress and patriotism (Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle, in Fair America, read it as one sign of renewed American interest in the Caribbean after 1898 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The encyclopedia names Fair Japan among the Midway's attractions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167). The two entries name no East, South or Southeast Asian commissioner, exhibit, performer or visitor at this fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Trade with the Caribbean — the stated aim of the fair, which the texts tie to the Spanish-American War and to the acquisition of Cuba and Puerto Rico (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)
  • Race and the Negro Building — a segregated building in which black exhibitors showed progress, set beside statuary of Negroes and Indians in the plaza (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Greenhalgh 1988p. 99)
  • Sectional reunion — the Liberty Bell and Roosevelt's visit presented as signs of patriotism and reconciliation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167)
  • Money and attendance — the encyclopedia's deficit and bankruptcy set against the dictionary's small profit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174)
  • New South products — cotton, silk and tea as the chosen exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167)

Questions for study

  1. The two entries give different accounts of the fair's finances. What evidence would decide between a small profit and a deficit that led to bankruptcy? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174)
  2. The Negro Building was segregated, yet black leaders used it to show progress. What did separate exhibits allow the organisers to claim, and what did they hide? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167)
  3. Trade with the West Indies was the stated aim, but neither entry names a West Indian exhibitor. Which sources would show whether the imperial purpose reached the exhibits? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167)
  4. The entries disagree on the site and the Cotton Palace's dimensions. Which figures would you trust, and how would you check them? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 167; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 174)

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Brief discussion 1

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
Ch. 1, 'Fairs in the Age of Industrialism's Advance' (PDF page 39; index p. 28)

Fair America's first chapter gives the Charleston exposition one paragraph, within its account of the southern fairs after Nashville. The authors say its principal object was to promote Charleston as a port for trade with the West Indies, and they link it to the Spanish-American War acquisitions of Puerto Rico and a protectorate over Cuba, which signalled a new interest in the economic potential of the Caribbean. They describe it, with the Jamestown exposition of 1907, as one of the two southern fairs held before World War I.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. These were displays of Filipinos organised by American officials, promoters and anthropologists, not Filipino participation. China appears in a Chinese Village at Omaha in 1898, which the authors call little more than a souvenir shop, and in the Chinese village at San Francisco in 1915, which framed China as ripe for American investment. Japan appears through the Fair Japan concession at St. Louis, with its Ainu display, and through Japanese exhibits sent to Portland, which were hampered by reported discrimination against Asians at St. Louis. Elsewhere coverage is slight. The interwar chapter notes the Asian ornamental elements and Pacific Basin focus of San Francisco's 1939-40 fair, with elephant towers modelled on Cambodia's Angkor Wat. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction and conclusion name Osaka 1970 and Aichi 2005 as later Japanese fairs. No passage mentions Thailand, Indochina or the Dutch East Indies.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Badger uses the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 as a test case for what the great world's fairs say about the societies that made them. He argues that the fairs expressed a Victorian faith in progress, order and culture, and that the White City's unity answered the fears of an unsettled age rather than describing American society. The book opens with a background of the London, Paris, Vienna and Philadelphia fairs and the American role in them. It then treats the Chicago fair in detail: its origins, organisation, site and design, finance and promotion, congresses, exhibits and Midway, and its legacy. Four parts and an epilogue carry the story to the fire and violence of 1894.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are slight in this book, and most of the material is in lists and quotations. Japan appears as an exhibitor and as a government. Chapter 3 (America and the Great World's Fairs) notes that Japanese displays at Philadelphia in 1876 showed how far America still had to go in the decorative arts. Chapter 9 (Finance and Promotion) says that of the fewer than twenty governments that had accepted by June 1891, only Germany, Turkey and Japan seemed enthusiastic. Chapter 14 (The Midway) lists Persian, Japanese and Indian bazaars, a Chinese village and a Dutch settlement with what the text calls South Sea island villages, without naming the islands. Chapter 13 (The Exhibits) discusses the ethnic displays on the Midway, where Indians, Eskimos, Egyptians and Africans are named, and quotes an Indian visitor. Chapter 12 (The World's Congress) quotes Barrows wanting Christianity to advance in China, and Chapter 15 (Achievement and Influence) quotes an 1890s passage that names the 'innocent Siamese' among peoples threatened by armaments. East Asian and Southeast Asian peoples are never treated as observers, as people on display in Asia, or as the subject of an exhibition held in Asia.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
Ch. 4, 'Human showcases' (PDF page 126), in a passage on Negro sections at fairs; index p. 99

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Introduction (Martin Lawn), chronology of international exhibitions (PDF page 15)

Lawn's introduction to this collection on education and world exhibitions includes a chronology of international exhibitions. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The chapters are grouped in five parts: the opening Brussels and Soviet-American contest, North American fairs, cultural conflict at the fairs, Asia and Australia, and endings and new beginnings. The sources include archives, press, films and visitor surveys.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in two chapters devoted wholly to them. Chapter 3, Morris Low's 'Atoms for Peace in Brussels and Osaka', treats Japan's pavilions at Brussels 1958, where a Hiroshima image confronted visitors, and at Osaka 1970 as ways of selling nuclear power to Japanese audiences. It draws on US Information Service surveys of Japanese opinion and on exit surveys of Japanese visitors to the American and Soviet pavilions. Chapter 13, Ellan F. Spero's 'A Garden City for Progress and Harmony', studies Singapore's Osaka pavilion, its film and stamps, and the government's planning papers, and argues that the pavilion left out colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and the brief union with Malaysia. Other chapters touch the region more briefly. Chapter 21 by Luca Massidda calls Osaka 1970 the first Asian world exposition and includes it in his table of fairs. Chapter 22 by Scott Gabriel Knowles presents Daejeon 1993 as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, with a pavilion for North Korean products, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China with its Chinese visitors. Chapter 14 notes the strong Asian and Pacific representation at Brisbane 1988, and Chapter 16 mentions scrolls sent to women in fourteen Asian and Pacific countries and the queen of Thailand as a member of the Woman's Pavilion. The Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina and Burma do not appear as places of exhibitions in the passages examined, and the volume's centre of gravity remains the United States, Europe and the Soviet Union.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.
