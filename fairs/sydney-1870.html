Metropolitan Intercolonial Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Metropolitan Intercolonial Exhibition was held at Sydney, New South Wales, in 1870; its catalogue is dated 16 August 1870 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 350). The night work at Alfred Park for it, on the grounds of the local Royal Agricultural Society, is described by Hoffenberg alone (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). The other texts mention it in a list or a single sentence (Cotép. 36; Filipová 2015p. 235). The texts do not say who organised it; they name the local Agricultural Society, whose grounds held it and which received state grants to collect exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90). The texts matter for their accounts of state support, hidden building labour and when international exhibitions began in Australia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90; Hoffenberg 2001p. 182; Filipová 2015p. 235).

The Filipová 2015 mention is merged here on identity; its international claim conflicts. Start date 1870-08-16 from Hoffenberg 2001 only; no end date. In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Empire and colony

Hoffenberg says the New South Wales government supported the show with grants to the local Agricultural Society for collecting exhibits, which were to go on to the 1871 London International Exhibition, and with free use of public telegraph lines and railways (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90). The main halls were hung with flags and medieval banners, which he places within the decorative display of imperial halls in London, Calcutta, Dublin and Melbourne (Hoffenberg 2001p. 245p. 244). The texts name no participating colonies and describe no colonial products or colonised people on display at this show (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90p. 245).

Building, labour and decoration

Hoffenberg uses the building work to argue about labour. Night work at Alfred Park, in preparation for the 1870 show, produced the image of a "satanic mill" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). Torches, not gas or electric lights, lit the erection of an early iron building, and a reporter described dark figures working in perilous places (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). Hoffenberg argues that nostalgia tended to erase accidents at such sites, and that the labour was not necessarily safer (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). The reporter's account is cited to the Illustrated Sydney News of 3 August 1870 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 348).

Dates and names

The texts vary in name and date. Hoffenberg calls it the Sydney Metropolitan Exhibition in the text and the Sydney Metropolitan and Intercolonial in his appendix (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90p. 279). The two reference works list a Sydney Intercolonial Exhibition of 1870 among excluded fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 397). Rebecca Rice says the first international exhibition in Australasia "did not take place until 1870 in Sydney, Australia" (Filipová 2015p. 235). Anne Neale, in another chapter of the same volume, dates the colonies' own international exhibitions from Sydney in 1878-79 (Filipová 2015p. 251). Hoffenberg says Sydney began the series in 1879 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Coté lists Sydney 1870 in a footnote, with no detail (Cotép. 36).

ASIA

Hoffenberg says that in Sydney Jules Joubert encouraged South Asian displays at the series of Metropolitan Exhibitions run by the local Royal Agricultural Society, but he does not date this to 1870 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 119). No text describes South Asian exhibitors, commissioners or visitors at the 1870 show.

Themes in the scholarship

  • State support — The New South Wales government gave grants and transport help, tying the show to the London exhibition of 1871 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 90).
  • Labour and accidents — Night work and torchlight at the site produced a "satanic mill" image, and Hoffenberg argues that accidents were forgotten (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182).
  • Decoration and imperial display — Flags and medieval banners hung in the main halls, which Hoffenberg reads as an attempt to anchor the empire in older myths (Hoffenberg 2001p. 245).
  • Names and status — The texts disagree on whether Sydney's 1870 show was the first international exhibition in the region (Filipová 2015p. 235; Filipová 2015p. 251; Hoffenberg 2001p. 11).

Questions for study

  1. What did the 1871 London grants show about how colonial exhibitions were linked to the imperial exhibition system?
  2. Why do the texts differ on whether 1870 or 1879 began international exhibitions in Sydney, and how does each text define "international"?
  3. Hoffenberg uses the night work to argue that labour was hidden. What evidence beyond one newspaper account would show who built this hall and under what conditions?
  4. If the halls' decorations were imperial, what would they have signalled to colonial visitors in Sydney?

Hoffenberg mentions the 1870 Sydney show in three chapters, in his appendix and in his notes, but does not make it a case study. He uses its night-time construction at Alfred Park to argue that the labour behind exhibition displays was hidden and that accidents were forgotten, and he notes state grants and decoration of its main halls with flags and medieval banners as part of his wider case about imperial display and state support.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

A footnote in this article on the 1914 Semarang exhibition lists colonial exhibitions in India and in Australia, including a Sydney show dated 1870, giving only years and places.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

On the web

Wikipedia

The first Sydney Intercolonial Exhibition was a series of exhibitions inspired by the historic Great Exhibition held in London in 1851. The Colony of New South Wales mounted its first such exhibition in 1854 in preparation for the Paris Exhibition of 1855, another in 1861 in preparation for the London Exhibition of 1862, and then several more until being held annually throughout the 1870s under the name Metropolitan Intercolonial Exhibition. The term "intercolonial" referred to the British colonies on the continent of Australia, which did not federate until 1901. The major impetus for intercolonial exhibitions derived from the Agricultural Society of New South Wales, which from 1858 onwards sponsored annual shows at the Society's grounds at Parramatta, about 20 km (12 mi) west of the Sydney central business district.
