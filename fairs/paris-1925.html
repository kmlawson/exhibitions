The International Exhibition of Decorative and Modern Industrial Arts (Exposition Internationale des Arts Decoratifs et Industriels Modernes) opened in Paris on 30 April 1925 and closed on 15 October (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 237). It covered about seventy acres on either side of the Seine and drew an estimated 14 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 236; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 240). The fair gave its name to Art Deco (Allwood et al. 2001p. 100; Greenhalgh 1988p. 165). Historians study it for how a new decorative style was made, how modernist architecture was shown beside luxury crafts, and how empires were displayed to the public (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166; Rydell 1993p. 67).

Origins and aims

The planning began before the First World War, and the texts differ on when. Roger Marx proposed the project in 1909 and predicted that it would end the scorn for the machine (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 241). Matthias gives 1914 as the first planned date, postponed by the war, with funds voted only in 1921 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Whalen gives 1915 for the Society of Artist-Decorators' first plan, and says the show could be scheduled only after the Marseilles colonial exposition of 1922 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 237).

The organisers wanted to restore France as the arbiter of taste in the decorative arts (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Reproductions and copies were excluded, and exhibits had to be original, practical and expressive of modern inspiration (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Greenhalgh says the brief banned copies of historical styles and judged manufacturers on whether their work showed clearly modern tendencies, though it discouraged historical styles without stopping them (Greenhalgh 1988p. 164; Greenhalgh 1988p. 160). Greenhalgh also argues that the fair was where "an exhibition gave birth to and then publicised a style" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 165).

Roche gives a different reason for the long gap between fairs. He says the French and Parisian elites were exhausted and jaded after their monumental 1900 exposition, and staged no other exposition for a generation, until the inter-war series of Marseilles in 1922 and Paris in 1925, 1931 and 1937 (Roche 2000p. 42).

Organisation, finance and site

Whalen lists three sources of money: 50-franc bonds, each worth twenty admission tickets, large state subsidies, and a grant of 3 million francs from the city of Paris (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 237). Matthias says Lucien Dior, minister of commerce, persuaded the city to give the 3 million francs and the land (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 240). The two entries also disagree on the area. Whalen gives about seventy acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 236), and Matthias gives 72 acres (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 240).

The fair ran on two axes, from the Champs-Elysees to the Place de la Concorde and from the Grand Palais to the Hotel des Invalides (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 236). The site was in the centre of Paris and reused the existing Grand Palais and Petit Palais (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 240). ## Architecture and the department stores Art Deco drew its motifs away from floral and neoclassical forms towards geometric ones, borrowing from Cubism, French colonial art and Egyptian culture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). The department stores built the most flamboyant pavilions. Galeries Lafayette, Bon Marche, Printemps and Magasins du Louvre stood on the Place des Invalides, and Whalen says they showed the exposition's commercial rather than democratic aims (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 239). Matthias argues that the stores and their interior designers set the vocabulary of Art Deco and shaped the taste of the new rich, while a cheaper French version reached the average buyer (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Greenhalgh calls the store and fashion pavilions Art Deco at its most flamboyant (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168). Ruhlmann's Grand Salon in the Pavillon d'un Collectionneur, Matthias says, epitomised the style (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 242). ## Modernist pavilions Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau was built of concrete, steel and glass and stood in stark contrast to the other pavilions (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 241). Palermo says it held a full-size housing cell and a diorama of Le Corbusier's Contemporary City of Three Million Inhabitants (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 242). The Plan Voisin for central Paris was shown on its terrace. Visitors and critics were outraged, because the plan proposed demolishing most of central Paris (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 244).

The texts disagree about how the pavilion was treated. Whalen says it was pushed into the shadow of the Grand Palais and surrounded by a thirty-foot fence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 239). Matthias gives the fence as twenty feet (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the full-size apartment was given one of the worst sites, and that the crowds grew angry until the authorities fenced it off (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). The Soviet pavilion by Konstantin Melnikov denied the role of the wall, with the hammer and sickle as its only narrative element (Greenhalgh 1988p. 167). Greenhalgh treats the two pavilions as isolated and largely ignored at the time (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166).

Colonies and nations on show

Colonial display was central to the fair. Rydell says French colonial authorities commissioned pavilions for their African and Southeast Asian possessions and a building of French colonial art, where crafts and colonial natural resources were shown (Rydell 1993p. 67). He argues that a fair calling itself a break with precedent presented a special challenge to imperial propagandists, who turned it to their own purposes (Rydell 1993p. 67; Rydell 1993p. 67). Greenhalgh says the fair kept the colonial element despite its stress on modern life, and that the definition of colonial art was a main issue (Greenhalgh 1988p. 68). Whalen lists the colonial pavilions as North Africa, French Equatorial Africa, Indo-China, Morocco, Tunisia and the colonial administration (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241).

Other nations were fewer. Matthias says four sites beside the central road were given to Italy, Great Britain, Belgium and Japan, and that the Japanese site was first intended for the United States (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241). The Italian pavilion combined ancient Roman and modern architecture to glorify fascism (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241). The United States did not respond to the invitation, saying it had no modern art to show, and its site went to Japan (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241). Rydell records that Herbert Hoover, as commerce secretary, refused to support an official American exhibit (Rydell 1993p. 73). Germany was not invited (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241).

Visitors and reception

The two reference works give the same visitor figure of about 14 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 237; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 240). Whalen says few exhibits addressed the needs of ordinary citizens. The French Village showed a church, an inn, a school and houses, and a few austere interiors for ordinary people stood in the Colonial Pavilion and in René Gabriel's workers' kitchen (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 240; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241). The entry says the fair "made no heavy intellectual or moral demands on the visitor" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 237). Whalen's 1937 entry says the 1925 attendance was a tenth of that of the 1900 Exposition universelle, and that the fair was not as ambitious as the five earlier Paris world's fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 290). It still counts as a success in critics' eyes rather than with the public, which let France claim cultural leadership (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 290).

Aftermath and legacy

Matthias says the influence of the fair and of Art Deco was enormous, felt in the United States for twenty years after 1925 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 242; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 242). Rydell says businessmen, politicians and intellectuals, after the economic crash, drew on the Paris fair to recast the world's fair in modernistic forms (Rydell 1993p. 6). Ganz says the architecture committee of the 1933 Chicago fair was fired by the crisp Art Deco lines of 1925 (Ganz 2008). Greenhalgh says Mallet-Stevens's Tourist Pavilion became a prototype for later exhibition architecture, at Chicago in 1933 and Glasgow in 1938 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168).

The fair's afterlife also ran through politics. A wooden pavilion described as a former Soviet exposition pavilion, originally used for the 1925 fair and then left vacant, was moved to Paris and housed an anti-colonial counter-exposition during the 1931 colonial exposition (Geppert 2010p. 199; Geppert 2010p. 199). Le Corbusier was excluded from all the design teams of the 1937 Paris exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 292). The 1937 fair was planned to repeat the success of 1925 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 8).

Modernist pavilions

Palermo describes Le Corbusier's pavilion as a 400-square-metre building, and says its right half held "in real full-size a whole unit" of housing (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 242). The unit's structure followed Le Corbusier's Dom-Ino method of 1914 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 243; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 243). Palermo ties the pavilion to the housing crisis after the war, when 11,000 public buildings and 350,000 houses were damaged or destroyed (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 242), and calls the building a "protest against the crepuscular program of the Exposition." (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 242). Its fittings were ordinary: the windows and doors were commercially available, laboratory glassware served as vases, and some chairs came from a hospital supplier (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 243).

The walls held paintings, and the two accounts name different artists. Greenhalgh says Fernand Léger's paintings decorated the walls (Greenhalgh 1988p. 167). Palermo calls the art the only handcrafted items in the house, and names paintings and sculptures by Picasso, Lipchitz, Ozenfant and Le Corbusier (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 243). Greenhalgh describes the Soviet pavilion's main facade as a huge bank of windows, with a staircase of criss-cross iron beams leading up to an entrance tucked away at the side (Greenhalgh 1988p. 167).

On the terrace, the Plan Voisin proposed razing most of central Paris for twenty-four identical towers, sixty storeys high, set on grass among planted trees (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 244). Palermo reads it as a provocative argument rather than a real plan for execution, and notes that the streets it would clear held many cramped flats below modern standards (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 244).

Allwood, Allan and Reid say the international jury wanted to give Le Corbusier first prize, but the French Academicians vetoed it, on the grounds that "it was not architecture" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). They also say the 20-foot fence was removed just before the fair opened, on the direct order of the minister of fine arts (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). This runs against the fence heights given above, which imply that it stood throughout.

Designers and craft firms

Greenhalgh names the designers who first set the French style: Ruhlmann, Pierre Legrain, Edgar Brandt, Jean Dunand, the Jallot brothers, Clement Rousseau, Sue et Mare, Eileen Gray and Rene Lalique. He says they had little to do with industry as such (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166). Rydell adds that artists and interior designers from the department stores put a modernistic style on public view in fashion, furniture, sculpture, glass, metal, ceramics and bookbinding (Rydell 1993p. 67).

Greenhalgh gives the architect of the Ruhlmann group's building, the "Pavilion of a Rich Collector", as Pierre Patout. It had a stepped, ziggurat-like silhouette and a wall-based volume (Greenhalgh 1988p. 167). Patout also designed the Porte de la Concorde, one of six monumental entrances, which had eight giant free-standing square pillars around a sculpture of a nude woman (Greenhalgh 1988p. 167). Greenhalgh reads Robert Mallet-Stevens's Tourist Pavilion differently. Its interlocking vertical and horizontal planes, he says, show knowledge of De Stijl and possibly of Gropius's Chicago Tribune Tower, but its austerity is softened (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168).

The Grand Palais and exhibition design

Allwood, Allan and Reid say the Grand Palais, one of the exhibition buildings, had its interior completely remodelled. Its false ceilings, columns and open spaces, with an Escalier Monumental, made it look like an enormous palace of the ancient world (Allwood et al. 2001p. 102). They describe the style's features as speed shapes, integrated lettering, rising-sun motifs, bright colours, early plastics and metal from the aircraft industry. On their account it stayed prominent in exhibition design until the Second World War (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh gives the most critical reading. Art Deco, he argues, was a symbolic answer to a problem that needed methodological answers, and "more than anything else, it was about disguise" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166). He calls it, in the French firms that first set the style, a Louis XIV method hidden under a machine-age veneer (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166).

Matthias and Palermo agree that the fair valued craft and luxury more than industry. Matthias argues that the celebration of fine traditional craftsmanship kept modernism marginal, and that Art Deco spread from an exclusive style into a broad trend (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239). Palermo argues that the style clearly targeted the haute bourgeoisie rather than widening the market (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 241). Palermo reads Le Corbusier's pavilion as a provocative argument whose universal claims hid blind spots (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 246).

Whalen treats the fair as a celebratory showcase, and reads the department-store pavilions as expressing its commercial rather than democratic orientation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 239). Rydell argues against overvaluing the 1925 fair as the source of modernistic taste, since colonial expositions, especially the 1931 Paris fair, played an equally decisive role (Rydell 1993p. 61). Allwood, Allan and Reid see the displays as showing a resemblance that was "another step down the path to the international uniformity of today" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 102).

Palermo sets Le Corbusier's argument against Louis XIV, whose objects, Le Corbusier said, marked social distinction. Against this he sets Lenin at the Cafe Rotonde, who paid twenty centimes for his coffee (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 244; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 245). Palermo says Le Corbusier counted the Decorative Arts Exposition, and the decorative arts industry, among the offenders (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 245). He also notes that the fair's pavilions were full of furniture and accessories made of exotic wood imported from the French colonies (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 241).

Greenhalgh shows the fair's own literature turning against art nouveau. It said that copying nature was as heavy an error as copying the forms of the past, and that "la mode n’est pas le modernisme" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 165). Claire O'Mahony, writing on Nancy in 1909, observes that scholarship on international exhibitions has attended more to pavilion architecture than to the contents of the displays, and names the 1925 and 1937 decorative arts fairs among the cases studied (Filipová 2015p. 275).

Japan

Japan had a pavilion among the foreign national pavilions. Matthias gives its site as one of four flanking the central road, first intended for the United States (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241). Whalen says the United States declined to take part and its site was given to Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241). The two entries disagree on where the foreign pavilions stood. Matthias places them on the Right Bank, while Whalen places them in the Court of Queens on the Left Bank (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241). The texts do not say who designed or paid for the Japanese pavilion, what it contained, or how it was received.

Indochina and the French colonial pavilions

French colonial authorities commissioned the pavilions for their African and Southeast Asian possessions, along with a building of French colonial art (Rydell 1993p. 67). Rydell says the structures displayed crafts of yarn, wood, metal, lacquer, and shell and ivory work, hinting at the value of colonial resources for modern decorative arts (Rydell 1993p. 67). Whalen names Indo-China among the colonial pavilions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241). Matthias says Indochinese tapestries, along with Madagascar laces, drew much excitement on the Cours des Metiers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241). The texts do not name any Indochinese visitors, commissioners or observers, nor say how the Indochinese displays were received.

Rydell reads the colonial pavilions as imperial propaganda, and argues that colonial expositions, above all the 1931 Paris fair, shaped the colonial taste more decisively than this one (Rydell 1993p. 61). Greenhalgh's list of sources for Art Deco names Aztec, ancient Egyptian, African and Oceanic forms, but no Asian ones (Greenhalgh 1988p. 165).

Themes in the scholarship

  • The origin and name of Art Deco — The texts agree that the fair gave the style its name and spread it widely, but they differ on whether it was a modern break or a disguise for luxury (Greenhalgh 1988p. 165; Allwood et al. 2001p. 100; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239).
  • Modernist pavilions — Le Corbusier's and Melnikov's pavilions were the only purely modernist ones, and Le Corbusier's was fenced in or hidden, depending on the text (Greenhalgh 1988p. 166; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 239).
  • Department stores and mass taste — The stores set the style's vocabulary and sold a cheaper version to a wider public, though Whalen reads their pavilions as commercial rather than democratic (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 239; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 239).
  • Colonial display — The colonial pavilions were a major part of the fair, and the texts treat them as imperial propaganda and as a contest over what counted as colonial art (Rydell 1993p. 67; Greenhalgh 1988p. 68; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241).
  • Nations and absences — The United States stayed away and its site went to Japan, Italy's pavilion glorified fascism, and Germany was not invited (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 241; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241).
  • Visitors and ordinary people — The texts say the fair had little for ordinary citizens, and that its amusements were mostly for the paying public (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 240; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 241).
  • Legacy beyond Paris — The fair's style shaped American and Chicago design, and one of its pavilions was reused for a later political counter-exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 242; Ganz 2008; Geppert 2010p. 199).

Questions for study

  1. If Art Deco was a disguise for luxury goods, as Greenhalgh argues, what did modern design gain from a fair that set it apart from its own rivals?
  2. Why did Le Corbusier's pavilion provoke anger when the decorative rooms pleased visitors? What did the crowds expect from a fair?
  3. Were the colonial pavilions a form of propaganda, a form of taste-making, or both? How would Rydell's reading change if the 1931 colonial fair were treated as the main event?
  4. What do the absences of the United States and Germany tell historians about national display at a fair that claimed to be international?
  5. How much did the department stores shape the style, given that their pavilions were built to sell goods?
  6. How far should a fair's own name, such as Art Deco, guide historians' judgments of it?
  7. Allwood, Allan and Reid say the fence came down before the fair opened. Did the jury's veto or the minister's order do more to shape how the public saw Le Corbusier's pavilion?
  8. Greenhalgh calls the store and fashion pavilions the most flamboyant part of the fair, while Palermo stresses the fair's craft and luxury materials. Did craft firms or department stores set the look of Art Deco, and how would the answer change the reading of the fair?
  9. Roche places the 1925 fair after a long pause in French exhibitions. Did the fair's break with the past come from a new generation of designers, or from the wish to revive French trade?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
9Between the Warsp. 96

Allwood surveys the exhibitions of the 1920s and 1930s. It begins with the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where the stadium, the Neverstop Railway and the amusement park are described, and where advertising designers, more than architects, gave the event its character. The chapter moves to Paris 1925, whose Art Deco name it traces, and to Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau, which was fenced off and nearly denied its prize. Barcelona 1929 and Mies van der Rohe's German pavilion, with its Barcelona chair, follow, along with the 1928 convention that tried to limit how often exhibitions were held. The argument is that these events were increasingly designed for commerce and display rather than education, and that their losses mounted. Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, with its science pavilions and the Sky Ride, is treated at length. The chapter ends with Paris 1937, where the German and Soviet pavilions faced one another across the Champ de Mars, and where the approach of war ended the exhibition's run.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
6The prefabricated and the mass-producedp. 142

Greenhalgh traces how the international exhibitions shaped design and architecture from 1851 to 1939, and argues that the historicist-to-modernist shift was neither linear nor complete. The Great Exhibition revealed the tension between machine production and ornament, and critics often blamed industrial methods for what was really a question of symbolic taste. The chapter then follows the buildings: Paxton's prefabricated Crystal Palace, the Model House for the Working Classes, the housing section at Paris in 1867, and the engineering halls of Paris in 1889, set against the stone-clad Grand Palais of 1900. It shows art nouveau at Paris in 1900, Turin in 1902 and London in 1908, and the arrival of full modernism at Paris in 1925, Paris in 1937 and New York in 1939. Its conclusion is that international modernism failed through its methods, losing sight of ordinary urban life, and that the earlier exhibitions had more to teach than the later ones.

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
4.2The 1925 Paris Exposition des Arts Decoratifs et Industriels Modernes and Le Pavillon de l’Esprit Nouveau: Le Corbusier’s Modernist ManifestoLynn E. Palermop. 241

Palermo examines the 1925 Paris decorative arts exhibition and, in particular, Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau built within it. The French organizers hoped to reclaim leadership in the decorative arts with a new style, but the exhibition's art deco favored luxury craft and exotic materials for the wealthy, so Le Corbusier's concrete, steel and glass pavilion stood out as a critique. Inside was a full-size housing cell based on his Dom-Ino principle, and on the terrace was the Plan Voisin, a proposal to raze central Paris for towers. Palermo links these to the postwar housing crisis, to Le Corbusier's idea of the 'homme tout nu', the modern everyman, and to his contrast between Louis XIV's objects of distinction and Lenin sitting in a cafe. The chapter concludes that Le Corbusier's claims to universal human needs were themselves idiosyncratic, so that he had a blind spot about his own assumptions.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
ThreeColoniale Modernep. 61Asia

Rydell argues that colonial expositions between the wars made empire look like part of modernity, a fusion he calls coloniale moderne, and that American fairs adopted it. He begins with the Marseilles colonial fair of 1922 and the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where living colonial subjects and a pageant of empire were shown, and then the 1925 Paris decorative arts fair, where French colonial pavilions were given modernistic designs. The central case is the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, with its Angkor Wat replica and the U.S. government's prefabricated Mount Vernon, stocked with artifacts from the overseas territories. Rydell shows how protests in Puerto Rico and the Philippines were erased from the American display. The chapter then turns to Chicago's 1933 fair, which borrowed the colonial villages idea, and to San Francisco's 1939 Golden Gate exposition, where Pacific Basin pavilions and a Latin American court presented the West as a market for American goods. Rydell concludes that these fairs tied American expansion to the imperial policies of European powers.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 3

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
2The Growth and Character of the Institutionp. 7

Badger examines the world's fair as an institution, which between 1851 and 1925 became a standard part of Western culture. Each fair claimed to display the latest stage of progress, from British machinery and Bessemer steel in London to German motors at Paris in 1867 and the telephone and typewriter at Philadelphia in 1876. Architecture and the arts had to show refinement, which gave them a largely functional role, and the congresses held alongside the fairs seldom did more than reflect current thought. National purposes shaped many fairs, from Austria's recovery in Vienna in 1873 to Napoleon III's rivalry with London in 1855 and 1867. Badger then turns to finance, showing that governments subsidised fairs and that few made a profit, and to the city, which he treats as the greatest exhibit of any fair. The chapter closes by arguing that the claimed economic gains to host cities, hard to measure, were used to win public money from city governments.

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
5Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolisp. 134Asia

Geppert examines the British Empire Exhibition of 1924 and 1925 at Wembley, and the stadium built for it, treating the site as a suburban metropolis rather than a simple national display. The chapter opens with the 1990s plan to demolish the stadium, then recovers a forgotten layer of the ground: Watkin's Folly, an unfinished imitation of the Eiffel Tower abandoned in 1895, which ties Wembley to the London–Paris rivalry. The exhibition, revived in 1920 to answer London's lack of a monumental image as an imperial capital, is shown to order the Empire spatially through its symmetrical layout, Kipling's street names, reinforced-concrete pavilions and the Wembley lion emblem. The main cases are the Government Pavilion with its illuminated world map, the Indian pavilion, the West African 'Walled City' and a 1924 student protest over a newspaper article about it. Visitor accounts, from schoolchildren to a young Somali and German official reports, show how readily the imaginary world tour was accepted. The chapter concludes that Wembley was a self-referential celebration of Empire that also marked the beginning of its end.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.

Henry opens in 1925 with the Korea Shrine on Namsan, built by the colonial government in Keijo, and with an official's report of Koreans who looked around but did not worship there. This failure of assimilation frames the book, which treats the city's public spaces as a crucible for Japanese rule. Henry reads assimilation as a set of contested projects in spiritual, material and civic life, and argues against top-down accounts by looking at how shrines, expositions and neighborhoods were used by settlers, Korean elites and poor residents. The introduction sets out three periods, 1910–25, 1925–37 and 1937–45, and the five chapters that follow. It draws on Mary Louise Pratt's idea of contact zones and explains that the sources are limited by the colonial archive, so the book reads Japanese-language newspapers and Korean papers against the grain.

Encyclopedia entry 'Paris 1925' by Philip Whalen, pp. 237-43 (the fair's own entry); Paris 1937 entry p. 290 for later comparison; Stockholm 1930, Chicago 1933 and Antwerp 1930 entries for later references to 1925

Whalen's encyclopedia entry describes the 1925 Paris exhibition's layout, inaugural ceremony, pavilions and the Art Deco style that the fair gave its name to. The entry presents the fair as a showcase that fused luxury, French craft tradition and modern design, and it argues that the department-store pavilions 'underscored the exposition's commercial rather than democratic orientation'. Le Corbusier's and Melnikov's modernist pavilions are treated as exceptions within a celebratory, decorative fair. Wikipedia

The International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts (French: Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes) was a specialized exhibition held in Paris, France, from April 29 (the day after it was inaugurated in a private ceremony by the President of France) to November 8, 1925 (Originally the event was scheduled to end on October 25, but since it was visited by over 16 million people by the end of October, it was extended for two more weeks). It was designed by the French government to highlight the new modern style of architecture, interior decoration, furniture, glass, jewelry and other decorative arts in Europe and throughout the world. Many ideas of the international avant-garde in the fields of architecture and applied arts were presented for the first time at the exposition.
