International Art Exhibition (Rome)

Esposizione internazionale d'arte
OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Esposizione internazionale d'arte, which Geppert calls the Esposizione Internazionale di Belle Arte, was held in Rome in 1911, the year Italy marked fifty years of unification (Della Coletta 2006p. 85; Geppert 2010p. 151; Filipová 2015p. 38). The texts name no organising committee for it. Della Coletta treats it as a representational event beside Turin's industrial fair, and reads its classical-revival architecture as a claim to imperial glory (Della Coletta 2006p. 9; Della Coletta 2006p. 85). Other texts mention it only briefly (Geppert 2010p. 219; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 401).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Rome beside Turin and Florence

Rome's exhibition was one of three held in 1911, the others being in Turin and Florence (Filipová 2015p. 38). Della Coletta says Rome had waited until 1911 to host an international exhibition and then had "a purely representational and subordinate role" (Della Coletta 2006p. 9). The mayors of Turin and Rome, Secondo Frola and Ernesto Nathan, issued a joint proclamation that gave Turin the industrial exposition and Rome the historical, patriotic and artistic exhibits (Della Coletta 2006p. 86).

Della Coletta reads this division as a contradiction. Rome supplied the idea and Turin the action, and he asks why an idea held in Rome had to travel to Turin to be put into practice (Della Coletta 2006p. 86). He argues that the arts exhibits served as a didactic tool for patriotic ideas and political propaganda, and that the bourgeoisie gave Rome a ceremonial role only belatedly, in 1911 (Della Coletta 2006p. 87).

Architecture and style

The architects Piacentini, Bazzani and Venturi used a classical revival for the exhibition (Della Coletta 2006p. 85). They explained that it was directly inspired by the pure forms of ancient Rome (Della Coletta 2006p. 85). Della Coletta reads this as a claim to the imperial glories of a revered collective past (Della Coletta 2006p. 85). He also notes that the same revival later served as a well-known instrument of Fascist propaganda (Della Coletta 2006p. 86).

Empire and colony

The texts show no colonial sections, colonial exhibits or colonised people on display at the Rome exhibition. Its imperial side lies in its architecture. Augusto Sistri, quoted by Della Coletta, saw the exhibits' columns, statues and pediments as materials for a future imperial course in Italian architecture, and noted that it was the year of the Libyan war (Della Coletta 2006p. 85). The texts give no evidence that the organisers wanted the exhibition to serve empire, trade or settlement. Della Coletta describes its purpose as national education and propaganda (Della Coletta 2006p. 87).

Who took part

Székely describes the Hungarian pavilions at Rome and elsewhere as conceived to be "true mirrors" of Hungary's modernised economy and culture for a mostly foreign audience (Filipová 2015p. 36). British involvement is recorded in more detail. Special royal commissions were formed for the 1911 exhibitions in Turin and Rome (Geppert 2010p. 219). Edwin Lutyens, responsible for the British pavilion at the 1900 Paris exhibition, designed a hall for the Rome exhibition eleven years later (Geppert 2010p. 150). A biographical note by Geppert says instead that Lutyens designed British pavilions for Rome 1911, later rebuilt as the British School (Geppert 2010p. 272). The texts do not reconcile the two accounts.

Its place in the series

Della Coletta says Turin aimed to replace Rome on the exposition floor as the rightful leader of the country (Della Coletta 2006p. 46). Rome had hosted no international exhibition before 1911, and had failed to host a universal exposition in the second half of the nineteenth century (Della Coletta 2006p. 9; Della Coletta 2006p. 87).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Rome and Turin — Della Coletta sets Rome's role as the source of ideas against Turin's role as the place of action, and treats Rome's 1911 role as subordinate (Della Coletta 2006p. 86; Della Coletta 2006p. 9)
  • Classical revival — the architects' style is read as a claim to imperial glory and later as Fascist propaganda (Della Coletta 2006p. 85; Della Coletta 2006p. 86)
  • Art as propaganda — the arts exhibits are read as a didactic tool for patriotic ideas and political propaganda (Della Coletta 2006p. 87)
  • Jubilee of unification — the exhibition was one of three 1911 exhibitions marking fifty years of unification (Filipová 2015p. 38)
  • Foreign national sections — Hungarian pavilions, British royal commissions, and Lutyens's work at Rome appear in the texts (Filipová 2015p. 36; Geppert 2010p. 219; Geppert 2010p. 151)

Questions for study

  1. If Rome's exhibition was representational and Turin's industrial, what did each city's exhibits teach visitors about the nation? The texts describe the Rome exhibits only through their architecture and purposes (Della Coletta 2006p. 86).
  2. Why did the Turin baroque fail as a national style, while the Roman classical revival could, in Della Coletta's reading, claim imperial glory? (Della Coletta 2006p. 85; Della Coletta 2006p. 85)
  3. Sistri linked the Roman classical exhibits to the year of the Libyan war. How far should a reader connect the Rome exhibition to Italy's colonial projects when the texts show no colonial displays there? (Della Coletta 2006p. 85)
  4. Why do the two encyclopedias list the Rome exhibition only among fairs excluded from their main entries? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 426; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395)
  5. Did Lutyens design a hall or British pavilions at Rome, and what does the difference suggest about the sources the texts use? (Geppert 2010p. 151; Geppert 2010p. 272)

Della Coletta, Cristina. World’s Fairs Italian-Style: The Great Expositions in Turin and Their Narratives, 1860–1915. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2006.
2Turin 1911: The ‘Fabulous Exposition’p. 79

Della Coletta argues that the 1911 Turin exposition, staged to mark fifty years of Italian unity, combined industrial display with propaganda for the nation, a national architectural style and colonial expansion. The chapter starts with the architecture, where the organizers adopted the Turin baroque of Juvarra as the fair's overall style and the Rome exhibition of the same year used a classical revival instead. It then turns to the colonial displays for Eritrea and Somalia, the Somali village, and the 'Oriental' fairground that the Frenchman Pourtauborde laid out across the Po. Official magazines, Touring Club guides, press coverage of Scipione Borghese's Peking-to-Paris motor race and of Luigi Amedeo's polar expedition supply further evidence. The argument is that the fair prepared public consent for the war on Turkey, declared on 30 September 1911 while it was open, and that the Turin baroque never became a shared national symbol. The chapter ends by calling Turin 1911 the last act of the fairs' drama of progress.

Della Coletta treats Rome's 1911 Esposizione internazionale d'arte as the counterpart to Turin's industrial fair held in the same year. In his account Rome had a representational and subordinate role, and its classical-revival architecture and its historical, patriotic and artistic exhibits served patriotic and propagandist ends. He reads the classical revival as a claim to imperial glory and notes its later use in Fascist propaganda. Rome thus serves his argument that the fairs' idea of the nation was split between ideas in Rome and industry in Turin.

“a purely representational and subordinate role” p. 9
About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

This book argues that Italy's exhibitions between 1860 and 1915 were tools of nation building rather than imitations of French and British fairs. Della Coletta reads the fairs as consensus-making machines that repeated ideas about national identity, imperial competition and progress, while their counter-narratives survived as silences and contradictions. Turin, which sponsored more national and international fairs than any other Italian city, is the main case: chapters cover the Turin fairs from 1850 to 1911, including 1911, which marked fifty years of unity. Two chapters then read texts that carry exposition habits into literature, Emilio Salgari's adventure novels and Guido Gozzano's writing on India and the 1911 fair. An introduction and a conclusion frame the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present but not central. Their fullest treatment is chapter 3, Emilio Salgari: Writing Exposition Style, where the Malay archipelago, Borneo and Siam supply the settings and ethnic inventories of the jungle novels, and Sandokan's Borneo kingdom, destroyed by British and Dutch allies of the sultan, is read against Garibaldi's campaigns. Here the concern is fiction and the European ranking of peoples, not exhibitions. Chapter 1 mentions a Chinese-pagoda kiosk at the 1884 Turin fair. Chapter 2, Turin 1911, has the most exhibition material: Pourtauborde's Oriental fairground, which evoked China, Siam, Japan and Indochina as 'Oriental peoples'; the Filipino villages at the 1904 Saint Louis fair, cited as a model for 'native' displays; and Barzini's Beijing-to-Paris race reports, read as a fair-like image of a motionless East. Chapter 4 touches Japan only through war photographs in illustrated magazines. Asia appears as a theatre of display and fiction, never as exhibitor: no Asian state, commissioner or merchant is discussed, no fair held in Asia is considered, and Asian observers barely appear. South Asia gets much more space, through Gozzano's India letters in chapter 4, which lie outside this question. The colonial sections of Turin 1911 concentrate on Eritrea and Somalia, so Southeast Asia is reduced to a list.

Ch. 1, by Miklós Székely (Budapest exhibition plans), pp. 36, 38

Székely's chapter on Budapest universal exhibition plans mentions the 1911 Rome exhibition in two places. It notes that the Hungarian pavilions at Rome and other cities were conceived as 'true mirrors' of a modernised Hungary for a mostly foreign audience. In the conclusion it groups the 1911 exhibitions in Turin, Florence and Rome as simultaneous commemorations of Italian unification, and argues that hosting an international exhibition had become a sign of a modern country's ambition.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
Chapter on Wembley 1924 (pp. 150-151); ch. 7 Conclusion (pp. 219-220); appendix (pp. 252-253, 272)

Geppert mentions the 1911 Rome exhibition in four passages, none of them extended. In one he says Edwin Lutyens designed a hall there, while a biographical note says Lutyens designed British pavilions for it, and the texts do not reconcile the two. In the conclusion he records that special royal commissions were formed for Turin and Rome in 1911, in the same passage that describes the Board of Trade's exhibitions branch founded in 1908, and he lists the exhibition in his appendix of European exhibitions. The Rome case serves his wider argument about how governments organised exhibitions within the European network.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

On the web

Wikipedia

International Exhibition of Art (Italian: Esposizione internazionale d'arte) was a world's fair held in Rome in 1911 to celebrate the 50th anniversary of the unification of Italy in the same year as another world's fair in Turin (which had a more scientific focus). It marked the beginnings of the National Roman Museum. The fair's receipts were disappointing over the summer of 1911 because of poor weather and a cholera epidemic.
