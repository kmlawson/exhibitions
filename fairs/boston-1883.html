Boston · 1883–1884

American Exhibition of the Products, Arts and Manufactures of Foreign Nations (Boston)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The American Exhibition of the Products, Arts and Manufactures of Foreign Nations, known in Boston as the Foreign Exhibition, ran in Mechanics Hall from 3 September 1883 to 12 January 1884 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). It was the scaled-back form of a plan for an 1885 Boston world's fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). A private association led by Charles Benjamin Norton organised it, with foreign goods only (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). The fair drew 300,000 visitors and made a loss, although its organisers called it a success (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Boston's business leaders began discussing a world's fair for 1885 in late May and early June 1881 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). By January 1882 the funds would not be raised, and the plan was put on indefinite hold (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). In December 1882 committed leaders formed the Foreign Exhibition Association to keep the idea alive on a smaller scale, for foreign arts, products and manufactures only (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). Economic growth and the hope of boosting international trade brought the fair into being (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Because it opened on the hundredth anniversary of the end of the Revolutionary War, subdued claims were made that it was the true centennial exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74).

Organisers and finance

Norton, the association's secretary, was a former civil war brigadier general (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). The association was chartered under the auspices of the Massachusetts Charitable Mechanics Association (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Nathaniel J. Bradlee is named as president and Frederick W. Lincoln as treasurer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 418; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 384). The fair had no federal financial support, but the government gave official contact with foreign representatives (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). The texts disagree on the duty-free act: Noah W. Sobe dates it to 22 June 1882 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74), while Andrea Witczak dates it to June 1883 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). Display space was offered free to manufacturers and tradesmen who shipped goods to Boston (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Newspapers set the losses between $25,000 and $50,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75), and Witczak gives $25,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

Site and buildings

The fair was held in Mechanics Hall, a 92,000-square-foot building at Huntington Avenue and West Newton Street (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). William Gibbons Preston designed it in 1880, and it was completed in 1882 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). The hall had been built for the Mechanics Association's triennial exhibitions, not for this fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Witczak gives the cost as $500,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). Electric lighting allowed the fair to stay open in the evenings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). The building stood until 1959, when it was demolished for Boston's Prudential Center (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74).

Exhibits and attractions

The sources disagree on the number of exhibitors. Sobe gives exhibits from thirty countries and almost 700 foreign exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Witczak gives nearly 680 exhibits from forty countries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). Allwood and colleagues describe "exhibits from forty countries but none from the host country" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55). No exhibits came from the United States, the host country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Decorative objects such as fabric, pottery, furniture and jewellery were prominent, and Germany, Italy and France supplied the most works of art and manufactured goods (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Witczak says that easily sold goods outnumbered industrial products and machinery, and that the fair was unofficially called the Grand Bazaar (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). Visitors could see the Japanese Tea House and the Vienna Beer Vaults, and the Western Art Gallery was among the most popular exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

Visitors and reception

Attendance was 300,000 over four and a half months, which the texts call a disappointment (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81). Norton estimated that four-fifths of visitors came from outside Boston and that only 5 percent of the city's population visited (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81). The fair was due to close on 5 January 1884, but the directors extended it by a week at the foreign exhibitors' request and cut the admission price to twenty-five cents (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81). Newspapers judged it a financial failure, but Witczak records that foreign exhibitors and officials pronounced it a success in "the intangible areas of increased education and commerce between nations" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

Empire and colony

The texts show no colonial organisation, colonial village or colonised people on display at this fair, and no plan to use it for empire, trade or settlement. Sobe notes that exhibits came from parts of the British Empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74), but he does not say more about them. The organisers' stated aims were economic growth, trade and education (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81). The fair had no host-country exhibits (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80).

How historians have interpreted it

Two reference entries interpret the fair. Noah W. Sobe argues that its most significant legacy may lie in the cultural dimensions of some of its art exhibitions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). He points to Paul Durand-Ruel's display of French Impressionist works, which helped create a taste for French and later American Impressionism in the United States, and to William Morris's work for the British arts and crafts movement (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75). Andrea Witczak reads the fair mainly in economic terms, stressing the hope of increased exports and imports (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80). She calls it a financial failure, but records that its educational and commercial aims were claimed as a success (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

Japanese and Chinese displays

Sobe reports that the Chinese and Japanese exhibits attracted considerable attention, and that the Japanese display had a tea room and a living ethnological display of fourteen Japanese women doing traditional craftwork (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). He argues that Japanese exhibits at Boston followed earlier fairs in introducing Japanese aesthetics to Western audiences, and helped cement late nineteenth-century American admiration for Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74). Witczak lists a Japanese Tea House among the attractions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81). Sobe's bibliography also cites an illustrated catalogue of the Japanese art exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75).

The Korean mission

Park's article says that in Boston the Pobingsa mission members were particularly intent on attending the American Exhibition (Parkp. 4). Impressed by the spectacle and technology, reformist officials planned an industrial exposition for Seoul the following year, but the plan failed for lack of capital and after the political turmoil that followed the coup of 4 December 1884 (Parkp. 5). On the mission's second visit, its members entered Korean material into the exhibition, and the official catalogue listed it under Korea, from the government, beginning with porcelain and china vases (Parkp. 5). Park quotes a New York Times report of 16 December 1883 that Minister Min Yong Ik showed great appreciation for the agricultural implements he saw at the Boston exhibition (Parkp. 5).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commerce and education — The fair is treated as an economic and educational project, from the 1885 plan onward, with officials claiming gains in education and commerce between nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74).
  • Foreign goods, no host exhibits — The fair showed foreign goods only, mostly decorative and easily sold, and had no exhibits from the United States (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 80; Allwood et al. 2001p. 55).
  • Japan and taste — The Japanese tea room and craftswomen drew attention, and Sobe links them to American admiration for Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 74; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).
  • Korean participation — Park treats the Boston fair as a stage in the Pobingsa mission's interest in expositions, with Korean material entered and a minister impressed (Parkp. 4; Parkp. 5; Parkp. 5).
  • Money and attendance — Weak attendance and losses made it a financial failure in the press, against the organisers' claims of success (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 81).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the organisers count a fair with 300,000 visitors and a loss as a success, and what did "education and commerce between nations" mean in practice?
  2. The texts give thirty or forty countries and between about 680 and 700 exhibits. Which figures can a student rely on, and what would the statistical appendices show if they were legible?
  3. The texts disagree on the year of the duty-free act. What evidence would settle the date?
  4. Sobe locates the fair's legacy in art exhibitions rather than in trade. How far does the evidence he gives support that claim?

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

Wikipedia

The American Exhibition of the Products, Arts and Manufactures of Foreign Nations was held in Boston in 1883 and 1884 in the Mechanics Hall. It was the world's fair in America that had hosted the most foreign exhibitors at that time, including China and Japan. Henry A. Peirce was the Special Commissioner for the Hawaii (then Kingdom of Hawaii) exhibition. Although a locally organized exhibition, Congress sanctioned the inclusion of exhibits without duty. Morris & Co. exhibited and occupied six rooms (total space forty-five feet by thirty feet) showing tapestries and carpets.
