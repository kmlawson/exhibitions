Sydney · 1879–1880

Sydney International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Sydney International Exhibition ran in the Garden Palace, in the Outer Domain of Government House, from 17 September 1879 to 20 April 1880 (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). It was the first international exhibition in the Southern Hemisphere (Findling & Pelle 2008) and the first of Australia's colonial international exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001) (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts treat it as a symbol of Australia's place among the nations, as a display of settler power over Aboriginal and Pacific peoples, and as a commercial showcase (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Hoffenberg 2001) (Thomas 1991). Historians disagree about who made it happen, what it meant and whom it served (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001) (Findling & Pelle 1990).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Orr says the proposal was first advanced by the Royal Agricultural Society, but that Governor Sir Hercules Robinson argued the colony's reputation was at stake, and the colonial government took over the project (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood traces the origin instead to the Agricultural Society of New South Wales, whose annual shows began in 1870 and by 1877 included exhibits from New Zealand, Fiji, Ceylon and Canada (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood also credits Jules Joubert, secretary of that society from 1867, with conceiving and largely organising the fair (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr lists the stated aims as new trade and finance, a higher international profile for the colony, public education in the arts and sciences, and nation building (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Coté's note lists Sydney among the colonial exhibitions held in 1854, 1861, 1870 and 1873, all before the 1879 fair (Cotép. 37). Hoffenberg, by contrast, says that Sydney initiated the series of colonial international exhibitions in 1879 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 11). Coté does not say whether the earlier Sydney shows were international, so the note qualifies the claim in the interpretation section above that Sydney began the colonial series.

Organisation and finance

The colonial government of New South Wales met the costs (Findling & Pelle 1990). Yengoyan says the initial budget of £25,000 nearly doubled in the end (Findling & Pelle 1990). Badger's appendix gives the 1879 fair receipts of £210,372 and costs of £313,987, a gap of £103,615 (Badger 1979). Yengoyan, citing the Official Exhibition Record of 1881, gives the same loss and says plainly: "Overall the exhibition lost money." (Findling & Pelle 1990) Yengoyan adds that the large sums spent in Sydney compensated for the loss, and Allwood's summary table records the same £103,615 loss (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood also reports that Joubert, who had been secretary to the New South Wales commissioners at the Paris exhibition of 1878, was left out of the 1879 commission and was later dismissed by the Agricultural Society (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts agree on the size of the loss but not on who ran the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001).

Site and architecture

Orr credits the colonial architect James Barnet with the design, and describes a dome of timber that was the largest in the Southern Hemisphere (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan describes a masonry principal dome with timber galleries, and says the building was modelled on London's Crystal Palace (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood describes a 100-foot dome of permanent masonry, with the rest of the building in wood (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts disagree on the materials. Before the opening, Allwood reports a public outcry over the building's site in the Outer Domain, where many feared it would ruin the appearance of the harbour (Allwood et al. 2001). Work began in January 1879 with 800 workers, and by May the labour force had reached 3,000 after electric light allowed night work (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg notes that weekly photographs of the building, taken from one spot in 1879, show no workers (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg also describes a strike by about four hundred skilled carpenters, which the commissioners and contractor defeated; a note adds that the strike failed because of the colony's mass unemployment (Hoffenberg 2001). The building burned on 22 September 1882 (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr says the loss devastated the community and its sense of identity (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood reports that many people thought the convict files stored in the building were a clue to the cause (Allwood et al. 2001).

Exhibits and trade

The classification followed the one used at Philadelphia in 1876, across six departments, and Yengoyan says the focus was mainly agriculture and livestock (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood sets this against Melbourne's exhibition of the following year: "Unlike the Sydney exhibition which was very agriculturally orientated," the Melbourne show concentrated on industry and manufacturers (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood's examples of Sydney exhibits include a patent food for fowls, mining tools and a prize-winning Devon bull (Allwood et al. 2001). Hotta-Lister says the Sydney fair gave an impetus to direct Japanese exports to Australia, after a Japanese government budget of about 40,000 yen; rice, green tea and red tea won prizes there, and Japan's expenditure, in an appendix, was 29,817 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999). Hoffenberg dates the Tea Association's push into Australia to the 1879 Sydney exhibition, working through James Inglis (Hoffenberg 2001). Thomas describes Fiji's Sydney and Melbourne exhibits as mostly European goods, such as timber samples, cotton seed and cigars, with a smaller space for Fijian cloth, mats, bowls, pottery and fishing nets (Thomas 1991).

Alexander Dobbie, a South Australian engineer, studied machinery at the Paris Universal Exposition of 1878. He then exhibited his own machines at Sydney and earned the First Degree of Merit there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 166). The New South Wales commissioner in Paris had written to the Sydney officials that the fair would teach artisans and colonists about labour-saving machinery from all parts of the world (Hoffenberg 2001p. 166).

Visitors to the Garden Palace were greeted by the Mineral Trophy, a towering obelisk that compared the ores of the Australasian colonies. Its sections matched the vertical sections of coal seams worked in New South Wales (Hoffenberg 2001p. 76). Hoffenberg says that the mounted obelisk dwarfed visitors and showed the colonies' coal and gold output in absolute, comparative and monetary terms (Hoffenberg 2001p. 204). Allwood notes that the Paris exhibition of 1878 included a direct copy of the State of Victoria's gold obelisk from London in 1862 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 50).

William Macleay sought a place for his wines in the food and beverage saloons at the Sydney exhibition of 1879 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109). Joubert served as a wine juror there, and the jurors were "judging side by side the wines of all countries" for the first time in the Southern Hemisphere (Hoffenberg 2001p. 109). After more than fifty meetings and tastings, the final report called Australian wine one of the most hopeful sources of national prosperity, because of the local climate and better cultivation and bottling (Hoffenberg 2001p. 110).

Peoples on display

Orr says no items were exhibited by Australian Aboriginal people, though there were displays of native weaponry and pseudo-historical tableaux of spear-carrying natives in fern valleys with wallabies and kangaroos (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hoffenberg describes a larger display. Sydney's organisers collected and displayed living natives and Aboriginal specimens, and an Ethnological Court, a display of costumes, weapons and specimens, held over five thousand objects which, according to Australian and English experts, portrayed a prehistoric or pre-European age (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg says Aboriginal people were admitted free to the Garden Palace on request, which the author treats as an exception to the usual practice of showing them through photographs and products (Hoffenberg 2001). The official catalogue, Hoffenberg adds, suggested that groups which did not adopt European clothes and ways faced extinction (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg's broader point is that "The apparent disappearance of such aboriginal peoples was made to seem a “natural” evolutionary development at the exhibitions." (Hoffenberg 2001) Maori and Fijian performers danced in houses built for the event, and an illustrator for a Sydney weekly added spears and skulls to an engraving of the performance to satisfy preconceptions of savagery (Hoffenberg 2001).

Visitors and reception

Yengoyan gives the attendance as 1,117,536, of which 850,480 were paid admissions, over seven months (Findling & Pelle 1990). Orr calls this the largest attendance in proportion to population of any international exhibition ever recorded (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hoffenberg says over one million visitors came in eight months, despite late planning and limited overseas advertising (Hoffenberg 2001), while Allwood gives the period as six months (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr concludes: "It would be hard to overestimate the exhibition’s cultural impact." (Findling & Pelle 2008)

Aftermath and legacy

Orr lists the legacies as new museum collections, tram lines and port development, a system of technical education, and growth of the Art Gallery of New South Wales (Findling & Pelle 2008). Orr names the Technological Museum, now the Powerhouse Museum, and Sydney Technical College among the institutions the fair helped to create (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan says that after 1880 steamship lines ran between Australian ports and France and Germany, and that non-British capital, mainly French investment and banking, appeared in Melbourne and Sydney (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Night work and accidents on the site

John Young, a Sydney contractor, founded the Master Builders' Association of New South Wales to control the workforce and the production process. He was among the first contractors in Australia to use electric arc lights on exhibition sites, so that the working day could be extended (Hoffenberg 2001p. 181). Reports reached London that the lights allowed the Garden Palace to be built by night as the opening day approached (Hoffenberg 2001p. 181). A manager of the Peel River Land and Mineral Company wrote to his board in London that Young was making marvellous strides. He was enthusiastic about eight hundred men working at night under electric light, though not yet convinced that the method was a success (Hoffenberg 2001p. 181). That night shift is a different count from the daytime labour force given in the site section above, and the two should not be merged (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182).

Hoffenberg also quotes a verdict that the arc lights "cast very black shadows, not a few accidents resulting therefrom" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). He sets the Garden Palace against the night work at Alfred Park in 1870, for the Metropolitan show, which was lit by torches. A reporter described dark figures working in dangerous places amid hammering and fire-balls (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). Hoffenberg argues that nostalgia tended to erase accidents at such sites from memory (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182). At South Kensington in 1862, workers had fallen to their deaths in the race to finish the twin towers of the London International Exhibition building (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182).

Policing the gates and the night opening

A deputation asked the colonial secretary to open the 1879 Sydney exhibition at night, so that people who worked during the day could visit it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 213). Commissioners in Sydney, Melbourne and Adelaide were anxious to supply gas or electric light for evening visits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 213).

The Sydney commissioners drew up special regulations for the police on duty. Officers had to watch over property, answer visitors' questions about exhibits, prevent overcrowding or blocking, remove intoxicated people and preserve good order (Hoffenberg 2001p. 235). The Garden Palace was among the first halls to use the recently patented, self-registering turnstiles, so the number of visitors could be readily found at any time in 1879 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237). Exit gates let people leave easily but were designed to stop them returning. They took the place of direct supervision by attendants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237).

Fiji and the Ethnological Court

The Fijian war dance was especially popular and reached the local and London press (Hoffenberg 2001p. 225). Hoffenberg notes that visitors could interview the Maori and Fijians, including the local reporter for the Graphic, but that commentary on Aboriginal people was limited (Hoffenberg 2001p. 226). Officials advertised the Ethnological Court as the last chance to see a collection of authentic wares (Hoffenberg 2001p. 225). The Official Catalogue concluded that the Australian Aboriginal "appears to have few aspirations beyond the satisfying of the necessities of nature" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 226).

Thomas's Fijian material at Sydney was different again. The Fiji stall was headed "Resources and Progress of Fiji" (Thomas 1991p. 176). Out of dozens of items, there were very few weapons at Sydney and none at all at Melbourne. This reversed the Fiji Cannibal Exhibition shown on the American West Coast, in which the central element was cannibalism (Thomas 1991p. 165). At Sydney and Melbourne, craftsmanship took its place, and Fijians could be drawn into economic enterprise (Thomas 1991p. 176).

Art sales after Sydney

Art sent from Britain for the Sydney exhibitions was shown at the Queensland Exhibition in Brisbane in July 1880, then at Melbourne, and then in the new public library building in Adelaide (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53). Some £14,000 worth of these pictures was sold at the exhibitions, many of them to public art galleries. The twenty-nine James Barry etchings sent by the London Society of Arts went to the Melbourne National Gallery (Allwood et al. 2001p. 53).

Joubert after 1879

Allwood says that Joubert was charged with sending private property out as returned exhibits, and that after much argument the Agricultural Society sacked him. He then left Sydney for Adelaide (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). There he organised South Australia's first international exhibition of 1881, with the help of R. E. N. Twopeny (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). He moved on to Perth at the end of 1881 and then to Christchurch, where the New Zealand International Exhibition of 1882 was run under the private management of Joubert and Twopeny (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). He organised the Calcutta International Exhibition of 1883-84, went bankrupt again in 1887, and in 1888 organised New South Wales's exhibits for the Melbourne Centennial (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). Later shows took him to Dunedin, Launceston and Hobart, and to Brisbane in 1897. He died in Melbourne in 1907, after more than fifty exhibitions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). Allwood thinks few have matched him for experience and excitement, though he adds a remark about nepotism (Allwood et al. 2001p. 55).

How historians have interpreted it

The readings differ mainly in emphasis. Orr reads the fair as a moment of colonial self-examination: it encouraged Australians to consider their identity, their achievements, their place in the world and the British Empire, and the prospect of nationhood (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan reads it through markets: the fair symbolised the colony's existence among the nations and encouraged non-British commercial interests to treat Australia as a market (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg reads the Sydney displays as reconfirming the power of settlers and their state, presenting Aboriginal people as a prehistoric past (Hoffenberg 2001). Roche, through MacKenzie, reads such colonial shows as a celebration of white settlement (Roche 2000). Allwood's emphasis falls on the agricultural origins of the fair and on the entrepreneurs who ran it, above all Joubert (Allwood et al. 2001).

Most of these texts agree that Sydney began the colonial series. Allwood calls it the first of a series of Australian international exhibitions (Allwood et al. 2001), Hoffenberg says it initiated the series (Hoffenberg 2001), and Neale describes the Australian colonies beginning with Sydney (Filipová 2015). They differ on the start date. Neale gives 1878–79, and Mandell places the fair in the summer of 1878–79 (Filipová 2015) (Mandell 1967); the reference works and most other texts give 17 September 1879 as the opening date (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). They also differ on who drove the fair: Orr credits the Royal Agricultural Society and the governor (Findling & Pelle 2008), while Allwood credits Joubert (Allwood et al. 2001). On Aboriginal display, Orr says no Aboriginal items were shown, but Hoffenberg describes an Ethnological Court with Aboriginal costumes and specimens (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Hoffenberg 2001). The texts do not reconcile these accounts. Only Hoffenberg discusses how Aboriginal people were admitted to the fair, and the texts say little about how Indigenous visitors experienced it (Hoffenberg 2001). Geppert, Lawn, Coté, Badger, Roche and Mandell mention the fair only in passing (Geppert 2010) (Lawn 2009) (Coté) (Badger 1979) (Roche 2000) (Mandell 1967).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan and India at the opening

Allwood says the commissioners of Victoria and New South Wales took the chance offered at Paris in 1878 to invite foreign nations to the Sydney exhibition, and that "all the great European nations, the United States of America, Japan, India and the other British colonies" were represented (Allwood et al. 2001p. 51). The texts name no other Asian country as exhibiting at Sydney. Hoffenberg records that the opening procession included official visitors from Germany, Japan, Holland and Italy (Hoffenberg 2001p. 263).

Japanese goods and prizes

Hotta-Lister says the Sydney exhibition gave "an impetus" to exporting Japanese goods directly to Australia, and that it had a government budget of about 40,000 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212). The appendix on Japan's exhibitions gives the Sydney expenditure as 29,817 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The two figures measure different things, budget and spending, and the texts do not reconcile them. At Sydney, rice, green tea and red tea won prizes (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212).

India and the tea trade

Hoffenberg says that India's commissioners forwarded samples of cotton, hides, medical plants and woods to the Sydney International Exhibition, and in return sought Australian manufactured goods and "Livestock, Horses, principally" for the Indian Army [31, l. 6353-6357; note 111, l. 16439-16440]. The Tea Association's "concerted" efforts to introduce Indian teas into Australia began with the 1879 Sydney exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). James Inglis, a former tea planter in India and commissioner for the Indian government, worked with the association and the Indian government's Department of Revenue and Agriculture to show, sell and exchange teas and other South Asian exhibits at the Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). Hoffenberg also says that Joubert, while in Sydney, encouraged South Asian displays at the Metropolitan Exhibitions of the Royal Agricultural Society (Hoffenberg 2001p. 119). Those were a separate series, not the international fair.

Origins

Allwood says the fair grew from the annual shows of the Agricultural Society of New South Wales, which by 1877 had exhibits from New Zealand, Fiji, Ceylon and Canada (Allwood et al. 2001p. 51). Ceylon's place in the fair itself is not described.

What the texts leave out

The encyclopedia and dictionary entries on the fair mention no Asian displays, exhibitors or visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 65; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 72). The texts give no account of how Asian visitors responded to the displays. Hoffenberg's contrast between Indian tea and "Chinese imports" concerns the Melbourne exhibition of 1880-81, not Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). The texts say nothing about any Chinese presence at Sydney in 1879.

German, French and Japanese exhibits

The local executive commissioner for the 1879 Sydney exhibition said that close public attention to the Industrial and Art sections of the German Court would give Sydney consumers a more accurate knowledge of German manufactures, and that existing trade with Germany might grow (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). He made similar remarks about the French and Japanese exhibits, which he said held out the promise of profitable direct trade in future (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122). Hoffenberg gives no sales figures for either country at Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 122).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial identity and nationhood — Orr treats the fair as a moment when Australians considered their identity, achievements and place in the world, and describes the 1882 fire as a loss of identity (Findling & Pelle 2008); Hoffenberg places the Sydney displays within settler power (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Indigenous and Pacific display — Orr notes spear-carrying natives in tableaux but no Aboriginal items (Findling & Pelle 2008); Hoffenberg describes the Ethnological Court and the Maori and Fijian performers (Hoffenberg 2001); Thomas reads the Fijian displays as making a colony visible (Thomas 1991); Roche quotes MacKenzie on white settlement (Roche 2000).
  • Trade and markets — Yengoyan links the fair to non-British commercial interests and steamship lines (Findling & Pelle 1990); Hotta-Lister to Japan's exports (Hotta-Lister 1999); Hoffenberg to the Tea Association's Indian tea trade (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Money and loss — the £103,615 loss is recorded by Yengoyan (Findling & Pelle 1990), Badger's receipts and costs imply the same figure (Badger 1979), and Allwood's table gives it too (Allwood et al. 2001).
  • The Garden Palace — its design, materials, construction and fire are described differently by Orr (Findling & Pelle 2008), Yengoyan (Findling & Pelle 1990), Allwood (Allwood et al. 2001) and Hoffenberg, who adds the carpenters' strike (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Agriculture against industry — Yengoyan stresses agriculture and livestock (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Allwood contrasts Sydney with Melbourne's manufacturing focus (Allwood et al. 2001).

Questions for study

  1. Orr credits the Royal Agricultural Society and the governor with the fair, while Allwood credits Joubert. What does this disagreement suggest about who controlled a colonial exhibition? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001)
  2. The texts disagree on whether the Garden Palace's dome was timber or masonry. Does the answer change how we read the fire of 1882 and the sense of loss that Orr describes? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001)
  3. Yengoyan records a loss of £103,615 but says the money spent in Sydney compensated for it. Should a fair that lost money be judged a success, and by whose measure? (Findling & Pelle 1990)
  4. Hoffenberg says Aboriginal people were admitted free to the fair, yet presents them as a prehistoric past. How did the Ethnological Court differ from the Maori and Fijian performances, and why might that matter? (Hoffenberg 2001)
  5. Orr reports that no Aboriginal items were exhibited, while Hoffenberg describes Aboriginal costumes and specimens in the Ethnological Court. How would a historian check which account is right? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Hoffenberg 2001)
  6. Hoffenberg's eight hundred men at night and the daytime labour force in the site section describe different shifts. How many men built the Garden Palace, and how would a historian check the figures? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 182)
  7. Hoffenberg says the turnstiles could record visitors in 1879, but he does not say which published attendance total they produced. Does the figure of 1,117,536 rest on that count? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237)
  8. Thomas finds very few weapons in the Fijian material at Sydney, while the Ethnological Court advertised its objects as a last chance to see authentic wares. Why did the same fair show Fijian craft and Aboriginal relics as relics of the past? (Thomas 1991p. 176; Hoffenberg 2001p. 225)

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
5Paris, Australia and all the Worldp. 46Asia

Allwood follows the exhibition circuit from Paris to the Antipodes in the 1870s and 1880s. The chapter opens with Paris 1878, set against the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian war and the Commune, and describes the Krantz building, the Rue des Nations, the ventilation system devised by the architect Hardy, and landmarks such as Doré's vase. Sala's complaints about exhaustion, dear restaurants and the dominance of large firms serve as evidence that exhibitions were turning from individual craftsmanship towards commerce. Most of the chapter then covers the Sydney exhibition of 1879 and the Melbourne exhibition of 1880, using visitor numbers, the Garden Palace and art sales to show their effects. The central figure is Jules Joubert, whose shows in Adelaide, Perth, Christchurch, Calcutta and Tasmania are traced in detail. The chapter ends with London's Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, which led to the founding of the Imperial Institute.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
1Exhibitions and the New Imperialismp. 1Asia

Hoffenberg argues that the popular exhibitions held in London and in colonial cities such as Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta between 1851 and 1914 deserve as much study as the Great Exhibition that overshadows them. The chapter shows how colonial commissioners used these shows to present Australia as a progressive settler society and India as a restored tradition. Its evidence includes the Doulton 'Impasto' panels at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, the Zenana Days in Calcutta, and Aboriginal Australians displayed at Sydney and Adelaide, cases in which visitors and exhibits sometimes returned the colonial gaze. The chapter also traces the afterlife of the shows in museum collections, catalogues and the classifications used to organise them. It argues that the exhibitions promoted an imperial market and a federated commonwealth, while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. It closes by claiming that exhibitions shaped social order through participation rather than coercion, and that their ideas about imperial federation carried into the Commonwealth.

In the contents of this work

4Consumers, Producers, and Markets: The Political Economy of Imperial Federationp. 99Asia

Hoffenberg examines how Australian and Indian commissioners, merchants and critics used international exhibitions to argue about the economics of empire. The displays of wool, coal, wine, cotton, silk and tea are read as political economies in their own right rather than neutral reflections of trade. Hoffenberg follows the Australian and Indian commodity courts from the Great Exhibition of 1851 through the London International Exhibition of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, with further material from Sydney in 1879 and Calcutta in 1883. Tea provides the chapter's clearest case: the Indian Tea Districts Association sold samples in London and then in Australia and North America, and the chapter traces how James Inglis and Jules Joubert encouraged direct trade between Australia and India without English middlemen. The argument is that colonial producers pursued independent commerce within an imperial framework, and that critics such as Carlyle and the radical press rejected the market ideals the shows celebrated. The chapter ends by noting that the displays both reinforced imperial economic unity and exposed its strains, which opens onto the question of colonial trade policy.

In the contents of this work

6Machines-in-Motion: Technology, Labor, and the Ironies of Industrialismp. 166Asia

Hoffenberg examines the working machines that drew crowds at English, Australian and Indian exhibitions from 1851 onwards. Machinery courts, reapers and steam-powered demonstrations presented industrialism as something visitors could watch and test, but the chapter argues that the same displays hid the labour that built and ran them. Machinery halls were framed as idealised factories, and visible hand workers, such as Indian artisans and women in the craft courts, were placed beside self-acting machines to suggest a harmonious industrial order. Hoffenberg shows this with Doulton panels at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, and with the building sites of London in 1862 and Sydney in 1879, where accidents and strikes were left out of official albums. Exhibitions did help diffuse new technology across England and the colonies, and machines kept on in museums and technical schools extended that reach. The chapter ends with the armaments displays, where guns and warships were admired as beautiful objects, so that the peaceful pledge of 1851 was undercut by war.

In the contents of this work

7Imperial and National Taxonomies: Entertaining and Policing Exhibition Visitorsp. 203Asia

Hoffenberg turns from exhibits to the people who visited exhibitions and to the ways commissioners classified, entertained and policed them. Visitors to the Great Exhibition of 1851, the London International of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 are treated as a visible social mass that organisers sorted by nation, race, class and gender. The evidence includes reduced fares for London workers, the appeal to guilds from neighbouring colonies for the Melbourne Intercolonial of 1866 to 1867, and the controversial zenana day at the Calcutta International of 1883 to 1884, when women in purdah were admitted to the grounds. Maori and Fijian performers at Sydney in 1879, and Aboriginal visitors from mission stations at Adelaide in 1887, are set against the static ethnographic models shown elsewhere, and the separate entrances at Nagpore in 1865 show how race and rank were policed at the door. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions offered social integration and imperial federation in economic and cultural terms, a citizenship that was real but not political. The chapter closes by linking these practices of ordering visitors to later management of migration in the twentieth century.

In the contents of this work

Thomas, Nicholas. Entangled Objects: Exchange, Material Culture, and Colonialism in the Pacific. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1991.
4The European Appropriation of Indigenous Thingsp. 125

Thomas turns from indigenous appropriation to European collecting, from Cook's voyages to the early twentieth century. Sailors and naturalists bought curiosities, and observers called objects curious without judging them, a vagueness Thomas links to the absence of any developed anthropological language of material culture. Later collectors gave objects narrative weight: Keate's Pelew bowl stood for a king's generosity, missionaries displayed burned idols as proof of conversion, and settlers in Fiji treated clubs and cannibal forks as evidence of murder. Anatole Von Hugel's systematic collecting and Governor Gordon's display of clubs and bowls at Government House in Levuka ordered Fijian culture into a totality for the colonial state. Fijian exhibits at Sydney 1879 and Melbourne 1880 showed craft with few weapons. The chapter ends with Frank Hurley's Papuan collecting, which it reads as a denial of the people who made the objects, and concludes that both sides reworked the meanings of things.

Substantial discussion 4

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
chapter 'Paris, Australia and All the World': Sydney description pp. 52-53; Joubert discussion from p. 54

Allwood's general history presents the Sydney fair as the start of Australia's run of international exhibitions, grown out of an agricultural show. Its account stresses the public outcry over the siting and scale of the Garden Palace and the popular belief, after the 1882 fire, that the convict files stored there had something to do with it. Mandell's book is a study of the Paris Exposition of 1900 set against the history of nineteenth-century world's fairs. Its argument is that the fair, planned as a display of French confidence, was shaped by politics: the state controlled it, opponents such as the Nancy editor Goulette campaigned against it, and the Dreyfus Affair was quietly suspended while it ran. The early chapters cover the French industrial exhibitions from 1798, the London exhibition of 1851 and the Paris expositions of 1855 to 1889. The rest follows the planning and opposition, a tour of the grounds, the political background, the verdict on success and the end of the era of great French expositions. The scope is Paris and France, with attention to national pavilions, decorative arts and finance.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention in this book, which mentions them in some 47 of its 7,866 lines, most of them in Chapter Four, 'At the Fair', where the coverage is substantial. Japan is named most often: its art made a sensation in Paris in 1867 and Vienna in 1873 (Chapter One), its request for more space was repeatedly refused and its delegation found the space insufficient (Chapter Three), and de Vogue judged that Japan surpassed France in taste (Chapter Six). China appears mainly in passing, such as a Chinese boy serving tea at the Asiatic Russia pavilion (Chapter Four). Chapter Four treats Asia as material on display, organised by others. The colonial section at the Trocadero held the pavilion of Indo-China, which Mandell says was grander than those of several sovereign states, and the Dutch East Indies; the Tour du Monde offered dioramas including Angkor Wat, and Javanese dancers and the Japanese performer Sadda Yakko drew crowds. Korean and Siamese pavilions appear only in plate captions. Siam is among the minor states that declined the invitation (Chapter Two). Southeast Asia beyond Indo-China, Java and Siam is not discussed, and no Asian visitors or observers are described.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.
