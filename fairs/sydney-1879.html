Sydney · 1879–1880

Sydney International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Sydney International Exhibition ran in the Garden Palace, in the Outer Domain of Government House, from 17 September 1879 to 20 April 1880 (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). It was the first international exhibition in the Southern Hemisphere (Findling & Pelle 2008) and the first of Australia's colonial international exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001) (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts treat it as a symbol of Australia's place among the nations, as a display of settler power over Aboriginal and Pacific peoples, and as a commercial showcase (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Hoffenberg 2001) (Thomas 1991). Historians disagree about who made it happen, what it meant and whom it served (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001) (Findling & Pelle 1990).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Orr says the proposal was first advanced by the Royal Agricultural Society, but that Governor Sir Hercules Robinson argued the colony's reputation was at stake, and the colonial government took over the project (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood traces the origin instead to the Agricultural Society of New South Wales, whose annual shows began in 1870 and by 1877 included exhibits from New Zealand, Fiji, Ceylon and Canada (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood also credits Jules Joubert, secretary of that society from 1867, with conceiving and largely organising the fair (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr lists the stated aims as new trade and finance, a higher international profile for the colony, public education in the arts and sciences, and nation building (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Organisation and finance

The colonial government of New South Wales met the costs (Findling & Pelle 1990). Yengoyan says the initial budget of £25,000 nearly doubled in the end (Findling & Pelle 1990). Badger's appendix gives the 1879 fair receipts of £210,372 and costs of £313,987, a gap of £103,615 (Badger 1979). Yengoyan, citing the Official Exhibition Record of 1881, gives the same loss and says plainly: "Overall the exhibition lost money." (Findling & Pelle 1990) Yengoyan adds that the large sums spent in Sydney compensated for the loss, and Allwood's summary table records the same £103,615 loss (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood also reports that Joubert, who had been secretary to the New South Wales commissioners at the Paris exhibition of 1878, was left out of the 1879 commission and was later dismissed by the Agricultural Society (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts agree on the size of the loss but not on who ran the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001).

Site and architecture

Orr credits the colonial architect James Barnet with the design, and describes a dome of timber that was the largest in the Southern Hemisphere (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan describes a masonry principal dome with timber galleries, and says the building was modelled on London's Crystal Palace (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood describes a 100-foot dome of permanent masonry, with the rest of the building in wood (Allwood et al. 2001). The texts disagree on the materials. Before the opening, Allwood reports a public outcry over the building's site in the Outer Domain, where many feared it would ruin the appearance of the harbour (Allwood et al. 2001). Work began in January 1879 with 800 workers, and by May the labour force had reached 3,000 after electric light allowed night work (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg notes that weekly photographs of the building, taken from one spot in 1879, show no workers (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg also describes a strike by about four hundred skilled carpenters, which the commissioners and contractor defeated; a note adds that the strike failed because of the colony's mass unemployment (Hoffenberg 2001). The building burned on 22 September 1882 (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr says the loss devastated the community and its sense of identity (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood reports that many people thought the convict files stored in the building were a clue to the cause (Allwood et al. 2001).

Exhibits and trade

The classification followed the one used at Philadelphia in 1876, across six departments, and Yengoyan says the focus was mainly agriculture and livestock (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood sets this against Melbourne's exhibition of the following year: "Unlike the Sydney exhibition which was very agriculturally orientated," the Melbourne show concentrated on industry and manufacturers (Allwood et al. 2001). Allwood's examples of Sydney exhibits include a patent food for fowls, mining tools and a prize-winning Devon bull (Allwood et al. 2001). Hotta-Lister says the Sydney fair gave an impetus to direct Japanese exports to Australia, after a Japanese government budget of about 40,000 yen; rice, green tea and red tea won prizes there, and Japan's expenditure, in an appendix, was 29,817 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999). Hoffenberg dates the Tea Association's push into Australia to the 1879 Sydney exhibition, working through James Inglis (Hoffenberg 2001). Thomas describes Fiji's Sydney and Melbourne exhibits as mostly European goods, such as timber samples, cotton seed and cigars, with a smaller space for Fijian cloth, mats, bowls, pottery and fishing nets (Thomas 1991).

Peoples on display

Orr says no items were exhibited by Australian Aboriginal people, though there were displays of native weaponry and pseudo-historical tableaux of spear-carrying natives in fern valleys with wallabies and kangaroos (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hoffenberg describes a larger display. Sydney's organisers collected and displayed living natives and Aboriginal specimens, and an Ethnological Court, a display of costumes, weapons and specimens, held over five thousand objects which, according to Australian and English experts, portrayed a prehistoric or pre-European age (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg says Aboriginal people were admitted free to the Garden Palace on request, which the author treats as an exception to the usual practice of showing them through photographs and products (Hoffenberg 2001). The official catalogue, Hoffenberg adds, suggested that groups which did not adopt European clothes and ways faced extinction (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg's broader point is that "The apparent disappearance of such aboriginal peoples was made to seem a “natural” evolutionary development at the exhibitions." (Hoffenberg 2001) Maori and Fijian performers danced in houses built for the event, and an illustrator for a Sydney weekly added spears and skulls to an engraving of the performance to satisfy preconceptions of savagery (Hoffenberg 2001).

Visitors and reception

Yengoyan gives the attendance as 1,117,536, of which 850,480 were paid admissions, over seven months (Findling & Pelle 1990). Orr calls this the largest attendance in proportion to population of any international exhibition ever recorded (Findling & Pelle 2008). Hoffenberg says over one million visitors came in eight months, despite late planning and limited overseas advertising (Hoffenberg 2001), while Allwood gives the period as six months (Allwood et al. 2001). Orr concludes: "It would be hard to overestimate the exhibition’s cultural impact." (Findling & Pelle 2008)

Aftermath and legacy

Orr lists the legacies as new museum collections, tram lines and port development, a system of technical education, and growth of the Art Gallery of New South Wales (Findling & Pelle 2008). Orr names the Technological Museum, now the Powerhouse Museum, and Sydney Technical College among the institutions the fair helped to create (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan says that after 1880 steamship lines ran between Australian ports and France and Germany, and that non-British capital, mainly French investment and banking, appeared in Melbourne and Sydney (Findling & Pelle 1990).

How historians have interpreted it

The readings differ mainly in emphasis. Orr reads the fair as a moment of colonial self-examination: it encouraged Australians to consider their identity, their achievements, their place in the world and the British Empire, and the prospect of nationhood (Findling & Pelle 2008). Yengoyan reads it through markets: the fair symbolised the colony's existence among the nations and encouraged non-British commercial interests to treat Australia as a market (Findling & Pelle 1990). Hoffenberg reads the Sydney displays as reconfirming the power of settlers and their state, presenting Aboriginal people as a prehistoric past (Hoffenberg 2001). Roche, through MacKenzie, reads such colonial shows as a celebration of white settlement (Roche 2000). Allwood's emphasis falls on the agricultural origins of the fair and on the entrepreneurs who ran it, above all Joubert (Allwood et al. 2001).

Most of these texts agree that Sydney began the colonial series. Allwood calls it the first of a series of Australian international exhibitions (Allwood et al. 2001), Hoffenberg says it initiated the series (Hoffenberg 2001), and Neale describes the Australian colonies beginning with Sydney (Filipová 2015). They differ on the start date. Neale gives 1878–79, and Mandell places the fair in the summer of 1878–79 (Filipová 2015) (Mandell 1967); the reference works and most other texts give 17 September 1879 as the opening date (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001). They also differ on who drove the fair: Orr credits the Royal Agricultural Society and the governor (Findling & Pelle 2008), while Allwood credits Joubert (Allwood et al. 2001). On Aboriginal display, Orr says no Aboriginal items were shown, but Hoffenberg describes an Ethnological Court with Aboriginal costumes and specimens (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Hoffenberg 2001). The texts do not reconcile these accounts. Only Hoffenberg discusses how Aboriginal people were admitted to the fair, and the texts say little about how Indigenous visitors experienced it (Hoffenberg 2001). Geppert, Lawn, Coté, Badger, Roche and Mandell mention the fair only in passing (Geppert 2010) (Lawn 2009) (Coté) (Badger 1979) (Roche 2000) (Mandell 1967).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan and India at the opening

Allwood says the commissioners of Victoria and New South Wales took the chance offered at Paris in 1878 to invite foreign nations to the Sydney exhibition, and that "all the great European nations, the United States of America, Japan, India and the other British colonies" were represented (Allwood et al. 2001p. 51). The texts name no other Asian country as exhibiting at Sydney. Hoffenberg records that the opening procession included official visitors from Germany, Japan, Holland and Italy (Hoffenberg 2001p. 263).

Japanese goods and prizes

Hotta-Lister says the Sydney exhibition gave "an impetus" to exporting Japanese goods directly to Australia, and that it had a government budget of about 40,000 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212). The appendix on Japan's exhibitions gives the Sydney expenditure as 29,817 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The two figures measure different things, budget and spending, and the texts do not reconcile them. At Sydney, rice, green tea and red tea won prizes (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212).

India and the tea trade

Hoffenberg says that India's commissioners forwarded samples of cotton, hides, medical plants and woods to the Sydney International Exhibition, and in return sought Australian manufactured goods and "Livestock, Horses, principally" for the Indian Army [31, l. 6353-6357; note 111, l. 16439-16440]. The Tea Association's "concerted" efforts to introduce Indian teas into Australia began with the 1879 Sydney exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). James Inglis, a former tea planter in India and commissioner for the Indian government, worked with the association and the Indian government's Department of Revenue and Agriculture to show, sell and exchange teas and other South Asian exhibits at the Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 117). Hoffenberg also says that Joubert, while in Sydney, encouraged South Asian displays at the Metropolitan Exhibitions of the Royal Agricultural Society (Hoffenberg 2001p. 119). Those were a separate series, not the international fair.

Origins

Allwood says the fair grew from the annual shows of the Agricultural Society of New South Wales, which by 1877 had exhibits from New Zealand, Fiji, Ceylon and Canada (Allwood et al. 2001p. 51). Ceylon's place in the fair itself is not described.

What the texts leave out

The encyclopedia and dictionary entries on the fair mention no Asian displays, exhibitors or visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 65; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 72). The texts give no account of how Asian visitors responded to the displays. Hoffenberg's contrast between Indian tea and "Chinese imports" concerns the Melbourne exhibition of 1880-81, not Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). The texts say nothing about any Chinese presence at Sydney in 1879.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial identity and nationhood — Orr treats the fair as a moment when Australians considered their identity, achievements and place in the world, and describes the 1882 fire as a loss of identity (Findling & Pelle 2008); Hoffenberg places the Sydney displays within settler power (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Indigenous and Pacific display — Orr notes spear-carrying natives in tableaux but no Aboriginal items (Findling & Pelle 2008); Hoffenberg describes the Ethnological Court and the Maori and Fijian performers (Hoffenberg 2001); Thomas reads the Fijian displays as making a colony visible (Thomas 1991); Roche quotes MacKenzie on white settlement (Roche 2000).
  • Trade and markets — Yengoyan links the fair to non-British commercial interests and steamship lines (Findling & Pelle 1990); Hotta-Lister to Japan's exports (Hotta-Lister 1999); Hoffenberg to the Tea Association's Indian tea trade (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Money and loss — the £103,615 loss is recorded by Yengoyan (Findling & Pelle 1990), Badger's receipts and costs imply the same figure (Badger 1979), and Allwood's table gives it too (Allwood et al. 2001).
  • The Garden Palace — its design, materials, construction and fire are described differently by Orr (Findling & Pelle 2008), Yengoyan (Findling & Pelle 1990), Allwood (Allwood et al. 2001) and Hoffenberg, who adds the carpenters' strike (Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Agriculture against industry — Yengoyan stresses agriculture and livestock (Findling & Pelle 1990), and Allwood contrasts Sydney with Melbourne's manufacturing focus (Allwood et al. 2001).

Questions for study

  1. Orr credits the Royal Agricultural Society and the governor with the fair, while Allwood credits Joubert. What does this disagreement suggest about who controlled a colonial exhibition? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Allwood et al. 2001)
  2. The texts disagree on whether the Garden Palace's dome was timber or masonry. Does the answer change how we read the fire of 1882 and the sense of loss that Orr describes? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Findling & Pelle 1990) (Allwood et al. 2001)
  3. Yengoyan records a loss of £103,615 but says the money spent in Sydney compensated for it. Should a fair that lost money be judged a success, and by whose measure? (Findling & Pelle 1990)
  4. Hoffenberg says Aboriginal people were admitted free to the fair, yet presents them as a prehistoric past. How did the Ethnological Court differ from the Maori and Fijian performances, and why might that matter? (Hoffenberg 2001)
  5. Orr reports that no Aboriginal items were exhibited, while Hoffenberg describes Aboriginal costumes and specimens in the Ethnological Court. How would a historian check which account is right? (Findling & Pelle 2008) (Hoffenberg 2001)

Thomas, Nicholas. Entangled Objects: Exchange, Material Culture, and Colonialism in the Pacific. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1991.
chapter 'The European Appropriation of Indigenous Things', pp. 175-76; note 109, p. 247

Thomas uses the Fijian displays at the Sydney (1879) and Melbourne (1880) exhibitions to show how colonial exhibitions made a colony visible. Most of the Fijian exhibit was European goods such as timber samples, cotton seed and cigars, with smaller space for Fijian cloth, mats, bowls, pottery and fishing nets. Thomas argues that expositions of this type, with dwellings, fake streets and live specimen natives, made an entire colonial world visible and tangible.
