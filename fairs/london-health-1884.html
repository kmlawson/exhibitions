International Health Exhibition (London)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The International Health Exhibition was held at South Kensington, London, in 1884, the second of four annual specialist shows on that site between 1883 and 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 206; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Directed by Cunliffe-Owen, it recorded 4,153,390 visits (Filipová 2015p. 206; Filipová 2015p. 221). Its reconstructed street of pre-Fire London, Old London, is treated as the prototype of the historic city exhibit (Filipová 2015p. 207). The show also housed an anthropometric laboratory, where Galton measured the strength of nearly 10,000 people (Qureshi 2011p. 257). Historians use it to study how exhibitions mixed instruction, commerce and entertainment (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Origins and aims

Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protégé and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, turned an unassuming Fisheries Exhibition of 1883 into a fashionable success by broadening its scope and adding outdoor attractions (Filipová 2015p. 206; Filipová 2015p. 206). The Prince of Wales then backed three further annual events under Cunliffe-Owen's direction: Health in 1884, Inventions in 1885 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 206). The earlier 1871-74 series had failed to excite the public (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Greenhalgh groups the four shows as giant conferences or trade fairs, the Colonial and Indian show excepted, and says they aimed to examine commerce and industry, with entrance profits second to national economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Greenhalgh adds that fine art featured only incidentally in the series (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

Organisation and finance

Cunliffe-Owen was executive commissioner for the South Kensington shows of the mid-1880s, the Health and Colonial and Indian Exhibitions among them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 39). The Royal Society of Arts supported the show. It loaned jurors, offered prizes, and provided a reading room and library with books on education, public health and work-related injuries (Hoffenberg 2001p. 89).

Old London had separate money. The City of London Corporation and several livery companies, which Wilson Smith describes as former craft guilds that had become wealthy charitable foundations, financed it outside the official South Kensington administration (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209). George Shaw, Master of the Plumbers' Company, was its moving spirit (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Wilson Smith's notes give 4,153,390 visits and a profit of £15,580 for 1884, and a loss of £18,089 for the 1885 Inventions show (Filipová 2015p. 221). Hoffenberg also puts attendance at over four million (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115).

Old London and the historic city

Old London was a street of re-creations of some 25 historic buildings, long since demolished, grouped to represent London before the Great Fire of 1666 (Filipová 2015p. 203). Its designer, George H. Birch, was an antiquarian and an architect who specialised in church restoration (Filipová 2015p. 205). Birch insisted on realism: the houses were "realistic, in that these houses are no pasteboard and painted canvas delusions, but honest structures" (Filipová 2015p. 205). Surfaces were distressed and lichen and moss were added, so that the buildings seemed to have survived undisturbed (Filipová 2015p. 205; Filipová 2015p. 206). Whittington's Palace was the centrepiece (Filipová 2015p. 205).

Geppert places the street at the start of a genre. Domestic historical sections, Geppert says, were first introduced in a British context at this show, and the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition repeated the idea with an authentic old London street under the same architect and curator, Birch (Geppert 2010p. 126; Geppert 2010p. 126). By 1908 such villages were standard (Geppert 2010p. 126). Wilson Smith calls Old London the prototype of the historic city exhibit, later followed by Old Edinburgh in 1886 and by exhibits in Manchester, Antwerp, Berlin, Stockholm and Paris (Filipová 2015p. 207; Filipová 2015p. 207).

Visitors enjoyed the illusion. Old London was predicted to be one of the hits of the show, and visitors and commentators revelled in its immersive quality (Filipová 2015p. 206). The craftsmen in historic costume were uncoordinated in their dress and performance (Filipová 2015p. 216). Old London operated under South Kensington's ban on over-the-counter sales (Filipová 2015p. 217).

Hoffenberg stresses a different side. The street offered an aesthetic contrast with modern structures and reminded observers of the insanitary dwellings of the past, so the domestic past was not always a source of nostalgia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 162; Hoffenberg 2001p. 163).

Exhibits and attractions

Beyond the street, the show offered machinery and evening entertainment. Sir Arthur Blyth described the excitement of the machinery court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 166). Journalists praised the reading room (Hoffenberg 2001p. 249). Hoffenberg says the Fisheries, Health and Colonial and Indian shows were open at night, with shops, lit gardens and concerts, and invited visitors to take part (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).

Galton's anthropometric laboratory, which measured bodily strength and other faculties, tested nearly 10,000 people at the show (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). The Dunedin fair of 1889-90 had an anthropometrical laboratory modelled on the London one (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

Nations and colonies on show

China, India and Ceylon all had a place at the show, and the Asian displays are set out in the ASIA section. A prominent South Australian visitor found the Chinese village interesting (Hoffenberg 2001). A report on the show's education section was addressed to the Queensland government (Hoffenberg 2001p. 335). None of the passages gathered for this show describes displays of colonial peoples. The performers in Qureshi's account were at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 257).

Visitors and reception

Commentators welcomed Old London and revelled in its illusion (Filipová 2015p. 206). Wilson Smith argues that the exhibit was meant to provoke pleasure rather than disgust (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Politics and controversy

Old London had a political purpose. The wealth and privileges of the Livery Companies were under scrutiny, and a Royal Commission had been investigating them since 1882 (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209). Its report appeared in May 1884, while the show was open (Filipová 2015p. 209). Shaw's stated aim was to impress the value of the guilds on the public, against modern reformers (Filipová 2015p. 210). The commentator George Augustus Sala treated Old London in the Illustrated London News as a reply in advance to the Commission's report (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Wilson Smith sees a wider split. The South Kensington shows were polarising between practical instruction and public entertainment, and Old London sat on the side of entertainment. The Builder called the exhibit "[T]he erection is, in fact, a most charming and costly toy" (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209).

How historians have interpreted it

Wilson Smith reads Old London as backward-looking inside an exhibition built on progress, offering a pleasurable immersion in the past (Filipová 2015p. 218). Smith argues that the South Kensington ethos confined the show's historic street to education, so that commercial popularisation brought the bazaar into it (Filipová 2015p. 219).

Geppert's reading concerns the genre. The 1884 show introduced the domestic historical section to British exhibitions, and Geppert's 1908 chapter reads Old London and the Irish village as different national pasts set against ethnic displays (Geppert 2010p. 126; Geppert 2010p. 128). In a note, Geppert says that only the Colonial and Indian show of the four South Kensington shows has drawn some, if limited, historiographical interest (Geppert 2010p. 305).

Hoffenberg places the show in a wider network where commissioners, producers and visitors shaped the empire through their interactions (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg stresses the street's sanitary contrast and its limits as nostalgia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 163). Smith, by contrast, reads the street mainly as entertainment and a political claim by the Livery Companies (Filipová 2015p. 209). The two readings differ over what the sanitary theme was meant to do.

Rydell reads the show's anthropometric exhibits as early eugenic work in the use of fairs (Rydell 1993p. 39). Qureshi notes that the same apparatus was adapted from fairground strength machines (Qureshi 2011p. 257). Greenhalgh reads the series as trade conferences rather than cultural displays (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Hur reads China's presence at the exhibitions generally as partly favourable, and cites Campbell's view of the London shows as part of that favourable reception (Hurp. 17; Hurp. 17).

The authors agree that the show mixed education with amusement and commerce (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250; Filipová 2015p. 209). They differ on its purpose: Smith stresses pleasure and politics, Greenhalgh trade, and Rydell the transmission of scientific racism. The reference works record only the year, place and listing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

China

The Chinese presence is the best documented Asian display. A prominent South Australian visitor found the Chinese village interesting, with its tea houses and music, but judged the Chinese songs odd and uncouth (Hoffenberg 2001). The Chinese Court was completed with soapstone wares, clay figures and toys, grains, merchants and commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg's note on the court cites an illustrated catalogue of the Chinese collection, published in the Imperial Maritime Customs series (Hoffenberg 2001p. 289).

Hur says China took part in the Fisheries (1883), Health (1884) and Inventions (1885) shows in London (Hurp. 17). Hur describes the Customs service as a European-run department of the Chinese government (Hurp. 17), and ties China's London displays to the London office of the Imperial Maritime Customs, through its non-resident secretary Campbell (Hurp. 17). Campbell thought the three shows did much to remove English ignorance of China (Hurp. 17). Hur does not say who paid for the Chinese displays at this show. Hur sets this favourable reading against more sinister presentations of China, but the examples come from Paris in 1867 and the 1885 Inventions show, not from this fair (Hurp. 17).

India and Ceylon

The most detailed Asian presence is the Indian tea display. Hoffenberg says the Indian tea association saw the show, which drew over four million visitors, as a chance to stimulate popular demand for the teas of India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). An assistant was hired to sell tea samples directly to merchants and tourists (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). The association's staff built "a miniature tea garden" where visitors could sample tea and see it prepared (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). Costumed staff served Indian and Ceylon teas and coffees (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115).

Hoffenberg says the association's members paid subsidies, and that sales repaid them: over 150,000 cups of tea at 3p a cup were sold, and the tea exhibits won six medals (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). The tea garden, Hoffenberg notes, offered "the truly Eastern luxury of a punkah" in contrast with the hot refreshment rooms elsewhere at South Kensington (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). A guidebook concluded that concerted action by the principal Indian planters would win British consumers for their teas (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). The success led to a larger Tea Court for the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).

Japan

Komatsu's chronology lists a 1884 London Hygiene Exhibition among international exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 77). The text does not name the International Health Exhibition, so the link to this show is an inference. Komatsu says donated materials to the Educational Museum in Japan increased after the Philadelphia, Paris and London hygiene exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 78), and that most of the materials were obtained at international exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 78). The texts do not say that Japan showed anything at the London show.

Southeast Asia and Asian visitors

The texts say nothing about a Southeast Asian presence at this show. None of the passages names an Asian visitor, commissioner or observer at the 1884 show.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Historic city exhibits — Old London was a domestic historical street at the 1884 show and the prototype of a genre that later exhibitions repeated (Filipová 2015p. 207; Geppert 2010p. 126).
  • Livery Companies and civic politics — the Corporation and the guilds funded Old London separately, and it doubled as a political defence of their privileges while a Royal Commission was investigating them (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209).
  • Entertainment and commerce — the show combined instruction with amusement, souvenirs, tea sales and evening attractions (Filipová 2015p. 209; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).
  • Measurement of the body — Galton's laboratory tested nearly 10,000 people, and a copy was used at Dunedin in 1889-90 (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Rydell 1993p. 39).
  • Tea and imperial trade — Indian and Ceylon tea was sold at the show, and its success led to a larger court in 1886 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).
  • China and display — China took part in the London shows, and Hur weighs favourable reception against more sinister presentations (Hurp. 17; Hurp. 17; Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Sanitation and nostalgia — Hoffenberg reads Old London's sanitary contrast as a limit on nostalgia, while Smith reads its aim as pleasure (Hoffenberg 2001p. 163; Filipová 2015p. 209).

Questions for study

  1. Old London was shown at a health exhibition, but the texts disagree about its sanitary message. What did the show want visitors to take from the historic street? (Filipová 2015p. 209; Hoffenberg 2001p. 163)
  2. How far can a show paid for by the Livery Companies be counted as public education? (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209)
  3. Were the tea sales and souvenirs part of what the show taught, or a commercial sideline? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Filipová 2015p. 217)
  4. What did it mean to measure visitors' bodies at an exhibition, and who used those measurements? (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Rydell 1993p. 39)
  5. Why have historians paid more attention to the Colonial and Indian show than to the other South Kensington shows? (Geppert 2010p. 305)
  6. How did visitors come to understand China from the Chinese village and court, and who decided what they saw? (Hoffenberg 2001; Hurp. 17)

Wilson Smith's chapter in this edited volume takes Old London, a reconstructed street of pre-Fire London built for the 1884 Health Exhibition, as the first 'historic city' exhibit, and compares it with Old Edinburgh of 1886. Smith argues that the historic city looked backwards inside an exhibition built on progress, and that its claim to authenticity sat uneasily with the commerce that grew up around it. The chapter also presents Old London as a political project: the City of London Corporation and the Livery Companies paid for it to defend their privileges while a Royal Commission was investigating them. The introduction summarises the chapter as an attempt to highlight the modern character of the contemporary city through a historic town.

On the web

Wikipedia

The International Health Exhibition was one of a series of international exhibitions held in the Royal Horticultural Society Garden in South Kensington, London, in the 1880s under the patronage of Queen Victoria and the Prince of Wales. Four million people visited the 1884 exhibition. The exhibition's motto was "From labour health, from health contentment springs".
