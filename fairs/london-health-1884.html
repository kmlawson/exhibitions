London · 1884

International Health Exhibition (London)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Health Exhibition was held at South Kensington, London, in 1884, the second of four annual specialist shows on that site between 1883 and 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 206; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Directed by Cunliffe-Owen, it recorded 4,153,390 visits (Filipová 2015p. 206; Filipová 2015p. 221). Its reconstructed street of pre-Fire London, Old London, is treated as the prototype of the historic city exhibit (Filipová 2015p. 207). The show also housed an anthropometric laboratory, where Galton measured the strength of nearly 10,000 people (Qureshi 2011p. 257). Historians use it to study how exhibitions mixed instruction, commerce and entertainment (Filipová 2015p. 209).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protégé and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, turned an unassuming Fisheries Exhibition of 1883 into a fashionable success by broadening its scope and adding outdoor attractions (Filipová 2015p. 206; Filipová 2015p. 206). The Prince of Wales then backed three further annual events under Cunliffe-Owen's direction: Health in 1884, Inventions in 1885 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 206). The earlier 1871-74 series had failed to excite the public (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Greenhalgh groups the four shows as giant conferences or trade fairs, the Colonial and Indian show excepted, and says they aimed to examine commerce and industry, with entrance profits second to national economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Greenhalgh adds that fine art featured only incidentally in the series (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

Organisation and finance

Cunliffe-Owen was executive commissioner for the South Kensington shows of the mid-1880s, the Health and Colonial and Indian Exhibitions among them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 39). The Royal Society of Arts supported the show. It loaned jurors, offered prizes, and provided a reading room and library with books on education, public health and work-related injuries (Hoffenberg 2001p. 89).

Old London had separate money. The City of London Corporation and several livery companies, which Wilson Smith describes as former craft guilds that had become wealthy charitable foundations, financed it outside the official South Kensington administration (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209). George Shaw, Master of the Plumbers' Company, was its moving spirit (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Wilson Smith's notes give 4,153,390 visits and a profit of £15,580 for 1884, and a loss of £18,089 for the 1885 Inventions show (Filipová 2015p. 221). Hoffenberg also puts attendance at over four million (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115).

Old London and the historic city

Old London was a street of re-creations of some 25 historic buildings, long since demolished, grouped to represent London before the Great Fire of 1666 (Filipová 2015p. 203). Its designer, George H. Birch, was an antiquarian and an architect who specialised in church restoration (Filipová 2015p. 205). Birch insisted on realism: the houses were "realistic, in that these houses are no pasteboard and painted canvas delusions, but honest structures" (Filipová 2015p. 205). Surfaces were distressed and lichen and moss were added, so that the buildings seemed to have survived undisturbed (Filipová 2015p. 205; Filipová 2015p. 206). Whittington's Palace was the centrepiece (Filipová 2015p. 205).

Geppert places the street at the start of a genre. Domestic historical sections, Geppert says, were first introduced in a British context at this show, and the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition repeated the idea with an authentic old London street under the same architect and curator, Birch (Geppert 2010p. 126; Geppert 2010p. 126). By 1908 such villages were standard (Geppert 2010p. 126). Wilson Smith calls Old London the prototype of the historic city exhibit, later followed by Old Edinburgh in 1886 and by exhibits in Manchester, Antwerp, Berlin, Stockholm and Paris (Filipová 2015p. 207; Filipová 2015p. 207).

Visitors enjoyed the illusion. Old London was predicted to be one of the hits of the show, and visitors and commentators revelled in its immersive quality (Filipová 2015p. 206). The craftsmen in historic costume were uncoordinated in their dress and performance (Filipová 2015p. 216). Old London operated under South Kensington's ban on over-the-counter sales (Filipová 2015p. 217).

Hoffenberg stresses a different side. The street offered an aesthetic contrast with modern structures and reminded observers of the insanitary dwellings of the past, so the domestic past was not always a source of nostalgia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 162; Hoffenberg 2001p. 163).

Exhibits and attractions

Beyond the street, the show offered machinery and evening entertainment. Sir Arthur Blyth described the excitement of the machinery court (Hoffenberg 2001p. 166). Journalists praised the reading room (Hoffenberg 2001p. 249). Hoffenberg says the Fisheries, Health and Colonial and Indian shows were open at night, with shops, lit gardens and concerts, and invited visitors to take part (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).

Galton's anthropometric laboratory, which measured bodily strength and other faculties, tested nearly 10,000 people at the show (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). The Dunedin fair of 1889-90 had an anthropometrical laboratory modelled on the London one (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

At the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, anthropologists had a performer from the Cape Colony tested on the strength apparatus that Galton had used at the 1884 laboratory (Qureshi 2011p. 257). The performer, identified only as a Bushman, was weighed and his height taken before he squeezed and pulled the machine (Qureshi 2011p. 257). Galton concluded that he was barely of the average strength of an Englishman, even allowing for his small weight (Qureshi 2011p. 257).

Nations and colonies on show

China, India and Ceylon all had a place at the show, and the Asian displays are set out in the ASIA section. A prominent South Australian visitor found the Chinese village interesting (Hoffenberg 2001). A report on the show's education section was addressed to the Queensland government (Hoffenberg 2001p. 335). None of the passages gathered for this show describes displays of colonial peoples. The performers in Qureshi's account were at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 257).

Visitors and reception

Commentators welcomed Old London and revelled in its illusion (Filipová 2015p. 206). Wilson Smith argues that the exhibit was meant to provoke pleasure rather than disgust (Filipová 2015p. 209).

A Melbourne visitor's entry in the visitors' book at the 1887 Adelaide Jubilee International Exhibition says he enjoyed that show more than the Colonial and Indian, Fisheries and Health exhibitions and others (Hoffenberg 2001p. 251). Colonial visitors followed exhibitions around the world, often on Thomas Cook's organised tours with special excursion trains (Hoffenberg 2001p. 251).

Politics and controversy

Old London had a political purpose. The wealth and privileges of the Livery Companies were under scrutiny, and a Royal Commission had been investigating them since 1882 (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209). Its report appeared in May 1884, while the show was open (Filipová 2015p. 209). Shaw's stated aim was to impress the value of the guilds on the public, against modern reformers (Filipová 2015p. 210). The commentator George Augustus Sala treated Old London in the Illustrated London News as a reply in advance to the Commission's report (Filipová 2015p. 209).

Wilson Smith sees a wider split. The South Kensington shows were polarising between practical instruction and public entertainment, and Old London sat on the side of entertainment. The Builder called the exhibit "[T]he erection is, in fact, a most charming and costly toy" (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209).

How Old London was planned and paid for

Birch first contemplated a complete street, a section of historical Cheapside (Filipová 2015p. 205). Lacking source material accurate enough for that, he assembled separate reproductions of lost buildings into one townscape (Filipová 2015p. 205). Smith cites a letter from Birch to George Shaw of 18 February 1884, held in the London Metropolitan Archives (Filipová 2015p. 220). The total cost was reported as £14,853 on 7 October 1884 (Filipová 2015p. 220). The second edition of Shaw's Revived Guild Action (1889) asserts his responsibility for the exhibit (Filipová 2015p. 221).

Craft demonstrations and costume

Nominated firms demonstrated craft skills in Old London's workshops, while its rooms showed objects from private collections and from the Livery Companies' resources (Filipová 2015p. 209). Smith says that costumed craft work was by its nature an exclusively male affair (Filipová 2015p. 217).

Old London after 1884

Old London was kept for the Inventions Exhibition of 1885. A few exhibitors from 1884 stayed there and were joined by industrial products, modern crafts and curios, to set ancient industries against modern ones (Filipová 2015p. 210). That year Mrs Ernest Hart's Donegal Industrial Fund put female textile workers in peasant dress into the street, a rural scene placed inside an urban re-creation (Filipová 2015p. 217). In 1886 the organisers made no connection with the subject at all, and the street was used as office accommodation (Filipová 2015p. 210).

Who ran the fair

Henry Wood, who held posts at the Royal Society of Arts from 1879 to 1917, was involved in organising the South Kensington exhibitions of 1883 to 1886, especially the 1884 Healtheries (Geppert 2010p. 279). Hoffenberg adds that Cunliffe-Owen's other posts as England's executive commissioner took him to Paris, Vienna and Philadelphia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 39).

Art and labels

Greenhalgh says painting and sculpture in the four South Kensington shows were placed wherever they could be found conceptually to fit (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). At the 1883 Fisheries show there were seascapes and art related to fish (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). At the 1886 show, where no Western art was shown, Indian painting and sculpture were called fine art, a label international exhibitions normally kept for Western nations (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). Greenhalgh notes that African art never received the label, and argues that the labels ranked peoples as well as art (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

How historians have interpreted it

Wilson Smith reads Old London as backward-looking inside an exhibition built on progress, offering a pleasurable immersion in the past (Filipová 2015p. 218). Smith argues that the South Kensington ethos confined the show's historic street to education, so that commercial popularisation brought the bazaar into it (Filipová 2015p. 219).

Geppert's reading concerns the genre. The 1884 show introduced the domestic historical section to British exhibitions, and Geppert's 1908 chapter reads Old London and the Irish village as different national pasts set against ethnic displays (Geppert 2010p. 126; Geppert 2010p. 128). In a note, Geppert says that only the Colonial and Indian show of the four South Kensington shows has drawn some, if limited, historiographical interest (Geppert 2010p. 305).

Hoffenberg places the show in a wider network where commissioners, producers and visitors shaped the empire through their interactions (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg stresses the street's sanitary contrast and its limits as nostalgia (Hoffenberg 2001p. 163). Smith, by contrast, reads the street mainly as entertainment and a political claim by the Livery Companies (Filipová 2015p. 209). The two readings differ over what the sanitary theme was meant to do.

Rydell reads the show's anthropometric exhibits as early eugenic work in the use of fairs (Rydell 1993p. 39). Qureshi notes that the same apparatus was adapted from fairground strength machines (Qureshi 2011p. 257). Greenhalgh reads the series as trade conferences rather than cultural displays (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Hur reads China's presence at the exhibitions generally as partly favourable, and cites Campbell's view of the London shows as part of that favourable reception (Hurp. 17; Hurp. 17).

The authors agree that the show mixed education with amusement and commerce (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250; Filipová 2015p. 209). They differ on its purpose: Smith stresses pleasure and politics, Greenhalgh trade, and Rydell the transmission of scientific racism. The reference works record only the year, place and listing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

China

The Chinese presence is the best documented Asian display. A prominent South Australian visitor found the Chinese village interesting, with its tea houses and music, but judged the Chinese songs odd and uncouth (Hoffenberg 2001). The Chinese Court was completed with soapstone wares, clay figures and toys, grains, merchants and commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001). Hoffenberg's note on the court cites an illustrated catalogue of the Chinese collection, published in the Imperial Maritime Customs series (Hoffenberg 2001p. 289).

Hur says China took part in the Fisheries (1883), Health (1884) and Inventions (1885) shows in London (Hurp. 17). Hur describes the Customs service as a European-run department of the Chinese government (Hurp. 17), and ties China's London displays to the London office of the Imperial Maritime Customs, through its non-resident secretary Campbell (Hurp. 17). Campbell thought the three shows did much to remove English ignorance of China (Hurp. 17). Hur does not say who paid for the Chinese displays at this show. Hur sets this favourable reading against more sinister presentations of China, but the examples come from Paris in 1867 and the 1885 Inventions show, not from this fair (Hurp. 17).

India and Ceylon

The most detailed Asian presence is the Indian tea display. Hoffenberg says the Indian tea association saw the show, which drew over four million visitors, as a chance to stimulate popular demand for the teas of India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). An assistant was hired to sell tea samples directly to merchants and tourists (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). The association's staff built "a miniature tea garden" where visitors could sample tea and see it prepared (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). Costumed staff served Indian and Ceylon teas and coffees (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115).

Hoffenberg says the association's members paid subsidies, and that sales repaid them: over 150,000 cups of tea at 3p a cup were sold, and the tea exhibits won six medals (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). The tea garden, Hoffenberg notes, offered "the truly Eastern luxury of a punkah" in contrast with the hot refreshment rooms elsewhere at South Kensington (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). A guidebook concluded that concerted action by the principal Indian planters would win British consumers for their teas (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116). The success led to a larger Tea Court for the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).

Japan

Komatsu's chronology lists a 1884 London Hygiene Exhibition among international exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 77). The text does not name the International Health Exhibition, so the link to this show is an inference. Komatsu says donated materials to the Educational Museum in Japan increased after the Philadelphia, Paris and London hygiene exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 78), and that most of the materials were obtained at international exhibitions (Lawn 2009p. 78). The texts do not say that Japan showed anything at the London show.

Southeast Asia and Asian visitors

The texts say nothing about a Southeast Asian presence at this show. None of the passages names an Asian visitor, commissioner or observer at the 1884 show.

Tea against Chinese tea

Tea planters shared the cotton industry's worry about an impression of impurities in Indian exports (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). Hoffenberg says the Indian tea display set machine-processed Indian tea against hand-processed Chinese tea, which the display described as distinctly dirty (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). The commissioners had also contracted English firms to compete with Chinese teas in the London market (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). Hoffenberg places the tea garden and Indian village at South Kensington in the same passage as the 1884 show, so this contrast is a South Kensington display whose link to this fair rests on that placement (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Historic city exhibits — Old London was a domestic historical street at the 1884 show and the prototype of a genre that later exhibitions repeated (Filipová 2015p. 207; Geppert 2010p. 126).
  • Livery Companies and civic politics — the Corporation and the guilds funded Old London separately, and it doubled as a political defence of their privileges while a Royal Commission was investigating them (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209).
  • Entertainment and commerce — the show combined instruction with amusement, souvenirs, tea sales and evening attractions (Filipová 2015p. 209; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).
  • Measurement of the body — Galton's laboratory tested nearly 10,000 people, and a copy was used at Dunedin in 1889-90 (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Rydell 1993p. 39).
  • Tea and imperial trade — Indian and Ceylon tea was sold at the show, and its success led to a larger court in 1886 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).
  • China and display — China took part in the London shows, and Hur weighs favourable reception against more sinister presentations (Hurp. 17; Hurp. 17; Hoffenberg 2001).
  • Sanitation and nostalgia — Hoffenberg reads Old London's sanitary contrast as a limit on nostalgia, while Smith reads its aim as pleasure (Hoffenberg 2001p. 163; Filipová 2015p. 209).

Questions for study

  1. Old London was shown at a health exhibition, but the texts disagree about its sanitary message. What did the show want visitors to take from the historic street? (Filipová 2015p. 209; Hoffenberg 2001p. 163)
  2. How far can a show paid for by the Livery Companies be counted as public education? (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209)
  3. Were the tea sales and souvenirs part of what the show taught, or a commercial sideline? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Filipová 2015p. 217)
  4. What did it mean to measure visitors' bodies at an exhibition, and who used those measurements? (Qureshi 2011p. 257; Rydell 1993p. 39)
  5. Why have historians paid more attention to the Colonial and Indian show than to the other South Kensington shows? (Geppert 2010p. 305)
  6. How did visitors come to understand China from the Chinese village and court, and who decided what they saw? (Hoffenberg 2001; Hurp. 17)
  7. Who paid for the craft demonstrations and costumes in Old London, and did the Livery Companies' sponsorship decide who could perform in the street? (Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 209; Filipová 2015p. 217)
  8. Did the Indian tea display's contrast with hand-processed Chinese tea shape what visitors believed about China, given that Hur also reads China's London displays as favourable? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115; Hurp. 17)
  9. Why did Old London survive the South Kensington series after its subject had stopped fitting the show? (Filipová 2015p. 210; Filipová 2015p. 210)

Chapters that treat it at length 2 in 2 works

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
9Old London, Old Edinburgh: Constructing Historic CitiesWilson Smithp. 203

Smith examines the 'historic city', a reconstruction of a host city's old streets built inside a modern exhibition. He takes two examples: Old London, shown at South Kensington from 1884 to 1886, and Old Edinburgh, at the Edinburgh International Exhibition of 1886. Old London, designed by the antiquarian architect George Birch, reproduced about 25 demolished buildings, aged with distressed finishes, and was funded by the City of London Corporation and the Livery Companies. Smith argues it defended the Livery Companies at a time when their wealth was under attack, and that visitors were asked to suspend disbelief. Old Edinburgh, by Sydney Mitchell, was a self-funding venture: its facades hid rented shops staffed by costumed saleswomen, and its handbook told Scottish history from a Liberal and Presbyterian viewpoint. Smith ends with three paradoxes: the backward-looking attraction inside an exhibition of progress, a site-specific type that nonetheless spread between fairs, and the tension between scholarly authenticity and commerce. The chapter concludes that each exhibit told its host city's own story, and that consuming the past came to mean consumption in the present.

In the contents of this work

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
4Consumers, Producers, and Markets: The Political Economy of Imperial Federationp. 99Asia

Hoffenberg examines how Australian and Indian commissioners, merchants and critics used international exhibitions to argue about the economics of empire. The displays of wool, coal, wine, cotton, silk and tea are read as political economies in their own right rather than neutral reflections of trade. Hoffenberg follows the Australian and Indian commodity courts from the Great Exhibition of 1851 through the London International Exhibition of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, with further material from Sydney in 1879 and Calcutta in 1883. Tea provides the chapter's clearest case: the Indian Tea Districts Association sold samples in London and then in Australia and North America, and the chapter traces how James Inglis and Jules Joubert encouraged direct trade between Australia and India without English middlemen. The argument is that colonial producers pursued independent commerce within an imperial framework, and that critics such as Carlyle and the radical press rejected the market ideals the shows celebrated. The chapter ends by noting that the displays both reinforced imperial economic unity and exposed its strains, which opens onto the question of colonial trade policy.

ch. 9, 'Old London, Old Edinburgh: Constructing Historic Cities' by Wilson Smith, pp. 205-211, 216-219; book introduction, l. 1140-1150 and 1650-1652

Wilson Smith's chapter in this edited volume takes Old London, a reconstructed street of pre-Fire London built for the 1884 Health Exhibition, as the first 'historic city' exhibit, and compares it with Old Edinburgh of 1886. Smith argues that the historic city looked backwards inside an exhibition built on progress, and that its claim to authenticity sat uneasily with the commerce that grew up around it. The chapter also presents Old London as a political project: the City of London Corporation and the Livery Companies paid for it to defend their privileges while a Royal Commission was investigating them. The introduction summarises the chapter as an attempt to highlight the modern character of the contemporary city through a historic town.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Moderate discussion 2

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
ch. 4, section 'London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition', pp. 126-127 (l. 5843-5849); note 4, p. 305 (l. 13452-13458)

Geppert treats the Health show mainly as the starting point of a genre. In his chapter on the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition he says the 1884 Health show first introduced domestic historical sections of this kind to British exhibitions, and that the 1886 Colonial and Indian show repeated the idea with an authentic old London street by the same architect and curator, George H. Birch. By 1908 such villages were standard, and Geppert reads the 1908 Old London beside the Irish village as different national pasts set against 'primitive' displays. In a note he observes that the four South Kensington shows of 1883-86 have had little historical attention, with only the Colonial and Indian show drawing some limited interest.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. The International Health Exhibition was one of a series of international exhibitions held in the Royal Horticultural Society Garden in South Kensington, London, in the 1880s under the patronage of Queen Victoria and the Prince of Wales. Four million people visited the 1884 exhibition. The exhibition's motto was "From labour health, from health contentment springs".
