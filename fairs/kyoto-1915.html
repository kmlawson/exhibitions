The Enthronement Commemoration Exposition (Taiten kinen Kyoto hakurankai) was held in Kyoto in 1915 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The texts describe only one part of it, a Manchuria pavilion sponsored by the South Manchuria Railway Company, Mantetsu, and the office of the Kwantung Governor-General (Kantō totokufu) (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). Its Qing-derived architecture matters because the Chicago Manchuria pavilion of 1933-34, which avoided such forms, can be read against it (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539p. 546p. 547).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Sponsors and setting

Kari Shepherdson-Scott's article on the Japanese Manchuria pavilion at Chicago is the only text in the collection that treats this exhibition, and it does so in a few sentences (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539p. 546). The Manchuria pavilion was sponsored by Mantetsu, whose full name is Minami Manshū testudō kabushikikaisha (Shepherdson-Scottp. 541), and by the office of the Kwantung Governor-General (Kantō totokufu) (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The texts do not name the organisers of the exposition as a whole, its opening and closing dates, its visitors, or the occasion it commemorated.

The Manchuria pavilion

Moriya Nobuo designed the pavilion (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). It had elaborate dragon roof-ridge ornaments, a complex bracketing system and large stone lions at the entrance (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The author says its design was strongly reminiscent of the towering gates of the Beiling, the Northern Mausoleum of the Qing Emperor Huang Taiji outside Shenyang, also called Fengtian and Mukden (Shepherdson-Scottp. 547). The author argues that this fitted a pavilion meant to speak to Manchurian culture and customs (Shepherdson-Scottp. 547).

Japanese designers had earlier used continental motifs for Manchuria pavilions, acknowledging the long connection between Han Chinese and Manchu cultures (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The Chicago pavilion of 1933-34 avoided overt continental references. The author argues that this helped dissociate Manchuria from China and left room for its cultural and political realignment with Japan (Shepherdson-Scottp. 539p. 547).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Continental architecture — the Kyoto pavilion borrowed the forms of Qing imperial tombs, which the author reads as suited to a pavilion about Manchurian culture (Shepherdson-Scottp. 547)
  • Corporate and official sponsorship — Mantetsu and the Kwantung Governor-General's office sponsored the pavilion, so a railway company and a colonial administrative office shaped how Manchuria was shown (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546)
  • Distance from China — the author treats architectural style as a political choice about Manchuria's relation to China, shown by the 1933 Chicago pavilion's avoidance of continental forms (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546p. 547)

Questions for study

  1. Why did the sponsors choose Qing tomb architecture for a Manchurian pavilion, and what did that choice say about Manchuria's relation to China? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 547)
  2. Why did the Chicago pavilion of 1933 drop the continental references that the Kyoto pavilion had used? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546p. 547)
  3. What did it mean for a railway company and a colonial administrative office to co-sponsor a pavilion at a Japanese exhibition? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546)
  4. Which sources would show the visitors, programme and opening dates of the Kyoto exposition, which the texts in this collection do not give? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546)

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
PrefaceBurton Benedictp. viiAsia

Benedict describes how the book began: the Lowie Museum at Berkeley held Japanese mannequins, Swedish dolls and a model of a Chinese temple from the Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915, and he was invited to curate a show from them. He recounts his own childhood visits to fairs and how he and his colleagues gathered images, postcards, a tower of glass jewels and souvenirs from the 1915 exhibition. He ends by noting that oral histories and a boy's diaries gave the research a perspective that documents could not.

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Panama-Pacific International Exposition, San Francisco, 1915: East Meets West in the Gardenp. 133Asia

This chapter examines the landscape of the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco, which it presents as a fair that joined Eastern and Western garden traditions on land made from the bay. It explains how the Harbor View site was created by dredging, and how Edward Bennett's block plan of courtyards was undermined when he resigned in 1912 after accusing the city's Department of Works of pernicious influence. Most of the chapter concerns John McLaren, the local landscape designer whose plantings are described in detail: the ice plant hedge that imitated an aged wall, the palm and cypress avenues, and the courts that the chapter treats as early small-space gardens. Its other principal cases are the Palace of Fine Arts and its lagoon, the Japanese garden, and the Horticulture Palace, whose exhibits moved from single fruit specimens to commercial processes. The chapter also covers the quarantine inspections that imported plants required. It ends by tracing the fair's legacy through the trees planted in Golden Gate Park and the de Sabla garden at El Cerrito.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

Representations of “Modern Korea” in the 1915 Exhibitionp. 7Asia

Park examines the architecture, layout and display of the 1915 exhibition as a way of representing 'modern Korea'. The chapter draws on the official report, the exhibition guide and visitor routes printed in Maeil shinbo and Keijō nippō, and on posters, photographs and postcards. It shows that the organisers built Western-style pavilions on the east of the Kyŏngbok Palace site, destroying a large part of its structures, and kept older palace halls on the west as small exhibition rooms. Visitors followed a numbered route from the 'modern' pavilions to the 'premodern' or 'old' space. Displays of penal and hygiene systems, provincial development and products were arranged to show Korean backwardness against Japanese progress. Park situates the architecture in the Tokyo Taisho exhibition of 1914 and in Renaissance and Secession styles, and concludes that the buildings served the colonial claim to have made a 'new modern' Korea.

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
1Introductionp. 15Asia

Peterson introduces a study of how independent Asian nations sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Peterson argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves at these fairs rather than being presented by Western powers, and that the story of Asian agency begins in San Francisco in 1915. Drawing on Timothy Mitchell and Greenhalgh, Peterson proposes a move from an exhibitionary order of colonial display to a performative order of live encounters within pavilions. Three modes of performance are distinguished: cultural shows, guides who perform national identity, and the metrics of neoliberal productivity. The case studies cover San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, and the introduction explains the archival and ethnographic methods and their limits. It concludes that state-directed self-representation at expositions has declined as the form became costly for host cities.

2The Master of the Formp. 39Asia

Peterson argues that by 1915 Japan had become the master of the international exhibition format, and that its self-representation at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco was built on staged encounters rather than on objects alone. He starts with diplomacy: the 1912 groundbreaking, the gold 'Loving Cup' and the public performances of Japanese and American officials that presented the two countries as peaceful partners on the Pacific. The first and longest case is the Japanese government site, with its temple-like pavilion and garden, where Peterson reads photographs of kimono-clad women as evidence of how fairgoers performed an emotional attachment to the place. He then turns to the commercial 'Japan Beautiful' concession in the Joy Zone, with its miniature Asakusa-style village, geisha and Mount Fuji tea house, then to the trade halls selling ceramics, lacquer and silk, and to the fine arts galleries, where San Francisco critics praised traditional work and dismissed Western-style painting. The chapter ends by arguing that Japan knew what America wanted to see, a pre-modern and refined nation, and supplied it.

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
8The Expositions in San Francisco and San Diego: Toward the World of TomorrowAsia

Rydell examines the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco and the Panama-California Exposition in San Diego, both of 1915, which celebrated the opening of the Panama Canal. Rydell argues that the two fairs staged a racial history of the American West in which white conquest appeared as natural progress, and that their wealthy promoters used this message to present an orderly, racially ranked future. The chapter reconstructs the rivalry between the two cities and the federal politics that gave San Diego only a narrower recognition. Its central evidence is San Diego's physical anthropology display, assembled with Smithsonian anthropologists, whose busts and skulls ranked peoples within an evolutionary and eugenic framework. Rydell then turns to San Francisco's Race Betterment congress and to the Joy Zone and Isthmus concessions, where Mexican, Samoan, African, Chinese, Japanese and Native American villages were staged as entertainment. The chapter closes with the fairs' legacy in Balboa Park and Forest Lawn cemetery, where the vision outlived the exhibitions.

Shepherdson-Scott, Kari. “Conflicting Politics and Contesting Borders: Exhibiting (Japanese) Manchuria at the Chicago World’s Fair, 1933–34.” Journal article.
SELLING JAPANESE MANCHURIAp. 546Asia

Shepherdson-Scott examines how the pavilion's architecture and displays presented a Japanese-led Manchuria to American visitors. The building shared the wood cladding and green tile roof of the Japan pavilion but dropped its karahafu gables and continental motifs, distancing Manchuria from China in a way that contrasts with the Qing-inspired Manchuria pavilion shown at Kyoto in 1915. Inside, a life-size diorama of a Manchu family, with Chinese calligraphy on the wall, casts Japan as the paternal guide, since few Americans could read the inscription. Paintings of Mukden and Dalian 'before and after' and a large relief map of Manchuria with Jehol inside its borders show development as already completed under Japanese control. Shepherdson-Scott argues that the displays presented Japan as the determined facilitator of Manchurian modernization and claimed Jehol for Manchukuo, leaving little room for American fantasies of development. Mantetsu pamphlets and Kawakami's Open Door arguments back this reading.

Shepherdson-Scott, Kari. “Conflicting Politics and Contesting Borders: Exhibiting (Japanese) Manchuria at the Chicago World’s Fair, 1933–34.” Journal article.
Section “Selling Japanese Manchuria” (pp. 546-547) of the article on the Japanese Manchuria pavilion at Chicago, 1933-34

Shepherdson-Scott’s article on the Manchuria pavilion at Chicago’s 1933-34 world’s fair uses the 1915 Kyoto exhibition only as a comparison. In a few sentences it names the Kyoto Manchuria pavilion, designed by Moriya Nobuo and sponsored by Mantetsu and the Kwantung Governor-General’s office, and describes its Qing-derived forms, drawn from the Beiling tomb gates near Shenyang. The author sets this continental style against the Chicago pavilion, which avoided such references so as to separate Manchuria from China and leave room for its realignment with Japan. The Kyoto material is therefore a background case for the larger argument about Japanese control of Manchuria, not a study of the exhibition itself.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Shepherdson-Scott examines the Manchuria pavilion that the South Manchuria Railway Company and the Manchukuo government sponsored at Chicago's A Century of Progress fair in 1933 and 1934, months after Manchukuo was founded and Japan left the League of Nations. The argument is that this small building, which drew about two percent of fairgoers, was a diplomatic instrument aimed at American opinion, and that Mantetsu let the exhibit present Manchuria as an economic project rather than a political one. The paper also treats the American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol, whose site and authenticity contested the pavilion's maps, which placed the recently occupied province of Jehol inside Manchukuo. It concludes that the fair never sold a single idea of Manchuria.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Manchuria is the subject of the whole article, so East Asia is central, but the coverage is confined to a few states. Japan is the organiser: the government, the Kwantung Army, Mantetsu and the exhibition committees shape the pavilion, which chapter 2 reads against Japan's own Kyoto Manchuria pavilion of 1915 and the Qing-inspired architecture it dropped. Manchukuo is treated as a new state whose sovereignty the exhibit had to perform. China enters as the party whose planned display prompted Japan to participate, and chapter 3 is concentrated on the Golden Temple of Jehol, a replica built in Beijing by Chinese craftsmen and sited beside the China and Japan exhibits. Mongolia appears through Jehol and the Sino-Mongolian culture Bendix claimed to conserve. Korea appears only as a Japanese colony shown on Mantetsu's map, its border with Manchukuo blurred. Southeast Asia is absent. None of its countries, colonies or peoples is named in the text, and it discusses no exhibition held in Southeast Asia. Its Asian comparisons are confined to Japan, China, Korea and Manchuria, and the wider 'Asia' language is used for the Japanese imperial project in Northeast Asia.
