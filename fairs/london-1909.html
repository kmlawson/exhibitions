Imperial International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Imperial International Exhibition was held in 1909 at the White City, Shepherd's Bush, London, the second show that Imre Kiralfy staged on the site after the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92; Geppert 2010p. 346). Kiralfy was its Commissioner-General, and the Duke of Argyll, its honorary president, gave the opening speech (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 40; Greenhalgh 1988p. 19). It reused the 1908 buildings and placed peoples from colonies and other places in villages (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 68; Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). Greenhalgh uses it to show a shift from instruction to mass entertainment (Greenhalgh 1988p. 44).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Organisers and aims

Imre Kiralfy, a Hungarian Jewish emigre, had organised exhibitions in Britain since the 1880s and was the most prominent organiser of the White City shows (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39). Hotta-Lister says that in Edwardian Britain he was regarded as a showman who used patriotic imperial themes for money (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 40).

The Duke of Argyll gave the fair's aims in his opening speech as "amusement without excess and knowledge without fatigue" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 19). The Official Daily Programme said that the pleasure side of the fair would have an international scope equal to its commercial sections, and that many little-known peoples would be shown in miniature villages occupied by natives (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92).

Greenhalgh writes that the full number of villages is not known. He thinks Kiralfy probably booked some from the French, Dutch and Belgian governments and paid for the British empire villages from his company's funds (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). The texts do not say what the performers were paid (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92).

Site and programme

The fair used buildings and features that had been built for the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 68). After that show, the White City held exhibitions every year until the First World War, with broad themes of an imperial or national character (Geppert 2010p. 116; Geppert 2010p. 117). The Franco-British drew more than twice the visitors of any later White City exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 117).

Greenhalgh says the White City shows introduced entertainments modelled on those Kiralfy had seen at Chicago and St Louis (Greenhalgh 1988p. 44). He names a Portuguese Street, part of a display of European holiday resorts in the Machine Hall (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105), and says the Palace of Women's Work was used in 1909 for women's work and for displays that could not fit elsewhere (Greenhalgh 1988p. 187).

Empire and colony

The fair had an imperial theme. Hotta-Lister writes that the 1909 show had an imperial theme (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). Its villages included Dahomeyan, Irish and Scottish ones, and a Kalmuck camp shown as a Russian exhibit (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). Greenhalgh says the Russians brought the Kalmuck camp as part of their imperial display, and that it drew the heaviest crowds (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). Greenhalgh spells the Dahomeyan village's name Dayomeyan in places (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94).

The Dahomeyan village's Guide presented French conquest as bringing order, trade and civilisation. Greenhalgh calls this "a familiar picture" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). The Guide described Dahomey as a self-governing French colony with a railway and plantations (Greenhalgh 1988p. 95).

The Irish village had first appeared at the Franco-British Exhibition in 1908, and the Scottish village joined it at this fair in 1909 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106). Greenhalgh says these villages raised questions about the status of Ireland and Scotland in relation to the colonies (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). He records a Portuguese pavilion at this fair, which he says began a trend for imperial display (Greenhalgh 1988p. 70), and Dutch colonial displays (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71).

The texts do not place a Senegalese village at this fair. Greenhalgh puts Senegalese villages in London in 1908 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94), and Hotta-Lister lists one at the Franco-British Exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). The texts do not say what the organisers hoped the fair would do for empire, trade or settlement.

Visitors and reception

Greenhalgh gives 21 May 1909 as the day of the largest attendance ever on the site, when the Kalmuck camp drew the heaviest numbers (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). The Official Daily Programme answered House of Commons questions about the previous year's native villages with reassurance (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). A commentator said that "Mr. Kiralfy knows his public", and that its public preferred rides and entertainments to instruction (Greenhalgh 1988p. 44). No text gives a visitor total for the fair.

Its place in the series

Hotta-Lister describes the 1909 show as having an imperial theme and the 1911 show a coronation theme (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). The 1910 Japan-British Exhibition reused the features built for 1908 and used for 1909 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 68). Geppert places the later shows in two patterns: imperial or national themes, and the bi-national pattern set in 1908 (Geppert 2010p. 117).

ASIA

The texts name no South, East or Southeast Asian exhibit, performer or commissioner at this fair. Hotta-Lister records one Japanese visitor, Uchigasaki, who saw the Imperial International while in England (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 143).

How historians have interpreted it

Greenhalgh and Hotta-Lister are the two texts that interpret the fair. Greenhalgh reads its villages as part of a wider display of empire, and he calls the Dahomeyan Guide's account of French rule a familiar picture (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). Hotta-Lister places the fair's villages among the common uses of exhibitions by imperial nations, which she describes as a way to show their possessions and power (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 116; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). Harvey and Coté use the fair only as an example: Harvey cites the Duke's words on education and diversion, and Coté lists the fair among exhibitions focused on the colonial world (Harvey 1996p. 190; Cotép. 38).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Human showcases — the villages that drew most attention were the Dahomeyan, Irish, Scottish and a Kalmuck camp, which was a Russian exhibit (Greenhalgh 1988; Hotta-Lister 1999).
  • Empire as spectacle — the 1909 show is described as having an imperial theme, and its Dahomeyan Guide presented French rule as a benefit (Hotta-Lister 1999; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Entertainment and instruction — commentators recognised a shift from education to mass entertainment at the fair (Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Site reuse — the fair used the 1908 buildings and features, and the 1910 show reused them in turn (Hotta-Lister 1999; Geppert 2010).
  • European colonial exhibitors — Portugal, the Netherlands and, probably, the French, Dutch and Belgian governments supplied displays or villages (Greenhalgh 1988).

Questions for study

  1. The Duke of Argyll set education against entertainment as the fair's aims (Greenhalgh 1988p. 19). How did rides and "Flip-flaps" fit those aims (Greenhalgh 1988p. 44)?
  2. Greenhalgh calls the Dahomeyan Guide's picture familiar (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94). What did a fair gain by presenting a conquest as a benefit to the conquered people?
  3. The Senegalese village appears for 1908 but not for 1909 in these texts (Greenhalgh 1988p. 94; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 117). How far can a village list in a single fair be trusted?
  4. The texts say the performers were probably paid but do not say how (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). What would the payment records show?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
4Human showcasesp. 82Asia

Greenhalgh examines the practice of displaying people at international exhibitions between 1889 and 1914, when colonised and other non-Western peoples were housed in reconstructed villages and watched by paying crowds. He sorts the displays into four types (imperial, educational, commercial and ambassadorial) but argues that they overlapped, and that the imperial type was the most numerous and lavish. The chapter works through the Paris 1889 and 1900 native villages, the Jardin d'Acclimatation's peopled exhibits that preceded them, Imre Kiralfy's London spectacles, and the Chicago, Buffalo and St Louis fairs, where Native American, Filipino and Dahomeyan residents were placed within a supposed evolutionary hierarchy. Commercial examples include the Cairo Street and the Indian tea-houses, and the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes, who toured under private entrepreneurs, receive detailed attention. Greenhalgh concludes that the displays did lasting damage by teaching mass audiences that non-European peoples were inferior, and that this harm was never repaired.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Gilbert, James. Perfect Cities: Chicago’s Utopias of 1893. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1991.
7Exit: The Gray Cityp. 209

Gilbert's closing chapter follows Chicago from the burning of the White City in 1894 to the realist and sociological image of a 'gray city' that replaced it. It argues that the fires, the Pullman strike and the depression swept away the fiction of a unified, elite-led city, and that later schemes, from Burnham's 1909 plan to the White City Amusement Park of 1905, either ignored or commercialised the popular culture the Fair had pushed onto the Midway. The evidence includes the fates of Pullman and Moody, Stead's If Christ Came to Chicago, the Hull-House maps and labor museum, the new Field Museum and Museum of Science and Industry, and the novels of Herrick, Dreiser, Sinclair and London, and the University of Chicago sociologists. Gilbert concludes that the city came to be seen as diverse, private and disorganised rather than as a pageant under command, and that the cultural problems posed in 1893 kept returning through twentieth-century American life.

Greenhalgh treats the Imperial International of 1909 as one of the White City shows that kept native villages as a constant feature, and names its Dahomeyan, Irish, Scottish and Kalmuck attractions. He reads the fair's official Guide to the Dahomeyan village as a familiar picture of French conquest bringing order and trade, and he notes that a Portuguese pavilion and Dutch and Belgian displays at the fair belonged to a wider pattern of European imperial display. He also presents the fair as part of the move from education to mass entertainment that marked the White City.

“amusement without excess and knowledge without fatigue” — the Duke of Argyll's opening speech, quoted by the author p. 19
About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Hotta-Lister mentions the 1909 Imperial International chiefly as the earlier White City show that reused the 1908 Franco-British buildings and features, and she uses its colonial villages to show how common such displays were at the White City. Her book is about the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition and does not analyse the 1909 fair itself. She reads colonial displays as occasions when imperial nations displayed their possessions and power.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
note 16 (printed p. 38)

Coté's footnote 16 names the Imperial International Exhibition of 1909 as one of several exhibitions that focused on the colonial world, alongside the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition and the 1893 Chicago fair. The note gives no place or detail for the 1909 fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Harvey, Penelope. Hybrids of Modernity: Anthropology, the Nation State and the Universal Exhibition. London: Routledge, 1996.
note 19 (printed p. 190)

Harvey's footnote 19 uses the Duke of Argyll's opening words for the 1909 fair at the White City as an example of exhibitions that combined education and diversion. It adds no other detail about the fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Harvey's Hybrids of Modernity is a reflexive study of Expo'92, the Seville universal exhibition, written by an anthropologist whose access to the fair was as a journalist with press credentials. Its main argument is that the nation state, the universal exhibition and social anthropology are linked modernist institutions, and that the hybrid forms of late twentieth-century culture do not dissolve them but carry effects their founders did not expect. The scope is one fair in one year, with comparison to earlier exhibitions such as London in 1851 and Osaka in 1970. The chapters take up anthropology's handling of culture and context, the nation state in the national pavilions, technology among multinational exhibitors, and visitors as citizens and consumers, and a conclusion sets representational knowledge against embodied practice.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, and nearly all of it concerns one case: Japan's pavilion at Expo'92. Chapter 2 (Anthropology) describes the Japanese pavilion, a wooden building by Tadao Ando sited opposite Fujitsu's spherical cinema, and says Japan concentrated on tradition rather than technology. Chapter 3 (The nation state) quotes the official guide on Japan's emergence as a technological power at Osaka in 1970, and Chapter 4 (The universal exhibition) notes that Fujitsu's staff denied any special link between Japan and the company. Chapter 5 (Hybrid subjects) reports visitors' disappointment that Japan showed tradition instead of the latest technology. Southeast Asia appears once, in Chapter 2, where an Indonesian procession passes through the site and the author, as a tourist, cannot interpret it. Chapter 3 cites the Expo press dossier's eighteen Asian participating countries, and the conclusion quotes a Swatch executive on Asian production. The book does not discuss Chinese or Korean pavilions, colonial display of Asian peoples, or Asian visitors.

Wikipedia

The Imperial International Exhibition was a world's fair held in White City, London in 1909. The exhibition was opened by the Duke of Argyll on 20 May 1909 and continued for five months before closing in October. This was the second of five exhibitions to be held at London's White City (following the Franco-British Exhibition and preceding the Japan–British Exhibition) and retained many of the 1908 exhibitions but on larger grounds and with additional amusements including an alpine railway. This gave the opportunity to reflect on the achievements of the three members of the recently agreed Triple Entente. As part of this France displayed a Dahomey (now Benin) village; and Russia a Kalmuk camp. Austria, China, Denmark, Italy, the Netherlands and Persia also participated.
