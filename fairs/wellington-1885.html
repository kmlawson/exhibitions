The New Zealand Industrial Exhibition was held at Wellington in 1885, in the colony of New Zealand (Filipová 2015p. 234; Filipová 2015p. 235; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425). The texts give the year but not its dates, venue, organisers or visitors (Filipová 2015p. 235; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425). Gottfried Lindauer's paintings The Laughing Girl and The Maori at Home were shown there (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 221). The texts treat the exhibition only briefly, so the case rests mainly on one chapter about how those paintings represented Maori (Bell 1992p. 218).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Empire and colony

The texts show little about the imperial side of this exhibition. They do not say who governed Wellington, which colonies or dominions took part, or what the organisers hoped it would do for trade or settlement (Filipová 2015p. 235; Bell 1992p. 218). Rice argues that in the colonies, international displays were meant to show colonists evidence of culture and civilisation (Filipová 2015p. 234). She frames this around the Dunedin fairs and does not apply it to Wellington (Filipová 2015p. 235). The one connection the texts draw is representational. Bell argues that Lindauer's Maori subjects existed primarily for European pleasure (Bell 1992p. 221), and two of Lindauer's paintings of Maori were shown at Wellington (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 221).

Exhibits and representation of Maori

At Wellington The Laughing Girl was presented as a portrait of a real person, Meri Nereaha, daughter of a well-known chieftain in the Forty Mile Bush (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 218). Bell's note 90 cites the 1885 Wellington catalogue, p. 149, for this identification (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 278). Bell argues that the painting idealises her: compared with a Lindauer photograph of the same woman, the painting smooths her body and reworks her features to conventional European ideas of female beauty (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 218). The Maori at Home shows the chief Harawira Mahikai and his wife (Bell 1992p. 221). Bell also spells the chief's name Hawawira (Bell 1992p. 221).

Its place in the series

Bell says both paintings were also shown at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 221). Rice notes that The Maori at Home had formed the centrepiece of Walter Buller's collection at that London exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 244). Rice lists Wellington 1885 among New Zealand exhibitions with Christchurch 1882 and Auckland 1898 (Filipová 2015p. 235). The texts do not connect Wellington with Dunedin's exhibitions beyond that listing (Filipová 2015p. 235).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Lindauer's paintings at Wellington — Bell names The Laughing Girl and The Maori at Home as the works shown there, and treats both as Lindauer's Maori subjects (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 221).
  • Idealised portraits of Maori — Bell argues that the Laughing Girl reworks a real woman's body and face to European ideals (Bell 1992p. 218).
  • Colonial representation — Bell argues that Lindauer's Maori existed for European enjoyment, not as records of ethnological fact (Bell 1992p. 221).
  • Catalogues as evidence — Bell ties the sitter's identity to the 1885 Wellington catalogue through his note 90 (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 278).
  • Colonial exhibitions in Dunedin — Rice's argument about colonial culture is made about Dunedin, and Wellington appears only in a list (Filipová 2015p. 234; Filipová 2015p. 235).

Questions for study

  1. Bell cites the 1885 Wellington catalogue for the sitter's identity (Bell 1992p. 218). What would the catalogue itself need to show for that identification to count as evidence, and how does the London catalogue treat the same painting (Bell 1992p. 218)?
  2. If a painting is shown as a portrait of a real person but is argued to idealise her, what does that suggest about how exhibitions presented Maori sitters (Bell 1992p. 218; Bell 1992p. 218)?
  3. Rice ties colonial displays to the civilising aims of colonists, but writes about Dunedin. How far can that argument be carried over to Wellington in 1885 (Filipová 2015p. 234; Filipová 2015p. 235)?
  4. Why do the texts mention the Wellington exhibition only in lists and notes, and which sources would be needed to learn its organisers and visitors (Filipová 2015p. 235; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 425)?

Annotated bibliography

The 3 texts in our collection that discuss this fair, grouped by how much they have to say about it. Annotations describe what each text says and argues about this fair in particular, not the work as a whole.

Brief discussion 1

Bell, Leonard. Colonial Constructs: European Images of Maori, 1840–1914. Auckland: Auckland University Press, 1992.
ch. 5, 'Lindauer's Paintings of Maori Customs and Legends'; printed pp. 216-221 (notes pp. 214-226)

Bell's chapter on Gottfried Lindauer's Maori paintings says that The Laughing Girl and The Maori at Home were shown at the 1885 Wellington exhibition, and that the Laughing Girl was presented there as a portrait of a real woman, Meri Nereaha. Bell argues that the painting is an idealised construct: he sets it beside a Lindauer photograph of the same woman and shows her body and features reworked to conventional European ideas of female beauty. The Wellington showing takes only a few sentences in a chapter about Lindauer's art, where it stands with the 1886 London exhibition as one occasion on which the painting was presented as a lifelike portrait. The chapter says nothing more about the Wellington exhibition itself.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Bell studies European paintings, watercolours, drawings and prints of Maori made for, exhibited in or published in Australasia and Britain, from the first organised settlement in New Zealand to the emergence of an independent nation. Its argument is that every image is a construction shaped by European conventions, patrons, racial theory and audiences, so it is better read as ideology than as a neutral record. The chapters follow artists and periods in turn: Angas and others in the 1840s and early 1850s, Gilfillan and Strutt, the artists active in New Zealand in the 1860s, the historical paintings of the 1890s and 1900s, Lindauer's pictures of customs and legend, and Dittmer's Te Tohunga of 1907. The conclusion considers later uses of these images, including their reuse by contemporary Maori artists and the 1987 Te Maori exhibition.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are slight in this book, which is about European images of Maori and the colonial settings in which they were made. No Asian display, participant or visitor is mentioned in any of the exhibitions discussed. Asia appears only as subject matter, in passing and mostly as a comparison for European painting: the Siamese twins among the Egyptian Hall's entertainments in London in the 1840s (chapter 1); the Orient of illustrated travel books (chapter 1); belles from Tahiti, the Philippines, India and China in Chevalier's 1872 London exhibition (chapter 3); Chinese heads among Hutchinson's sculptures shown at the A.S.A. in 1901 (chapter 5); and the European romanticisation of Africa, Asia and Oceania, to which Bell relates Dittmer's rhetoric (chapter 6). The Orientalism Bell discusses refers mainly to North Africa and the Middle East. South Asia enters only through the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London, where New Zealand exhibits were shown.

Passing mentions 2

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
ch. 10 (Rebecca Rice), opening pages on the Dunedin exhibitions; printed pp. 234-235

Rice's chapter on the fine art displays at the Dunedin exhibitions of 1865 and 1889-90 names the Wellington exhibition of 1885 once, in a list of New Zealand exhibitions of the period alongside Christchurch 1882 and Auckland 1898. The list is an aside that sets Dunedin's two major international exhibitions against the exhibitions held in other towns. Rice's general argument about colonial exhibitions is framed around Dunedin, and the text does not describe the Wellington exhibition's contents, organisers or reception.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

The encyclopedia's appendix of fairs left out of the main body lists the New Zealand Industrial Exhibition at Wellington in 1885 and says nothing further about it. The appendix explains that its fairs fell short of the book's qualifications: many were very specialised and others lacked a suitable international scope. The entry supports only the bare facts that the fair was held at Wellington in 1885.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

On the web

Wikipedia

The New Zealand Industrial Exhibition was an industrial exhibition held in a large Industrial Exhibition Building in Wellington, between Lambton Quay and Stout Street in 1885. Organised by Julius Vogel it was intended to display New Zealand's industries to both encourage foreign investment and to boost New Zealand's self-confidence.
