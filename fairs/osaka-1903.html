Osaka · 1903

Fifth National Industrial Exhibition

第五回内国勧業博覧会 (Dai-go-kai Naikoku Kangyō Hakurankai)
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Fifth National Industrial Exhibition (Dai-go-kai Naikoku Kangyō Hakurankai) was held in Tennōji Park, Osaka, from 1 March to 31 July 1903 (Filipová 2015p. 335). The Meiji government planned it as the fifth and last of its domestic encouragement-of-industry exhibitions (Hurp. 47; Hurp. 47), and a local association of Kansai leaders helped run it (Hurp. 48). Foreign demand for space made it effectively international (Filipová 2015p. 335). Its Jinruikan, a live display of peoples outside the grounds, drew protests that led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean displays (Filipová 2015p. 340; Kwonp. 27).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Japan's domestic exhibitions began with a national exhibition at Ueno Park, Tokyo, in 1877 (Filipová 2015p. 334). The fourth had been held at Kyoto in 1895, and it had no buildings for foreign goods (Filipová 2015p. 334p. 335). The unequal treaties with the Western powers were due to be revised by 1902, and the fifth exhibition was meant to show Japan's new status on a par with the West (Hurp. 47). Hur also calls Osaka the first international exhibition Japan held (Hurp. 7).

Daykin argues that international participation was a sign of a host nation's standing, and that Osaka stood on the margin between domestic and international status (Filipová 2015p. 333p. 345).

Organisers and site

An association of Kansai local leaders supported the fair, and its first chairman was Doi Michio (Hurp. 48). The government sent about 10,000 invitations abroad (Hurp. 49). Planners expected little foreign interest and reserved 300 tsubo for foreign exhibits; applications needed more than 2,000 tsubo (Filipová 2015p. 335). The texts I read give no figures on cost or finance.

Daykin gives the site as about 105,000 tsubo, or 34 hectares (Filipová 2015p. 335). Hur gives 100,000 tsubo, or 82 acres (Hurp. 48). Daykin describes a Foreign Samples Building, and a Canadian pavilion and five Western firms' pavilions built by the exhibitors themselves (Filipová 2015p. 335). The Obayashi Tower, 28 metres high, served as the landmark (Filipová 2015p. 338). The Jinruikan stood outside the grounds beside the entrance (Filipová 2015p. 340). Hur places it next to a zoo in an entertainment quarter, and says the exhibition committee did not organise it; Nishida Masatoshi, an Osaka businessman, proposed it (Hurp. 56).

Exhibits and attractions

Daykin presents the Machinery Building as the showcase of Japan's industrial progress, and quotes a Japan Weekly Chronicle piece on the Osaka Iron Works' steam navvy (Filipová 2015p. 335p. 336). China staged a large exhibit in the Foreign Samples Building, with porcelain, bronze vessels, lacquer and stationery (Hurp. 55). The Netherlands-India section printed its catalogue in Dutch and Japanese (Filipová 2015p. 337).

The Formosa section was built as a Formosan village, with a tea house served by young women in Chinese-style dress (Filipová 2015p. 341p. 342). Hur says the Taiwan Pavilion attracted over 5,300,000 viewers from March to July (Hurp. 76). That figure is larger than any total attendance the texts give (Filipová 2015p. 343).

Visitors and reception

The texts disagree on attendance. Daykin gives 4,350,693 visitors, including 14,443 Europeans and Americans and 8,677 Chinese and Koreans (Filipová 2015p. 343), but elsewhere says nearly 4.5 million (Filipová 2015p. 335). Hur gives more than 4.3 million (Hurp. 48), and 22,600 foreign visitors, which he gives as 5.2 per cent of the total (Hurp. 49). Those numbers do not reconcile. Hotta-Lister, using hotel registers, counts about 9,570 foreign visitors (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). On Osaka Day more than 100,000 people hoped to enter, and paid admissions reached 93,464 (Filipová 2015p. 343).

Foreign visitors found little help at first. The Japan Weekly Chronicle complained of the lack of foreign-language guides, and bilingual guides appeared within the first month (Filipová 2015p. 337). A guide to the Foreign Samples Building appeared in early June (Filipová 2015p. 338).

The awards policy also favoured Japan. Only domestic exhibitors received awards, and foreign exhibitors got Letters of Thanks, because the fair was "not considered an International one" (Filipová 2015p. 338).

Politics and controversy

The Jinruikan prospectus, quoted by Kwon, listed seven peoples: the Ainu of Hokkaido, Taiwanese seiban, Ryukyuans, Koreans, Chinese, Indians and Javanese (Kwonp. 21). It ranked them by civilisation (Kwonp. 21). Daykin says the Chinese displays, including opium-smoking and foot-binding dioramas, were cancelled before the opening after protests from Chinese students in Japan, who objected to being classed as yabanjin (Filipová 2015p. 340; Kwonp. 27). Hur adds that Sun Shifu, a Chinese merchant in Osaka, threatened a mourning flag if the plan went ahead, and that the Chinese minister, Cai Jun, had intervened on 24 February (Hurp. 63p. 64). A Chinese viewer had said that "watched like animals" was how Chinese would be seen there (Hurp. 58).

The Korean display showed two women, whom Kwon identifies as kisaeng entertainers (Kwonp. 22). Three Koreans, Cho Hŭi-yŏn and two others, protested in the Osaka mainichi shimbun on 19 March (Kwonp. 28). The Japanese Foreign Ministry then ordered the display withdrawn for diplomatic reasons (Kwonp. 27). Kwon cites an Osaka vice-governor's report of 24 March, saying the display was never formally shown to Koreans (Kwonp. 28).

Okinawan women were also removed. The Ryūkyū Shimpō protested that they stood for Okinawan women as a whole, and Okinawans left the live displays after 17 May (Filipová 2015p. 340).

The Taiwanese woman in the Jinruikan was taken by Chinese viewers for a Chinese exhibit, because of her dress and bound feet. Hunan students petitioned the Chinese consul at Kobe on 26 March, and the Japanese Foreign Ministry told the Osaka prefect to withdraw her (Hurp. 77). Daykin reads the whole dispute as a question of "which nations were privileged to be the spectators" (Filipová 2015p. 341).

The colonial setting

Kwon notes that the Ainu and Ryukyuans had been absorbed into Japan, the Taiwanese lived in Japan's formal colony, and Koreans were within its external sphere of influence (Kwonp. 22). Kwon argues that the Korean display was smaller than the others, perhaps because Korea lay outside Japan's colonial borders (Kwonp. 23). Daykin argues that the Formosa section made Japan the civilised spectator, set against a backward Asia (Filipová 2015p. 342). Hur argues that Japan used Taiwan's exhibits to show its power over China (Hurp. 7).

Aftermath and legacy

Hur reports that, according to a Hong Kong paper, Zaizhen told the Empress Dowager that China's display ranked lowest (Hurp. 84). The Qing government then invested more in the St Louis Exposition of 1904 (Hurp. 84). Hur calls Osaka China's first meaningful step into official overseas exhibiting, and says the conflicts there foreshadowed how St Louis would be received (Hurp. 84).

Kwon argues that the Korean reaction hardened by 1907. He quotes the T'aegŭk hakpo, a journal of Korean students abroad, which said the 1907 Crystal Pavilion was no different from the 1903 display (Kwonp. 33). His conclusion is that common race "developed into a rationale for Japanese imperialism" (Kwonp. 38). Kato wrote to Komura in November 1909 and reminded him of the 1903 Osaka case (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 180). The book also calls Osaka 1903 the largest domestic exhibition held in Japan before 1914 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).

Its place in the series

Daykin places the fair after the 1872 Tokyo exposition and the national series that began at Ueno in 1877 (Filipová 2015p. 334). A souvenir guide to Osaka, quoted by Hur, expected that Japan would soon host a large world exhibition (Hurp. 47). Henry Dosch, commissioner for Oregon at Osaka, later persuaded Japanese authorities to attend the Alaska-Yukon Exposition (Filipová 2015p. 344; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 215). Rydell dates Dosch's Osaka visit to 1902 (Rydell 1984), while Hotta-Lister dates it to 1903 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 215). Hirayama Seishin, chairman of the commission for the 1917 exhibition, said on 15 December that it would be three times the size of Osaka 1903 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189).

How historians have interpreted it

Daykin argues that Osaka was a marginal exposition, one that asserted Japan's equality with the West two years before the Russo-Japanese War (Filipová 2015p. 345). Filipová's introduction takes the same view and links the fair to the American model (Filipová 2015p. 14). Hur argues that the Qing government's attempt to present China as a modern state was interrupted and criticised by Chinese publications, led by reformers and revolutionaries (Hurp. 163p. 85). Kwon argues that Korean intellectuals moved from hope in Northeast Asian solidarity to nationalism (Kwonp. 38). Hotta-Lister uses the Chinese protests of 1903 as a parallel to Japanese resentment of colonial displays in London (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 142).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Chinese participation

The Qing government sent an imperial commission under Lord Zaizhen, who came to Kobe on 29 April and left for China on 28 May (Hurp. 55). Japan sent complimentary tickets to Chinese officials, and Konoe Atsumaro's East Asia Common Culture Society sought funds to bring Qing officials to the fair (Hurp. 49). About 8,600 Chinese and Korean visitors are given by Hur (Hurp. 49).

Taiwan, the Ainu and Okinawa

Taiwanese women worked as waitresses at the Taiwan Pavilion, and the three girls Hur names were aged 11, 14 and 15 (Hurp. 75). Daykin, citing Matsuda, notes that the Ainu were paid for their role as exhibits with money for a school on Hokkaido (Filipová 2015p. 347). Kwon describes the Ainu display as a village with a family group (Kwonp. 22). Ryukyuan people in Okinawa also began a movement to withdraw the displays, feeling that removing only Chinese and Koreans insulted them as Japanese (Kwonp. 29).

Korea

Daykin names M. Yikami as special commissioner for Korea, who wrote that the fair was the first international exhibition in Japan (Filipová 2015p. 344p. 345). Henry says late Chosŏn leaders had set up displays at Osaka in 1903, but gives no details (Henry 2014p. 93). Kwon finds no official Korean government documents on the display (Kwonp. 28).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Human displays and social Darwinism — Kwon reads the prospectus as ranking peoples by civilisation, and the Korean display as combining that scale with entertainment (Kwonp. 21p. 23).
  • Spectators and spectacle — Daykin argues that the dispute was about which nations could watch and which were displayed, and that Japan remained among the watchers (Filipová 2015p. 341).
  • Chinese protest and the Qing court — Hur shows Chinese students, merchants and the minister forcing changes to the displays, and sees the protests as a turning point for the court (Hurp. 63p. 84).
  • Korean nationalism — Kwon traces a move from trust in shared race and culture to nationalism (Kwonp. 29p. 38).
  • Colonial display and Japan's status — the Formosa section and the Taiwan Pavilion are read as demonstrations of Japanese power over China and Asia (Filipová 2015p. 342; Hurp. 7).
  • Commerce and international standing — Daykin reads international participation as a marker of standing, and the awards policy as a failure to prepare for it (Filipová 2015p. 333p. 338).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the Japanese government accept the removal of the Chinese and Korean displays, when the Jinruikan had been presented as a commercial and educational show? (Filipová 2015p. 340; Kwonp. 22p. 28)
  2. Henry says late Chosŏn leaders established displays at Osaka, while Kwon attributes the Korean display to Japanese organisers and finds no official Korean documents. Which account do the texts support? (Henry 2014p. 93; Kwonp. 21p. 28)
  3. Did the 1903 fair count as an international exhibition? Daykin and Hotta-Lister answer differently. (Filipová 2015p. 344; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217)
  4. How far can visitor figures be trusted, when the texts give different totals for the same fair? (Filipová 2015p. 335p. 343; Hurp. 49; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217)

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
Introduction: The Margins of Exhibitions and Exhibitions StudiesMarta Filipováp. 1Asia

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

15International Ambitions of an Exhibition at the Margin: Japan’s 1903 Osaka ExpositionJeffer Daykinp. 333Asia

Daykin asks what it meant for Japan to host an international exposition in 1903, using Osaka's Fifth National Industrial Exhibition in Tennoji Park as the case. The event was planned as a domestic show and became international only when foreign applications far exceeded the space reserved for them, so a Foreign Samples Building and separate national pavilions had to be provided. Daykin uses the Japan Weekly Chronicle, foreign-language guides and the commissioners' introductions to show how the awards policy and language arrangements lagged behind the event. His central example is the Jinrui Kan, a live 'native peoples' concession outside the grounds, which was altered after protests from Chinese and Korean students and officials and from the Okinawan press. The Formosan section, by contrast, presented Taiwan as a colonial foil that made Japan the civilised spectator. Daykin concludes that Osaka was a marginal international exposition, one that asserted Japan's equality with the West just two years before the Russo-Japanese War.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
AbstractAsia

Hur's abstract summarises a dissertation on the Qing government's attempt to stage modern statehood at world exhibitions and how that attempt was undermined. The Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan and Chinese reformers each shaped China's image, and criticism of the Qing grew after Osaka in 1903 and St Louis in 1904. It ends with the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which Chinese intellectuals came to support once the government promised constitutional reform.

In the contents of this work

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

TwoChina, the “Barbarian” Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903p. 47Asia

Hur examines the Fifth Domestic Encouragement of Industry Exhibition, held in Osaka in 1903, which Japan staged as a semi-international show and a rehearsal for a future world exposition. The chapter first shows how the Meiji government courted Chinese officials and gentry with invitations and complimentary tickets, and how Japanese newspapers cast Japan as a model for Chinese reform. Its main case is the Jinruikan, an entertainment building beside a zoo that planned to display 'native' peoples, including Chinese. Hur follows the protests of Chinese students and merchants in Japan, the involvement of the Chinese minister Cai Jun, and the coverage in Chinese newspapers, then turns to the Taiwan Pavilion and a Taiwanese woman mistaken for a Chinese exhibit. The chapter concludes that Zaizhen's imperial commission made Osaka China's first meaningful step into official overseas exhibiting, but that press distrust of the court was already visible in these disputes.

Conclusionp. 163Asia

Hur concludes the dissertation by arguing that the Qing government saw world exhibitions as a diplomatic arena in which to present China as a modern state, while Chinese reformers and revolutionaries used the same displays to attack the court. Osaka, St. Louis and Nanjing are recalled as cases in which Chinese exhibits were exaggerated, distorted or ignored by the press. The chapter notes that 'national humiliation' rhetoric, which had targeted foreign powers, was turned inward against the Qing. Hur then argues that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories that treat exhibitions either as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist, and calls for audience-oriented approaches. The conclusion ends with comparisons between Chinese and Japanese exhibition traditions, and with a note on the transnational Chinese press and a late Qing public sphere that extended beyond the national boundary.

Kwon, Hyeokhui. “An Analysis of Korean Intellectual Responses to the Exhibition of Koreans at Japanese Expositions: Nationalism and the Discourse on Northeast Asian Solidarity at the Turn of the Century.” Journal article.
Korean Reactions to the 1903 Display at the Anthropological Pavilionp. 20Asia

Kwon begins with the 1903 Anthropological Pavilion at the Fifth National Industrial Exposition in Osaka, a Japanese version of the Western native-village exhibit that the Tokyo Anthropological Institute helped to organise. The Japanese organisers arranged the peoples on display by a social Darwinist scale of civilisation, and the Korean display differed from the family groups of Ainu and Taiwanese. Kwon argues that the Korean display consisted of two women who were in fact kisaeng entertainers, shown in performance dress and reproduced on souvenir postcards. The exhibit mixed classification with commercial entertainment, and its smaller scale may have reflected Korea's position outside Japan's colonial borders. The section closes by framing the displays as part of a discourse of Northeast Asian unity that Korean responses would later test.

In the contents of this work

The Anthropological Pavilion and Korean Responsesp. 27Asia

Kwon's longest section follows the protests that brought about the withdrawal of the 1903 Korean display. Japanese officials, worried about relations with Korea and China, ordered the display removed after a protest by three Koreans living in Japan, who appealed to shared race and culture. Kwon notes that these protesters were pro-Japanese bureaucrats, and sets their stance beside the Chinese students' fierce objections to a Chinese display cancelled before it opened. Turning to 1907, Kwon argues that Korean newspapers and the T'aegŭk hakpo responded with far more emotion, condemning the display as racist and as national shame. The Japanese Yomiuri, by contrast, framed the affair as women's liberation and downplayed discrimination. The section ends by linking these reactions to the nationalism of the Patriotic Enlightenment movement after the 1905 Protectorate Treaty.

In the contents of this work

The Transformation in Korean People’s Perceptions of the Display of Koreansp. 34Asia

Kwon's penultimate section traces how Korean intellectuals' views of the displays changed between 1903 and 1907. Kwon places both reactions within social Darwinism and the idea of a racial struggle that Japanese politicians such as Itō Hirobumi promoted, and argues that elites at first hoped Japan would lead a yellow-race solidarity against the West. The 1903 protest grew from that hope, Kwon suggests, but the Russo-Japanese War and the 1905 Protectorate Treaty weakened it. Quoting the diaries of Yun Ch'i-ho and the editorials of Chang Chi-yŏn, Kwon shows that the 1907 reaction was angrier and more nationalist as Japan took control of the Korean state. The section notes that the display coincided with the emperor's envoys to The Hague and the later dethronement of the emperor. It concludes that Korean intellectuals turned from solidarity to nationalism.

In the contents of this work

Conclusionp. 37Asia

Kwon concludes that the Korean displays combined social Darwinist classification with commercial spectacle, and that Korean responses reveal the shifting politics of Northeast Asia. The Anthropological Pavilion claimed scientific authority, while the Crystal Pavilion was mainly entertainment, yet both drew public curiosity. Korean intellectuals first trusted in common race and culture, Kwon argues, then turned to nationalism as Japan's imperial ambitions became clear. Common race, the chapter concludes, became a rationale for Japanese imperialism.

Kwon introduces the human displays at Japanese expositions, which combined social Darwinist evolutionary schemes with freak-show entertainment and colonial ideology. In the contents of this work

The 1907 Crystal Pavilion and Its Representation of Koreansp. 24Asia

Kwon turns to the 1907 Crystal Pavilion at the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition, which Japanese merchants built beside a small Korea hall to draw visitors. It was a fantasy and terror attraction with a dark cave, mirror illusions and a final room where a Korean man and woman sat in traditional dress, and no anthropologists took part. Kwon reconstructs the pair's identities from competing Japanese and Korean newspapers, which disagree over names, origins and whether the woman had come willingly. A table sets out how the display was described, and Korean newspapers likened it to the exhibition of animals. Kwon concludes that the Crystal Pavilion was both educational and commercial, like the 1903 display, and that both were withdrawn after Korean protests stirred ethnic feeling against Japan.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards.
