Chosŏn Exposition of 1929

Shisei 20 nen kinen Cho-sen hakurankai
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Chosŏn Exposition of 1929, which Todd A. Henry calls the Korea Exposition, was held in Keijō (Seoul) from 12 September to 31 October 1929 (Henry 2014p. 113p. 121). It stood behind the new Government-General building on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds (Henry 2014p. 113). The colonial government staged it to mark twenty years of Japanese rule, as one expression of the accommodationist policy of cultural rule (Henry 2014p. 113). Henry treats it as a case study in the uneven rewards of colonial development, which drew sharp criticism from Korean nationalists (Henry 2014p. 121p. 129).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Officials first discussed an exposition in 1921. It was to mark the fifteenth anniversary of Japanese rule and the opening of the Government-General's new administrative complex (Henry 2014p. 113). Henry says its aims were to introduce the world to the goods of Korean culture and the productive capacity of its twenty million inhabitants, and to promote industrial education (Henry 2014p. 114). The event was then planned for 1926 with a budget of three million yen. Budget shortfalls postponed it until 1929 (Henry 2014p. 114). The introduction gives a different original date, 1925 (Henry 2014p. 19). The texts therefore disagree on whether the plan was for 1925 or 1926 (Henry 2014p. 19p. 114).

The colonial setting

Henry links the event to a change in colonial policy after the March First Uprising of 1919. Authorities then redoubled their efforts at assimilation under the accommodationist policy of cultural rule (Henry 2014p. 113). Their aim was to co-opt liberal-minded elites and poorer residents who might lean toward socialism (Henry 2014p. 113). Henry dates the completion of the central administrative complex to 1925, on the site of the 1915 Industrial Exhibition (Henry 2014p. 113).

Organisers and finance

The Government-General's plan relied on an outlay of 1.43 million yen, 56 per cent of the total. Provincial governments were to give 130,000 yen and the Keijō municipal government 250,000 yen (Henry 2014p. 114). Planners expected two to three million visitors. Two million paying twenty sen each would bring in 400,000 yen, and sales and other income would add two to three hundred thousand yen more (Henry 2014p. 114).

The Keijō Cooperative Committee (KCC) did most of the mobilising. It sold tickets, postcards and guidebooks through commercial boards and local bodies, ran the commerce and recreation, and arranged travel to the capital. It called on nearly 350 businessmen, councillors and neighbourhood administrators, more than one hundred of them Korean (Henry 2014p. 114). Henry argues that membership marked the privileged status of local elites and gave the colonial state a way to reach poorer Koreans through them (Henry 2014p. 114).

The total budget fell from three million yen to less than 1.5 million yen. The Government-General, facing a recession, could contribute only 800,000 yen, and construction of the halls began only months before the gates opened (Henry 2014p. 116).

The last-minute donation campaign was contested. The councillor Sŏng Song-nok proposed cancelling it, and two others proposed cutting the 250,000 yen target for Koreans (Henry 2014p. 119). A report published weeks before the opening described two officials in Sunch'ŏn, South Chŏlla Province, pressing a landlord, Kim Pu-ho, to give. He refused, responded with violence, and was arrested (Henry 2014p. 120). The KCC could not raise enough from individuals. It turned to the Korea Bank and the Development Bank, each of which promised twelve thousand yen, to Keijō Electric, and to talks with Mitsui Bussan and Mitsubishi (Henry 2014p. 120). Its head went to Tokyo to seek a large donation from the Central Korea Association (Henry 2014p. 120).

Site and buildings

The 1929 plan moved the main gate to the east side of Kyŏngbok Palace (Henry 2014p. 116). Hong Kal, whom Henry cites, showed that this destroyed the palace's north-south axis and created a new east-west corridor (Henry 2014p. 116). The relocated gate remained the main entrance, and the Kyŏnghoe Pavilion became the western anchor of the corridor (Henry 2014p. 116). The winning poster of the KCC's competition showed the Kyŏnghoe Pavilion in front, with the new Government-General building behind it (Henry 2014p. 115).

The halls along the corridor were built in what the media called a "pure Korean style" (Henry 2014p. 117). Henry notes that their shapes and proportions were in fact new, and that they copied palace buildings destroyed under early colonial rule (Henry 2014p. 117). The North and South Industrial Halls, the Socioeconomics Hall and the Rice Hall stood on this corridor. They showed the colony's agriculture, forestry, maritime goods, light industry, handicrafts, mining, finance and rice (Henry 2014p. 117).

Other halls represented the empire. The Naichikan (Metropolitan Hall) was built in a Western style and showed products from every Japanese prefecture (Henry 2014p. 118). Halls for Karafuto, Taiwan and Manchuria-Mongolia also stood on the grounds (Henry 2014p. 118). The thirteen provincial halls were built in a modernist style, with one exception, and local residents helped create their images (Henry 2014p. 118).

Exhibits and attractions

Henry says the Korean halls showed only the colony's primary goods and the tools used to make them, while the Western-style halls showed advances in engineering, architecture and communications (Henry 2014p. 118). Kim Ki-gon, an exhibitor from Hamhŭng, found the displays scanty and repetitive, and he proposed that products be shown in separate buildings with fuller statistics (Henry 2014p. 125p. 126). A reporter later described the Socioeconomics Hall as a hall for promoting assimilation (Henry 2014p. 128).

Visitors and reception

Organisers had aimed for one million visitors. Twenty days before the close, officials admitted that less than 75 per cent of that number had come, and they considered extending the event (Henry 2014p. 121). They relied on rural tour groups. Planners had predicted that nearly half of some 700,000 rural visitors would come in groups (Henry 2014p. 121). The colonial state used intermediary organisations to bring nearly 300,000 group visitors (Henry 2014p. 121). The final figures were more than 1.1 million paid admissions and nearly 1.5 million visitors in all (Henry 2014p. 121). A note gives a different breakdown: 290,438 group visitors, 431,862 individual visitors and 612,300 residents of Korea. The group and individual totals sum to 722,300, well short of the 1.5 million in the text, and the origin figures do not add up to that note's own total (Henry 2014p. 246).

About six hundred female guards were hired for two months, two-thirds of them Korean (Henry 2014p. 124). Press cartoons sexualised them, and some guards sold pecks on the cheek for fifty sen (Henry 2014p. 125). Chongno merchants had borrowed to stock up for the event, and four major commercial houses closed before it opened (Henry 2014p. 124). Ten days after the opening, only two of the Korean inns had guests, according to their association's complaint (Henry 2014p. 124).

Henry bases a satirical dialogue on an account in Sinmin of November 1929 (Henry 2014p. 127p. 247). In it, a reporter and a couple skip the Metropolitan Hall and find diseased female specimens behind a curtain in the Hygiene Hall (Henry 2014p. 127). They also find the robot in the Reference Hall disappointing (Henry 2014p. 128).

Politics and controversy

Henry argues that the nationalist commentators came from a nascent middle class. They welcomed modernity but wanted a larger share of its wealth (Henry 2014p. 121). The Tonga ilbo reported that officials in North Kyŏngsang Province visited townships several times a day to compel visits, and that more than two thousand inhabitants of Sangju eventually acquiesced (Henry 2014p. 122). Another account said township heads forced residents to pawn clothing and dishware (Henry 2014p. 122). One critic estimated that a trip cost up to twenty yen, and that the exposition would drain at least one million yen from the countryside (Henry 2014p. 122).

Yun Ch'i-ho, a privileged Korean, wrote in his diary that "The whole show is a show of the Japanese, by the Japanese" (Henry 2014p. 123). Yu Kwang-yŏl argued that the event should be called the Exposition for the Japanese in Korea (Henry 2014p. 126). In the satirical dialogue, Mr S questioned the authenticity of the halls, which he compared with a Japanese style found at Nagoya and Himeji castles (Henry 2014p. 127).

Henry concludes that "Held amid a worldwide depression, the Korea Exposition perhaps best exposed the harsh realities and practical limits of cultural rule" (Henry 2014p. 129).

Its place in the series

Henry presents the 1929 event as the third of three colonial expositions on the palace grounds. The Keijō Exhibition of 1907 was a small precursor dominated by Japanese merchants (Henry 2014p. 93). Henry notes that the 1915 Industrial Exhibition had been held on the site where the central administrative complex was later built (Henry 2014p. 113). Young-sin Park, writing on the 1915 exhibition, says it displayed many features later seen at the Chosŏn Exposition of 1929 and the Great Chosŏn Exposition of 1940 (Parkp. 21).

In a later passage Henry returns to the palace grounds. He notes that colonial officials struggled to give them a Korean style that served their own interests, and that South Korean leaders have since found an authentic version of the site hard to produce (Henry 2014p. 214).

Halls and visitors from the wider empire

Halls for Karafuto, Taiwan and Manchuria-Mongolia were among the empire's displays (Henry 2014p. 118). Yu Kwang-yŏl noted Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean lees, and he predicted that Manchuria and Mongolia would overtake Korea in livestock and farm output (Henry 2014p. 126).

Japanese visitors were a deliberate target. Henry says the Korean-style design aimed to satisfy metropolitan tourists and expatriate settlers (Henry 2014p. 116). Officials went to Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto to seek sponsorship for display halls, and Nagoya and Fukuoka decided to fund special halls at four to five hundred thousand yen each (Henry 2014p. 115). According to a colonial official who visited the metropole in spring 1929, the campaign produced a Korea fever among Japanese in western Japan. The Government-General negotiated rail and ferry discounts above 20 per cent (Henry 2014p. 116). Only 21,746 Japanese visitors came, and 4,267 came from Manchuria, according to the note figures (Henry 2014p. 245p. 246).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Material assimilation — Henry frames the event as a way to make Koreans see colonial development as personal progress, with uneven results (Henry 2014p. 129).
  • Cultural rule — the event is a showcase of the policy of accommodation, mobilised through the KCC and local elites (Henry 2014p. 113p. 114).
  • Colonial aesthetics — the relocated gate and the pure Korean style halls are read as Japanese control of Korean palace architecture (Henry 2014p. 116p. 117).
  • Finance and coercion — the budget cut, forced donations and the Sunch'ŏn arrest (Henry 2014p. 116p. 120).
  • Rural tour groups and nationalist critique — groups were central to the crowds, and critics saw them as a burden on the peasantry (Henry 2014p. 121p. 122).

Questions for study

  1. How far did the pure Korean style halls work as propaganda, given that Henry reports critics saw them as reproductions of destroyed palace buildings (Henry 2014p. 117)?
  2. Why do the texts give different original dates for the event, and what does that reveal about planning under a shrinking budget (Henry 2014p. 19p. 114)?
  3. Who gained from the exposition and who lost out? Compare the Chongno merchants, inn owners and female guards (Henry 2014p. 124).
  4. Can the figures of 1.1 million paid admissions and 1.5 million visitors be trusted, given that the breakdown in the note does not add up (Henry 2014p. 121p. 246)?

3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.

Wikipedia

The Chōsen Exhibition (Japanese: 朝鮮博覧会; Korean: 조선박람회) was held to mark the 20th anniversary of the Japanese colony in Korea. It was held at Gyeongbokgung, Keijō (Seoul), Korea, Empire of Japan between 12 September 1929 and 31 October 1929. It was attended by 986,179 people.
