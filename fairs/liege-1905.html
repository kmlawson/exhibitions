Liège Universal and International Exposition

Exposition universelle et internationale de Liège
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle et internationale de Liège opened on 27 April 1905 and closed on 6 November, on a site at the confluence of the Meuse and Ourthe rivers (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179). Stanard calls it the fourth universal exposition in Belgium in twenty years, and the reference works put its attendance at about seven million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Historians use it to study Belgian imperial ambition, the reconstructed historic streets that Belgian fairs repeated, and the first Chinese commission led by a civil official. Together these show how a small country used exhibitions to claim standing (Roche 2000p. 58; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Filipová 2015p. 190; Hurp. 121; Filipová 2015p. 185).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and the 1905 date

The idea of a Liège fair was aired in the early 1890s, but the city faced strong competition from Brussels and Antwerp (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178). Florent Pholien made a new proposal in 1895, and a provisional committee was formed in June 1897 with the support of two ministers, Paul de Favereau and Albert Nyssens (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). The target was 1903, but delays pushed the opening to 1905 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178). Stanard and Gendron both tie the date to the 75th anniversary of Belgian independence (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). Stanard also links the fair to the 40th anniversary of Leopold II's accession and says it "established Belgium as a major exhibitory power" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178).

Site and buildings

The reference works disagree on the size of the grounds. Stanard gives 70 hectares plus a 20-hectare annex, with 135,000 square metres under roof (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179). Gendron gives 52 acres on the plain of the Vennes, a site that required rerouting the Ourthe and building a new bridge over the Meuse (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). Badger's appendix table gives 173 acres (Badger 1979p. 131). Stanard says the grounds lay in three separated parts, Les Vennes, La Boverie and La Fragnée, and that the main halls stood in Les Vennes, an area prone to flooding until about 1900 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178). Prince Albert laid the first stone of the halls' facade on 21 July 1903, and de Braey, an Antwerp architect, won the facade competition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). The Palais des Beaux-Arts was left to the city and became the Musée d'Art Moderne et d'Art Contemporaine (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179).

Exhibits and attractions

The most popular attraction was Le Vieux Liège, dozens of buildings replicating the city of the prince-bishops before the revolutions of 1789 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron describes an Old Liège of about ten acres, a tribute to Walloon architecture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Stanard also lists a Village Sénégalais, panoramas, theatres, ballooning and congresses (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron records a Hall of Machines with a section on Belgian scientific achievement, a lace hall, and bicycles, motorcycles and automobiles (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). The French display included a chromophone, which combined cinema and phonograph (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187), and Gendron also notes a Paris police exhibit on fingerprints (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). In fine art, John Singer Sargent won one of the four Grand Prizes, the Grand Medal of Honor, for a full-length portrait, which Gendron describes as "full of strength of touch and delicacy of treatment" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Depelchin notes that numerous exotic pavilions lined the paths of the fair (Filipová 2015p. 191), and the Water Chute concession is listed among its attractions (Filipová 2015p. 338). Benedict mentions a Palace of Lace and Women's Work, and groups it with women's buildings that were meeting places rather than shows of women's work (Benedict 1983p. 40; Benedict 1983p. 40).

Nations, colonies and imperial display

Stanard counts 39 nations, including China, and says the organisers secured Russia and Japan, which were at war with each other for most of the exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron gives 33 participating countries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). Both entries put France first: its section covered 30,000 square metres and 7,950 exhibitors, and it built pavilions for its Asian and African colonies, including Algeria and Tunisia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187). Germany, which had taken part in Paris (1900) and St Louis (1904), declined to take part officially, though German products were shown by private firms (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188).

The Belgian colonial showing is where the sources part company. Stanard calls the Etat Indépendent du Congo (EIC) minor beside the French imperial displays and earlier Belgian colonial exhibits. Its pavilion, by the architect Sneyers, resembled the colonial governor's residence at Boma and held ivory sculptures and a large relief map, but Stanard thinks it drew little interest, perhaps for lack of African people on hand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron describes the colonial exhibits as impressive (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Greenhalgh places Liège among the Belgian expositions with extensive imperial sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71), and Roche sets the colonial fairs, Liège among them, within Belgium's claim for recognition as an imperial nation (Roche 2000p. 58; Roche 2000p. 58).

Visitors, money and reception

Attendance is the figure the sources share most closely. Stanard gives more than seven million visitors, Gendron about seven million by closing day, and Badger's table 7,000,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188; Badger 1979p. 131). Stanard lists visits from King Leopold I of Belgium (as printed), King Vittorio Emanuele III of Italy, the Shah of Persia, and Prince and Princess Arisugawa of Japan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). The exhibitor counts differ: Stanard gives 17,004 exhibitors, and Badger's table 16,119 exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179; Badger 1979p. 131). Gendron says the fair made a small profit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Allwood's table has a Liège row showing a loss of £3,005 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Badger's table gives a cost of £14,451,813 (Badger 1979p. 131). Hur gives the closing date as 11 June, against the 6 November of the reference works (Hurp. 120).

Aftermath and legacy

Gendron says the fair's success encouraged the organisers of Brussels 1910 and Ghent 1913 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Depelchin states that a Ghent society was set up only after the people of Liège turned their 1905 exhibition into a huge success (Filipová 2015p. 187). Depelchin contrasts the two fairs: Liège had numerous exotic pavilions along its paths (Filipová 2015p. 191). Depelchin notes that Ghent could be read as favouring its own past and industrial future over exotic display, but qualifies that reading, since Ghent's Moroccan, Tunisian and Algerian contributions were relegated to a wooded part of the site (Filipová 2015p. 191; Filipová 2015p. 191). Allwood notes that Belgium had held more shows than France (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87).

How historians have interpreted it

Stanard and Gendron write as encyclopedists, but their entries carry readings. Stanard presents the fair as a showcase of Liège's past and present and of Belgium's standing, and judges the Congo showing minor (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron presents it as a national celebration linked to progress from 1830 to 1905, and as a success that encouraged later Belgian fairs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188).

Greenhalgh and Roche read Belgian fairs as central to imperial claims. Greenhalgh argues that the Belgian empire received its greatest embellishment at its own international fairs (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Roche argues that the colonial fairs were a major claim to recognition as an imperial nation (Roche 2000p. 58). Stanard's smaller estimate of the Congo showing is the main point on which these readings diverge.

Depelchin places Liège's Vieux Liège among the historical quarters of Belgian fairs and contrasts Liège's exotic pavilions with Ghent (Filipová 2015p. 190; Filipová 2015p. 191). Geppert places Liège in the fair calendar: the retrospective principle of recreated historic streets reached Liège (Geppert 2010p. 52); Liège and Barcelona 1888 were the first European international expositions held outside a capital (Geppert 2010p. 222); and Liège was among eight large international expositions of 1880 to 1910 (Geppert 2010p. 213).

Hur, Fernsebner and Hotta-Lister read the fair through Asian participation. Hur judges China's performance successful to some degree (Hurp. 121). Fernsebner treats 1905 as the end of Customs Service management of Chinese displays (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). Hotta-Lister reads Japan's decision to take part as the result of persuasion by the Belgian authorities (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). Benedict and Peterson mention the fair only in passing (Benedict 1983p. 40; Peterson 2020p. 73).

Japan

Japan joined the fair only after Belgian exhibition authorities persuaded its government, although the war with Russia had made participation less welcome (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). Hotta-Lister adds that the 1904 St Louis fair, held under similar conditions, had proved more fruitful than first thought (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216). An appendix list of Japan's exhibitions gives 40,000 against the Liège fair, in a column headed in yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222). Stanard notes that Russia and Japan, at war with each other, both took part, and that Prince and Princess Arisugawa of Japan visited (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 179; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180). Gendron says the Japanese section, decided late and despite the war with Russia, sent exhibits and won medals, and that Japan received much praise (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 188). Peterson lists Liège among the earlier fairs at which Japanese decorative-arts vendors had won awards, alongside Chicago, Paris and the 1910 Japan-British Exposition (Peterson 2020p. 73).

China

According to Fernsebner, employees of the Qing Dynasty's Imperial Customs Service managed China's displays at fairs from Vienna in 1873 until the Liège exposition of 1905 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). The service's head, Robert Hart, served from 1863 to 1910 and was known for exacting instructions (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174). Hur adds that Hart still collected items in China for Liège, and that Yang Zhaoyun, the Chinese minister to Belgium, was appointed president of the Chinese commission (Hurp. 121; Hurp. 121). Hur writes that "this was the first case in which a Chinese civil official, not a member of the Manchu imperial family, was in full charge of the Chinese exhibit at the foreign exhibition" (Hurp. 121). Chinese participants, including 17 individual merchants, won 100 awards, roughly as many as Britain, America, Austria and Italy, which Hur takes as a sign of partial success (Hurp. 121; Hurp. 121).

Before the fair, Chinese students in Belgium wrote a letter of caution to merchants, carried by the newspaper Jingzhong ribao, which told merchants that the students had means to deal with Yang if he opposed them (Hurp. 121; Hurp. 121). The letter invoked Huang Kaijia, suspected of embezzlement at St Louis, though Hur notes that the suspicion had not been corroborated, and Hur reads the students' stance as revealing distrust of Chinese officials (Hurp. 122; Hurp. 122). An article in Dongfang Zazhi, which Hur quotes on the Milan exposition, credits the students: "Last year the Liége Exposition saw few nationally embarrassing incidents thanks to the eager intervention of the group of Chinese students in Belgium for Chinese officials and merchants at the exhibition" (Hurp. 123).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Belgian national celebration — The fair marked the 75th anniversary of Belgian independence and the fortieth year of Leopold II's accession, which Stanard and Gendron both record (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 178; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 187).
  • Imperial and colonial display — Stanard calls the Congo showing minor beside French displays, while Greenhalgh and Roche place colonial and imperial sections at the centre of Belgium's claims (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Roche 2000p. 58).
  • Historicism and Old Liège — The reconstructed "Old Liège" section was among the most popular attractions and part of a tradition that Depelchin traces back to Antwerp in 1894 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 180; Filipová 2015p. 190).
  • Fairs outside capitals — Geppert treats Liège and Barcelona 1888 as the first European international expositions held outside a capital (Geppert 2010p. 222).
  • Chinese commission and merchants — Hur describes a Chinese commission led by a civil official, a merchant contingent and student intervention (Hurp. 121; Hurp. 121).
  • Japan's participation — Japan joined after persuasion, despite its war with Russia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 216).
  • Customs control of Chinese displays — Fernsebner dates the end of Customs Service management of Chinese displays to Liège (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 174).

Questions for study

  1. Why do the reference works disagree about the size of the grounds, and what does a reported acreage or hectarage tell us about how organisers presented the fair?
  2. How much weight should a study give to the Congo showing, given that Stanard and Gendron assess it differently?
  3. To what extent did Japanese and Chinese participation depend on persuasion by the host, and how far on diplomatic calculation or on the lessons of St Louis?
  4. Did the Liège fair's success or its finances matter more to the organisers of later Belgian fairs?
  5. How do the reconstructed historic streets fit with the argument that fairs claimed modernity while looking to the past?

Hur looks at China's official participation in the Louisiana Purchase Exposition at St. Louis in 1904, the first Qing participation in a Western world fair. The chapter argues that Chinese newspapers read the fair through the Jinruikan trauma of Osaka, so that a run of episodes at St. Louis were framed as national shame. It works through four cases: a portrait of the Empress Dowager painted by the American artist Katharine Carl, which Chinese papers denounced as an insult; a tableau of a bound-foot woman and an opium smoker in the Palace of Liberal Arts; a 'Chinese Village' on the Pike where a bound-foot woman was displayed; and the Japanese section, with its wooden map that included parts of China. Hur also weighs the rumors of embezzlement against Huang Kaijia, the vice commissioner who built the Chinese Pavilion, and finds the charge uncorroborated. The chapter concludes that the fair left a lasting image of official incompetence, which later reappeared at the Liege exposition of 1905.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). On the web

Wikipedia

The Liège International Exposition (French: Exposition universelle et internationale de Liège) of 1905 was a world's fair held in Liège, Belgium, from 27 April to 6 November 1905 just eight years after a previous exposition held in Brussels. Intended to show Liège's industrial importance, the fair also marked 75 years of Belgian independence and 40 years of King Leopold II's reign. The exposition received 7 million visitors, covered 52 acres and made 75,117 Belgian francs.
