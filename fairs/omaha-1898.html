Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition

The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition opened in Omaha, Nebraska, on 1 June 1898 and closed on 1 November, on 184 acres of northern Omaha overlooking the Missouri River (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). Its exhibits were meant to show the history, strengths and future of the states west of the Mississippi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Its Indian Congress, a staged encampment, was to be modelled on colonial exhibits at recent European fairs, according to a senator's 1897 letter (Rydell 1984). Historians read the fair as an early instance of the American exposition used to promote empire, held just after the Spanish-American War (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984).

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the 1893 Chicago exposition. Its backers had watched the Chicago fair succeed, and Omaha's business groups wanted to speed their region's recovery from the 1893 depression (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). At the eighth annual meeting of the Trans-Mississippi Commercial Congress, Dudley Smith drew up a resolution urging federal support for an Omaha fair in 1898, and William Jennings Bryan introduced it (Rydell 1984). Edward Rosewater, owner of the Omaha Bee, proposed Omaha as the site in 1895 and saw the fair as a way of proclaiming the recovery of the region (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 152). The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition Corporation was formed on 25 January 1896 under Gordon Wattles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147).

The organisers wanted the fair to show the region's history in a positive light. The encyclopedia says that "Fair organizers wanted to focus the exposition on the positive outcomes of this history" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). That history included railroad land purchases, excessive homesteading and a failed attempt to turn grazing land into farms (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). Opposition in the Nebraska legislature was strong. The appropriation passed on a seventy-to-twenty vote, but the sum was reduced, and the Populist leader Charles Wooster attacked the Trans-Mississippi Congress (Rydell 1984).

Organisation, finance and site

Five railroads, utility companies, banks, newspapers and breweries invested in the fair, and Gurdon W. Wattles, a railroad investor and bank vice-president, was its president (Rydell 1984). Schoolchildren contributed funds for a Boys' and Girls' Building, which women's clubs placed in the White City in place of a woman's building (Rydell 1984). A board of twenty-seven women presided over that building, a Congress Department and some exhibits of women's work (Weimann 1981p. 597).

The sources differ on the size of the fair. The encyclopedia gives more than 2,500,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147); the dictionary gives 2.7 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170); Rydell gives a final count of 2,718,508 (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia gives the site as 184 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147), while Fair America gives two hundred acres (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The money came out well. The encyclopedia gives a net revenue of $215,812.62 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), and Rydell reports a 92.5 percent dividend to stockholders (Rydell 1984).

Two local architects, Thomas Rogers Kimball and C. Howard Walker, supervised the buildings, and the New York landscape architect Rudolph Ulrich drew up the overall plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). The Grand Court enclosed a lagoon, which the encyclopedia gives as 2,000 feet long (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147), while Rydell describes a half-mile lagoon (Rydell 1984). The buildings broke with the ivory colour of Chicago by painting trim and decoration in colour (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). Frederic W. Taylor, the horticulturist, worked with Ulrich on the grounds (Maloney 2012p. 211).

The Indian Congress

Rosewater proposed an ethnological exhibit of the aboriginal inhabitants of the western hemisphere in August 1897 (Rydell 1984). James Mooney of the Bureau of American Ethnology advised him to show house types on a relief map of nations, rather than a gigantic wigwam holding every tribe of the Americas (Rydell 1984). In December 1897 Senator William V. Allen sent the plan to the federal departments, presenting it on the model of colonial exhibits at recent European fairs, notably France and Belgium (Rydell 1984). Congress made $40,000 available on 30 June 1898 (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia, however, says the Bureau of American Ethnology funded the Congress (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), and Benedict repeats the $40,000 appropriation (Benedict 1983p. 50).

The Congress opened on 4 August under army captain William A. Mercer (Rydell 1984). The sources differ on the number of tribes. The encyclopedia says more than thirty-five tribes, with some five hundred Native Americans, including Geronimo (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), while the dictionary says twenty-three tribes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153).

The sham battles were the leading feature of the exhibit (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the idea came from the Improved Order of Red Men; when their members did not arrive, Mercer improvised a battle with Native performers from the Midway's Wild West show (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the Red Men persuaded the managers to stage the battles (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Mooney complained that the Congress had degenerated into a Wild West show (Rydell 1984). Fair America states the message plainly: "The battles, always won by the dominant white race (or, presumably, the more civilized Indian tribe), underscored the message that Indians were a race in decline" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The dictionary says Mooney tried to separate the exhibit from the Wild West shows, but that it remained a huge success to the end of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153).

Shortly before the Congress opened, James Mooney returned from Oklahoma with 106 Kiowas, their ponies and a Wichita grass house. The exposition management paid for the grass house and a tipi (Rydell 1984). Mooney informed W. J. McGee of the Bureau of American Ethnology that the Congress had become a show "with the sole purpose of increasing gate receipts" (Rydell 1984). Rydell also records Mooney's praise for Rosewater and the exposition's payment of his costs, and his article in the American Anthropologist, which Rydell says raised interest in anthropology (Rydell 1984). Fair America, by contrast, stresses only his dismay at the battles (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Three Native Americans died while the exhibit was open, and one Indian woman attempted suicide. Geronimo came with twenty-one other Apaches, prisoners of war from Fort Sill (Rydell 1984). The American Review of Reviews judged the Congress "something genuine in a wilderness—a howling wilderness—of Midway fakes" (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia presents the Congress as an educational forum, in which reservations were seen as temporary, but says the public preferred staged dances and sham battles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Benedict quotes a Scientific American writer of 1898 who said the government sent Omaha not its most civilised tribes but those it had conquered with the greatest bloodshed (Benedict 1983p. 50p. 51).

The successor fair mobilised another Indian encampment under P. E. Iller, owner of the Willow Springs Distillery (Rydell 1984). The Greater America Exposition closed after about three months as "a huge joke and failure" (Rydell 1984).

The Midway and its peoples

The Midway offered a cabaret of villages and restaurants, among them a German Village, a Moorish Palace, the Streets of Cairo, sideshows and a Chinese Village (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147p. 148). The Afro-American village came straight from the Nashville fair of 1897, and Rydell describes it as presenting Black Americans in slave cabins, with Aunt Jemima serving pancakes in the Home Kitchen (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the Old Plantation was designed to convince visitors that slavery had been a pleasant experience and a positive good for Black people (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh names the fair among Southern fairs with Negro sections, which integrated the black population into Southern life and economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99).

Managers policed the entertainment. The head of the concessions department made the dancers at the Streets of Cairo moderate their act (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153). Children were admitted free on Children's Day, when advertisers handed them cards and badges (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1993p. 26). The Midway was especially popular (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 152).

The Peace Jubilee and imperial politics

During the first half of 1898 the fair nearly disappeared from public view while newspapers covered the war with Spain (Rydell 1984). The fair then became the focus of the national Peace Jubilee. President McKinley visited in October, and the dictionary says nearly 100,000 people attended on the day of his visit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153). Rydell says that the directors used the occasion to present the American imperial project. In his opening-day address, John Webster said: "In the fulfillment of our destiny, and to hold trade and commerce within our grasp, we have to work out the problem of universal civilization" (Rydell 1984). The sham battle staged for McKinley had Indians fighting one another, which Rydell reads as showing both the degeneracy of Native Americans and the paternal role of government (Rydell 1984).

Aftermath and legacy

The encyclopedia says the fair's remaining holdings were sold for $17,500 to the Greater American Exposition, which hoped to continue the fair's success but lasted three months (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). The site became Kountze Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Rydell says the federal government did not take part in the successor (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the Omaha fair was followed by Buffalo in 1901 and St. Louis in 1904 as the first of a series of expositions designed to show the value of colonialism (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Buffalo Bill day and the shows of war

Buffalo Bill's special day, in late August, was built around a parade billed as the greatest ethnological event in the history of the world. Cody led it, followed by Cuban troops who had fought Spain, Midway villagers and Native Americans from the Indian Congress (Rydell 1984). The Omaha Bee presented the procession as a lesson in the standing of Uncle Sam among the powers (Rydell 1984). The Giant Tunnel of the Scenic Railway carried a representation of the Battle of Manila on its walls, and Henry Roltair's show ran from the sinking of the Maine to a portrait of McKinley (Rydell 1984). Fair America adds a mural of the battle of Manila Bay and a multimedia portrayal of the war in Cuba (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Shortly before opening day a Salvation Army lieutenant was arrested for trying to destroy nude statues on the Arch of States, and was fined $75 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Money, contracts and the federal purse

Badger's appendix of federal appropriations to fairs up to 1904 lists $240,000 for Omaha in 1898 (Badger 1979p. 132). Its source is a Treasury letter to the Senate of December 1904 (Badger 1979p. 132). The same table gives $5,840,329.64 for Chicago in 1893, $200,000 for Atlanta, $130,000 for Nashville, $1,015,000 for Buffalo and $11,427,500 for St Louis (Badger 1979p. 132). Badger does not say what the sum paid for. Rydell's $40,000 covers the Indian Congress alone (Rydell 1984), so the two figures may not be comparable. Rosewater told the Interior secretary that the directors would use their own money for the display if Congress delayed (Rydell 1984). The Union Pacific gave money both to the exposition and to the Chinese Village (Rydell 1984).

The press and publicity

Rosewater ran the exposition's Department of Publicity as well as owning the Omaha Bee (Rydell 1984). The Bee's rival, the World-Herald, was edited by Gilbert Hitchcock, and Alfers traces the rivalry between the two papers through the fair's political debate and scandal (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 154). William Kahrl, writing in 1983, offers a contrasting view of Rosewater's role (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 154).

Visitors and their impressions

Rydell quotes a Harper's Weekly correspondent who saw men and women stand stupefied at the Grand Court entrance, and who found most visitors to be hard-working people (Rydell 1984). At dusk thousands of incandescent lights came on, and a searchlight shone from the torch of the statue of Liberty above the Government Building (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the lighting impressed French planners, who adopted the idea for Paris in 1900 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

On Children's Day many poorly clad children, some after long rides, arrived chilled and shivering (Rydell 1984). Before the fair opened, the Bee had children clean the city's streets for the expected crowds (Rydell 1984). Groups of children were expected to learn and sing a song written for them before they walked the grounds (Rydell 1984).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell argues that from the day it opened, the fair gave mass support to the government's imperial policies (Rydell 1984). Rydell reads the Indian Congress as offering Native Americans two futures, assimilation or extinction, and as presenting federal government as in control of the matter (Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle read the fair as the first of the imperial-era fairs that ran from 1898 to 1916 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The encyclopedia calls the fair an essentially Western enterprise and stresses regional boosterism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), while the dictionary, following Alfers, reads its early success as a triumph for the entire country: "The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition was at first planned as a symbol of progress achieved in only one area of the country" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153). The reference works and the monographs therefore differ over whether the fair was mainly regional or mainly imperial. Houze places the Indian Congress among ethnographic village displays that paralleled those in Central Europe (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). Hur treats the Chinese exhibits as exotic and often humiliating displays (Hurp. 20). Benedict argues that imperial powers had different aims in their colonial displays, and that these aims changed over time (Benedict 1983p. 51). Samuel says most would now argue that the Native Americans in Wild West shows were exploited (Samuel 2007).

The Chinese Village

The best-documented Asian display at Omaha was the Chinese Village. Rydell says it was organised by Hip Lung, a Chicago merchant, with the help of Hong Sling, the Union Pacific Railroad's Chinese agent, and that it followed similar villages at earlier fairs (Rydell 1984). The Union Pacific gave money both to the exposition and to the village (Rydell 1984). A separate artisans' exhibit was promoted by the Wong Chin Foo Trading Company of Chicago, which Rydell says was backed by wealthy Chinese in Chicago, New York and Hong Kong (Rydell 1984). Permission was given to bring 238 artisans to the fair, and by the close all of them had vanished (Rydell 1984).

The artisans' exhibit never materialised. Wan Loy, an agent of the company, said none of the Chinese brought to the fair had intended to return to China, and the display came down to a small curio store (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the store served trading companies in Chicago and New York as a front for getting round the immigration restriction laws (Rydell 1984). The advertising had promised visitors a display of Chinese cunning (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell argues that such stereotypes let the promoters encourage hopes of a China market and secure cheap labour (Rydell 1984). Hur uses the same village as an example of the Chinese presented as exotic and often humiliating objects in the entertainment sections of early world's fairs (Hurp. 20). Hur adds that foreign staff of the body abbreviated IMCS, sent to the fairs, did not challenge such displays, because the entertainment sections were arranged by individual promoters (Hurp. 20).

The Philippine village

Rydell says the exposition's official historian recorded a Philippine Village on the entertainment street. It probably consisted of artefacts until late in the season, when George D. Steele brought sixteen Manila warriors to Omaha on the way to Washington (Rydell 1984). The World-Herald reported that some of the party had cannibalistic tendencies (Rydell 1984). Fair America says that the natives were imported late in the run, and that the publicity hinted some of them were cannibals (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Omaha Bee described Filipinos as untruthful and dishonest by nature, and Rydell argues that the fair's directors helped make the annexation debate a matter of race, with national Indian policy as its frame (Rydell 1984). Pony Moore, who had managed the short-lived Philippine Village at both Omaha expositions, later took a village of Filipinos to Buffalo (Rydell 1984). Fair America says that within months U.S. forces were fighting an insurrection, and that mock Philippine villages became fixtures of American fairs (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Japan and other Asian presence

The texts record no Japanese, Indian or Southeast Asian exhibit at Omaha apart from the Philippine village and the Chinese artisans, and they name no Asian commissioner or official. Rydell says that most foreign nations declined to take part because they doubted Omaha could hold a world's fair, so that the Midway concessions supplied much of the claimed international participation (Rydell 1984). Rydell quotes Lanier, who named Chinese, Japanese and other Eastern people among the Midway examples that helped Americans understand other races (Rydell 1984). Among the Asian individuals the texts name at Omaha, only Wan Loy is recorded as speaking about the fair (Rydell 1984).

The Chinese artisans and the immigration argument

The Wong Chin Foo company presented its artisans' exhibit partly as evidence, the press explained, that Chinese from the north, unlike those from the south, would make desirable immigrants and good workers (Rydell 1984). The promoters had used Chinese performers before. For the Nashville fair of 1897, Chinese from a Chinese Village concession toured Tennessee on an exposition train (Rydell 1993p. 24).

The Philippine village and the press

In July the World-Herald had praised Admiral Dewey for requisitioning sixty thousand pounds of soap for the Philippines, arguing that soap and the Bible went hand in hand in developing backward races (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — The fair is read as an early instance of the imperial exposition, with the Indian Congress and the Midway as its lessons (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Sham battles and the Indian Congress — The mock battles are the feature most often discussed; Fair America and Rydell read them as showing Native Americans in decline, and the dictionary notes the concern over their authenticity (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Midway peoples and race — The Midway's villages, from the Old Plantation to the Chinese Village, are read as racial stereotypes that served commerce and labour (Rydell 1984; Greenhalgh 1988; Hur; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Regional boosterism and finance — The encyclopedia and dictionary treat the fair as a regional enterprise that made money, and disagree over its size (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Politics and the Peace Jubilee — The fair was tied to the war with Spain and to McKinley's visit, and Populist opposition shaped its finance (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Women and children as audiences — Women's boards and children's days show how the fair addressed these groups (Weimann 1981; Rydell 1993; Rydell 1984).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle examine how American fairs from 1898 to 1915 presented the new overseas empire as natural and the racial hierarchy that justified it. They begin with the Trans-Mississippi Exposition in Omaha, where an Indian Congress, staged battles and an Old Plantation Village taught visitors about conquest and slavery, and move to Buffalo in 1901, where a color scheme ranked races from dark to light and a Filipino village cast American rule as uplift. St. Louis in 1904 receives the most space, for its anthropology displays and the Olympic events held alongside the fair. The authors then cover the Portland and Seattle fairs, which relied on Igorot and Eskimo villages, and the 1915 fairs in San Francisco, with its eugenics congresses, and San Diego, which reinvented the city's Spanish past and displayed human evolution. The evidence comes from fair records, newspapers and visitors' recollections. The chapter argues that these fairs were designed to show the desirability of colonialism and to justify racial hierarchy, and it closes by noting that the war which followed would reshape the next generation of fairs.

Rydell devotes a full chapter to the 1898 Omaha fair and argues that it was the start of the imperial era of American expositions. He reads the white Grand Court and the Midway's ethnic villages as a display of civilisation as progress, and he reads the Indian Congress, shaped by Bureau of American Ethnology involvement and sham battles, as presenting Native Americans as a vanishing race while confirming federal Indian policy. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Weimann's study recounts the Woman's Building at the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 in Chicago, and the Board of Lady Managers that ran it under Bertha Palmer. Its argument is that the building was a real achievement for women but a constrained one: Congress created the Board with almost no defined duties, it depended on male commissioners and the Fair's architects, and it was split between clubwomen, suffragists and class and sectional loyalties. The book moves in rough chronological order, from the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876 and Chicago's bid for the Fair, through the Board's quarrels, its building, art, library, congresses and exhibits, to the aftermath of 1893 and later women's buildings up to Chicago in 1933. Its scope is American women's organised work at one fair, with the wider world entering through foreign commissions and exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia enter the book mainly as part of the Board's foreign work, and the coverage is occasional and scattered. It is concentrated in Chapter VII, 'Circling the Globe', and Chapter XI, 'Women in the White City'. In Chapter VII, Japan's women's committee is described as formed under the Empress, who paid for it, while China's participation fails: Palmer's hope for a semi-official Chinese women's committee, with Lord Li's wife in charge, comes to nothing, and only a few embroideries and weavings arrive without a representative. Chapter XI describes the Japanese exhibit, funded by the Empress and run by Princess Yasu Mori, with rooms furnished by forty-four women artisans, a Japanese Parlor filled by the commissioner, and the Siamese pavilion, a reproduction of a Buddhist temple with heirlooms of the Queen of Siam. Ceylon appears alongside as a contrast. Elsewhere Asia is passing material: Siamese cradles in the Children's Building nursery (Chapter XIII), Japanese and Chinese embroidery praised by the Committee on Awards (Chapter XV), the Japanese book Nippon Fajin and Japan's statistics in the library (Chapter XIV), the Siamese queen's album and the Siamese commissioner's wife (Chapter XXI), and a Japanese temperance bell (Chapter XIX). Missionary societies in the Organizations room are said to uplift the Chinese and Japanese, which the book does not examine. Asian women are almost never voices in the book, and colonial display of Southeast Asia does not feature.

Wikipedia

The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition was a world's fair held in Omaha, Nebraska, from June 1 to November 1, 1898. Its goal was to showcase the development of the entire West from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Coast. The Indian Congress was held concurrently. Over 2.6 million people came to Omaha to view the 4,062 exhibits during the five months of the Exposition. President William McKinley and William Jennings Bryan were among the dignitaries who attended at the invitation of Gurdon Wattles, the event's leader. A hundred thousand people assembled on the plaza to hear them speak. The Expo stretched over a 180-acre (0.73 km2) tract in North Omaha and featured a 2,000-foot-long (610 m) lagoon encircled by 21 classical buildings that featured fine and modern products from around the world.
