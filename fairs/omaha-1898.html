Omaha · 1898

Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition opened in Omaha, Nebraska, on 1 June 1898 and closed on 1 November, on 184 acres of northern Omaha overlooking the Missouri River [35, l. 8005; 8008]. Its exhibits were meant to show the history, strengths and future of the states west of the Mississippi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Its Indian Congress, a staged encampment, was to be modelled on colonial exhibits at recent European fairs, according to a senator's 1897 letter (Rydell 1984). Historians read the fair as an early instance of the American exposition used to promote empire, held just after the Spanish-American War (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984).

In depth: what our sources say

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the 1893 Chicago exposition. Its backers had watched the Chicago fair succeed, and Omaha's business groups wanted to speed their region's recovery from the 1893 depression (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). At the eighth annual meeting of the Trans-Mississippi Commercial Congress, Dudley Smith drew up a resolution urging federal support for an Omaha fair in 1898, and William Jennings Bryan introduced it (Rydell 1984). Edward Rosewater, owner of the Omaha Bee, proposed Omaha as the site in 1895 and saw the fair as a way of proclaiming the recovery of the region [39, l. 6875; 6877]. The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition Corporation was formed on 25 January 1896 under Gordon Wattles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147).

The organisers wanted the fair to show the region's history in a positive light. The encyclopedia says that "Fair organizers wanted to focus the exposition on the positive outcomes of this history" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). That history included railroad land purchases, excessive homesteading and a failed attempt to turn grazing land into farms (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). Opposition in the Nebraska legislature was strong. The appropriation passed on a seventy-to-twenty vote, but the sum was reduced, and the Populist leader Charles Wooster attacked the Trans-Mississippi Congress [02, l. 6762; 6767].

Organisation, finance and site

Five railroads, utility companies, banks, newspapers and breweries invested in the fair, and Gurdon W. Wattles, a railroad investor and bank vice-president, was its president [02, l. 6742; 6743]. Schoolchildren contributed funds for a Boys' and Girls' Building, which women's clubs placed in the White City in place of a woman's building (Rydell 1984). A board of twenty-seven women presided over that building, a Congress Department and some exhibits of women's work (Weimann 1981p. 597).

The sources differ on the size of the fair. The encyclopedia gives more than 2,500,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147); the dictionary gives 2.7 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170); Rydell gives a final count of 2,718,508 (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia gives the site as 184 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147), while Fair America gives two hundred acres (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The money came out well. The encyclopedia gives a net revenue of $215,812.62 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), and Rydell reports a 92.5 percent dividend to stockholders (Rydell 1984).

Two local architects, Thomas Rogers Kimball and C. Howard Walker, supervised the buildings, and the New York landscape architect Rudolph Ulrich drew up the overall plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). The Grand Court enclosed a lagoon, which the encyclopedia gives as 2,000 feet long (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147), while Rydell describes a half-mile lagoon (Rydell 1984). The buildings broke with the ivory colour of Chicago by painting trim and decoration in colour (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 147). Frederic W. Taylor, the horticulturist, worked with Ulrich on the grounds (Maloney 2012p. 211).

The Indian Congress

Rosewater proposed an ethnological exhibit of the aboriginal inhabitants of the western hemisphere in August 1897 (Rydell 1984). James Mooney of the Bureau of American Ethnology advised him to show house types on a relief map of nations, rather than a gigantic wigwam holding every tribe of the Americas (Rydell 1984). In December 1897 Senator William V. Allen sent the plan to the federal departments, presenting it on the model of colonial exhibits at recent European fairs, notably France and Belgium (Rydell 1984). Congress made $40,000 available on 30 June 1898 (Rydell 1984). The encyclopedia, however, says the Bureau of American Ethnology funded the Congress (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), and Benedict repeats the $40,000 appropriation (Benedict 1983p. 50).

The Congress opened on 4 August under army captain William A. Mercer [02, l. 6813; 6820]. The sources differ on the number of tribes. The encyclopedia says more than thirty-five tribes, with some five hundred Native Americans, including Geronimo [35, l. 8088; 8090], while the dictionary says twenty-three tribes (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153).

The sham battles were the leading feature of the exhibit (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the idea came from the Improved Order of Red Men; when their members did not arrive, Mercer improvised a battle with Native performers from the Midway's Wild West show [02, l. 6832; 6834]. Fair America says the Red Men persuaded the managers to stage the battles (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Mooney complained that the Congress had degenerated into a Wild West show (Rydell 1984). Fair America states the message plainly: "The battles, always won by the dominant white race (or, presumably, the more civilized Indian tribe), underscored the message that Indians were a race in decline" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The dictionary says Mooney tried to separate the exhibit from the Wild West shows, but that it remained a huge success to the end of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153).

The Midway and its peoples

The Midway offered a cabaret of villages and restaurants, among them a German Village, a Moorish Palace, the Streets of Cairo, sideshows and a Chinese Village [35, l. 8048; 8057]. The Afro-American village came straight from the Nashville fair of 1897, and Rydell describes it as presenting Black Americans in slave cabins, with Aunt Jemima serving pancakes in the Home Kitchen (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the Old Plantation was designed to convince visitors that slavery had been a pleasant experience and a positive good for Black people (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh names the fair among Southern fairs with Negro sections, which integrated the black population into Southern life and economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99).

Managers policed the entertainment. The head of the concessions department made the dancers at the Streets of Cairo moderate their act (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153). Children were admitted free on Children's Day, when advertisers handed them cards and badges (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1993p. 26). The Midway was especially popular (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 152).

The Peace Jubilee and imperial politics

During the first half of 1898 the fair nearly disappeared from public view while newspapers covered the war with Spain (Rydell 1984). The fair then became the focus of the national Peace Jubilee. President McKinley visited in October, and the dictionary says nearly 100,000 people attended on the day of his visit [39, l. 6925; 6929]. Rydell says that the directors used the occasion to present the American imperial project. In his opening-day address, John Webster said: "In the fulfillment of our destiny, and to hold trade and commerce within our grasp, we have to work out the problem of universal civilization" (Rydell 1984). The sham battle staged for McKinley had Indians fighting one another, which Rydell reads as showing both the degeneracy of Native Americans and the paternal role of government [02, l. 6936; 6937].

Aftermath and legacy

The encyclopedia says the fair's remaining holdings were sold for $17,500 to the Greater American Exposition, which hoped to continue the fair's success but lasted three months [35, l. 8064; 8068]. The site became Kountze Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148). Rydell says the federal government did not take part in the successor (Rydell 1984). Fair America says the Omaha fair was followed by Buffalo in 1901 and St. Louis in 1904 as the first of a series of expositions designed to show the value of colonialism [38, l. 1908; 1912].

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell argues that from the day it opened, the fair gave mass support to the government's imperial policies (Rydell 1984). Rydell reads the Indian Congress as offering Native Americans two futures, assimilation or extinction, and as presenting federal government as in control of the matter [02, l. 6870; 6937]. Rydell, Findling and Pelle read the fair as the first of the imperial-era fairs that ran from 1898 to 1916 [38, l. 1807; 1908]. The encyclopedia calls the fair an essentially Western enterprise and stresses regional boosterism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 148), while the dictionary, following Alfers, reads its early success as a triumph for the entire country: "The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition was at first planned as a symbol of progress achieved in only one area of the country" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 153). The reference works and the monographs therefore differ over whether the fair was mainly regional or mainly imperial. Houze places the Indian Congress among ethnographic village displays that paralleled those in Central Europe (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). Hur treats the Chinese exhibits as exotic and often humiliating displays (Hurp. 20). Benedict argues that imperial powers had different aims in their colonial displays, and that these aims changed over time (Benedict 1983p. 51). Samuel says most would now argue that the Native Americans in Wild West shows were exploited (Samuel 2007).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The Chinese Village

The best-documented Asian display at Omaha was the Chinese Village. Rydell says it was organised by Hip Lung, a Chicago merchant, with the help of Hong Sling, the Union Pacific Railroad's Chinese agent, and that it followed similar villages at earlier fairs (Rydell 1984). The Union Pacific gave money both to the exposition and to the village (Rydell 1984). A separate artisans' exhibit was promoted by the Wong Chin Foo Trading Company of Chicago, which Rydell says was backed by wealthy Chinese in Chicago, New York and Hong Kong (Rydell 1984). Permission was given to bring 238 artisans to the fair, and by the close all of them had vanished (Rydell 1984).

The artisans' exhibit never materialised. Wan Loy, an agent of the company, said none of the Chinese brought to the fair had intended to return to China, and the display came down to a small curio store (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the store served trading companies in Chicago and New York as a front for getting round the immigration restriction laws (Rydell 1984). The advertising had promised visitors a display of Chinese cunning (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell argues that such stereotypes let the promoters encourage hopes of a China market and secure cheap labour (Rydell 1984). Hur uses the same village as an example of the Chinese presented as exotic and often humiliating objects in the entertainment sections of early world's fairs [16, l. 1151; 1161]. Hur adds that foreign staff of the body abbreviated IMCS, sent to the fairs, did not challenge such displays, because the entertainment sections were arranged by individual promoters (Hurp. 20).

The Philippine village

Rydell says the exposition's official historian recorded a Philippine Village on the entertainment street. It probably consisted of artefacts until late in the season, when George D. Steele brought sixteen Manila warriors to Omaha on the way to Washington [02, l. 6911; 6912]. The World-Herald reported that some of the party had cannibalistic tendencies (Rydell 1984). Fair America says that the natives were imported late in the run, and that the publicity hinted some of them were cannibals (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Omaha Bee described Filipinos as untruthful and dishonest by nature, and Rydell argues that the fair's directors helped make the annexation debate a matter of race, with national Indian policy as its frame [02, l. 6914; 6915]. Pony Moore, who had managed the short-lived Philippine Village at both Omaha expositions, later took a village of Filipinos to Buffalo (Rydell 1984). Fair America says that within months U.S. forces were fighting an insurrection, and that mock Philippine villages became fixtures of American fairs [38, l. 1904; 1906].

Japan and other Asian presence

The texts record no Japanese, Indian or Southeast Asian exhibit at Omaha apart from the Philippine village and the Chinese artisans, and they name no Asian commissioner or official. Rydell says that most foreign nations declined to take part because they doubted Omaha could hold a world's fair, so that the Midway concessions supplied much of the claimed international participation (Rydell 1984). Rydell quotes Lanier, who named Chinese, Japanese and other Eastern people among the Midway examples that helped Americans understand other races (Rydell 1984). Among the Asian individuals the texts name at Omaha, only Wan Loy is recorded as speaking about the fair (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display — The fair is read as an early instance of the imperial exposition, with the Indian Congress and the Midway as its lessons (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Sham battles and the Indian Congress — The mock battles are the feature most often discussed; Fair America and Rydell read them as showing Native Americans in decline, and the dictionary notes the concern over their authenticity (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Midway peoples and race — The Midway's villages, from the Old Plantation to the Chinese Village, are read as racial stereotypes that served commerce and labour (Rydell 1984; Greenhalgh 1988; Hur; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Regional boosterism and finance — The encyclopedia and dictionary treat the fair as a regional enterprise that made money, and disagree over its size (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Politics and the Peace Jubilee — The fair was tied to the war with Spain and to McKinley's visit, and Populist opposition shaped its finance (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Women and children as audiences — Women's boards and children's days show how the fair addressed these groups (Weimann 1981; Rydell 1993; Rydell 1984).

Questions for study

  1. How far was the fair a regional enterprise when its best-documented features were built around imperial themes?
  2. What did the sham battles tell visitors about Native Americans, and who decided what counted as authentic?
  3. Why did the Chinese artisans' exhibit fail to show any artisans, and what does that say about immigration law and show business?
  4. Why do the reference works disagree about attendance and site size, and how should a student weigh such figures?
  5. In what ways did the Omaha fair prepare the colonial displays at Buffalo and St. Louis?
  6. Who paid for the Midway villages, and how did their promoters' interests shape what visitors saw?

Central: a book or chapter on this fair 1

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
ch. 4, 'Concomitant to Empire' (index places the chapter at about pp. 105-125)

Rydell devotes a full chapter to the 1898 Omaha fair and argues that it was the start of the imperial era of American expositions. He reads the white Grand Court and the Midway's ethnic villages as a display of civilisation as progress, and he reads the Indian Congress, shaped by Bureau of American Ethnology involvement and sham battles, as presenting Native Americans as a vanishing race while confirming federal Indian policy. He argues that the Chinese Village and the other Midway shows served commercial and labour interests, and that the national Peace Jubilee with President McKinley gave ideological support to imperial policy after the Spanish-American War. Rydell concludes that the fair's success kept the exposition movement a 'concomitant to empire' well into the future, with the Greater America Exposition, Buffalo and St. Louis to follow.

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
ch. 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', pp. 57-59

Rydell, Findling and Pelle treat the Omaha fair as the first of the 'imperial era' expositions of 1898-1916, when American fairs were designed to demonstrate the desirability of colonialism. They argue that the Indian Congress, which turned into a Wild West show of mock battles, presented Native Americans as a race in decline, and they describe the racism of the fair as apparent in the Old Plantation Village, the Chinese Village and the Streets of Cairo. Brief discussion 4

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
p. 50, in Benedict's essay on the anthropology of world's fairs

Benedict uses the Omaha fair's Indian encampment as one example of imperial display at world's fairs. He notes that Congress appropriated $40,000 for a great Indian encampment and quotes Scientific American's view that the government chose not to show its most civilized wards but the tribes it had conquered with the greatest bloodshed. From such displays Benedict concludes that the imperial powers had different aims and that these aims changed over time. He refers readers to Rydell's article for the Omaha display.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
p. 19 (chapter not identified in the extract)

Hur uses the Trans-Mississippi fair's Chinese Village as a brief example of how Chinese people were put on display as exotic and often humiliating objects in the entertainment sections of early world's fairs. Advertised with the phrase 'sly tricks of the heathen Chinese', the village proved to be little more than a souvenir shop, because the Chinese brought from mainland China had gone into the immigrant labour force. Hur suggests that the foreign IMCS staff sent to the fairs did not challenge such displays, probably because the entertainment sections were arranged by individual promoters and seemed outside their responsibility.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
ch. 'An Exhibitionary Culture', pp. 24 and 26

Rydell uses the Omaha fair to argue that world's fairs were advertisements in themselves. He notes that the Post Office's stamp for the 1898 fair was praised by an Omaha newspaper as giving the fair the prestige of government recognition. He describes how advertisers loaded children with business cards, product literature, pins and badges on Children's Day. For Rydell such examples show how fair promoters borrowed the prestige of government and the methods of advertising, so that advertising came to seem as 'true and natural' to American life as the midway's ethnological exhibits.

Wikipedia

The Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition was a world's fair held in Omaha, Nebraska, from June 1 to November 1, 1898. Its goal was to showcase the development of the entire West from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Coast. The Indian Congress was held concurrently. Over 2.6 million people came to Omaha to view the 4,062 exhibits during the five months of the Exposition. President William McKinley and William Jennings Bryan were among the dignitaries who attended at the invitation of Gurdon Wattles, the event's leader. A hundred thousand people assembled on the plaza to hear them speak. The Expo stretched over a 180-acre (0.73 km2) tract in North Omaha and featured a 2,000-foot-long (610 m) lagoon encircled by 21 classical buildings that featured fine and modern products from around the world.
