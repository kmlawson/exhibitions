Proposed national exposition in China (Zhang Zhenxun proposal)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther reading

In 1904 Zhang Zhenxun, an overseas Chinese merchant from Southeast Asia, had an audience with the Empress Dowager and asked her to hold a grand-scale national exposition in China (Hurp. 125). This was a proposal, not a known exhibition. The passage gives no city, organiser or date for the event, and no text in the dossier reports that it was held (Hurp. 125). The Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910 at Nanjing was a separate event, which Hur calls the first national exposition hosted by China (Hurp. 124p. 125).

Background and aims

Hur gives the proposal a short passage. The aim, as he reports it, was to broaden the perspective of the Chinese people and to stimulate the economy (Hurp. 125). The passage names no city, site or organiser for the proposed exposition (Hurp. 125). Hur places it beside local product exhibitions at city and provincial level in Tianjin, Wuhan and Chengdu from 1907, which he describes as independent events (Hurp. 125).

Earlier advocacy and the 1908 plan

Hur says that the idea of a national exposition in China had appeared before Duanfang's plan, and refers the reader back to Chapter One (Hurp. 125). In Chapter One he discusses the proposal of Zheng Guanying for exhibits in China and calls Zheng the first advocate of holding exhibitions in China (Hurp. 31). Zhang Zhenxun is not named there (Hurp. 31). Duanfang, Governor-General of Liang-Jiang and Minister-superintendent of Trade in the South, first proposed the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in 1908 (Hurp. 125). Chen Qi, manager of the Jiangning Park Office, and Yan Qizhang had already proposed a national exposition on a site of about 600 mu around Jiangning Park in Nanjing that year (Hurp. 125). Duanfang replied that the first exposition should be simple and modelled on Japanese exhibitions (Hurp. 126). On 7 December 1908 he and Chen Qitai, Governor of Jiangsu, submitted a petition for it to the Qing court (Hurp. 126). Hur does not say that the 1904 proposal led to these plans (Hurp. 125).

What became of the proposal

Hur does not say what became of Zhang's proposal (Hurp. 125). The exposition he treats as China's first national one is the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, which he calls the first national exposition hosted by China and the first and last international exhibition of the Qing dynasty (Hurp. 123p. 124). Its planned site was the area around Jiangning Park in Nanjing (Hurp. 125).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Early calls for a national exposition — Hur places the 1904 audience before Duanfang's plan and refers back to Chapter One for earlier advocacy (Hurp. 125p. 31).
  • Duanfang and the 1908 Nanyang plan — Duanfang first proposed the Nanyang exposition and asked for a simple first show modelled on Japan (Hurp. 125p. 126).
  • Overseas Chinese merchants — Hur identifies the proposer as an overseas Chinese merchant from Southeast Asia (Hurp. 125).
  • Exhibitions and the economy — the stated aim of the 1904 proposal was to broaden the outlook of the people and stimulate the economy (Hurp. 125).

Questions for study

  1. What did Zhang Zhenxun's audience with the Empress Dowager lead to, and which records would show whether any plan followed from it? (Hurp. 125)
  2. Hur refers back to Chapter One for the earlier idea but names Zheng Guanying there, not Zhang. How does that choice shape his account of where the Nanyang exposition came from? (Hurp. 125p. 31)
  3. Duanfang asked for a simple first exposition modelled on Japan. Why might a Qing official have preferred that model? (Hurp. 126)
  4. The 1904 proposal and the 1908 plan both aimed at a national exposition. What separates a request from a merchant from a plan put forward by a provincial governor-general? (Hurp. 125)

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
1.3Living Authenticity: The World’s Fair and the ZooAaron Santessop. 41Asia

Santesso argues that the world's fair and the zoo grew out of the same desire for authenticity, and that the zoo's 'African village' is the lasting successor to the exotic villages of the fairs. The chapter begins with the close ties between the two institutions, from the Philadelphia Zoo and the Centennial Exhibition of 1876 to the origins of the St Louis Zoo in the 1904 fair. It then reconsiders the Dahomey Village at Chicago in 1893 and the case of Ota Benga, which are usually read only as expressions of power and racism. Santesso adds that fair visitors were preoccupied with the artificial, as at the Corliss steam engine and the unfinished Chinese exhibit in Philadelphia, where living workmen supplied a sense of authenticity. The chapter traces this through the German zoo directors Bodinus and Hagenbeck, whose animals in exotic replicas and living panoramas were copied at colonial fairs in Paris and at Chicago in 1933. It ends with today's zoo 'African villages', where visitors take the place of the people once displayed and so find themselves inside the living panorama.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
AbstractAsia

Hur's abstract summarises a dissertation on the Qing government's attempt to stage modern statehood at world exhibitions and how that attempt was undermined. The Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan and Chinese reformers each shaped China's image, and criticism of the Qing grew after Osaka in 1903 and St Louis in 1904. It ends with the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which Chinese intellectuals came to support once the government promised constitutional reform.

In the contents of this work

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

ThreeRecurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904p. 86Asia

Hur looks at China's official participation in the Louisiana Purchase Exposition at St. Louis in 1904, the first Qing participation in a Western world fair. The chapter argues that Chinese newspapers read the fair through the Jinruikan trauma of Osaka, so that a run of episodes at St. Louis were framed as national shame. It works through four cases: a portrait of the Empress Dowager painted by the American artist Katharine Carl, which Chinese papers denounced as an insult; a tableau of a bound-foot woman and an opium smoker in the Palace of Liberal Arts; a 'Chinese Village' on the Pike where a bound-foot woman was displayed; and the Japanese section, with its wooden map that included parts of China. Hur also weighs the rumors of embezzlement against Huang Kaijia, the vice commissioner who built the Chinese Pavilion, and finds the charge uncorroborated. The chapter concludes that the fair left a lasting image of official incompetence, which later reappeared at the Liege exposition of 1905.

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
9When the local is the global: case studies in early twentieth-century Chinese exposition projectsSusan R. Fernsebnerp. 173Asia

Fernsebner follows Chinese exposition managers, mostly state officials and commercial elites, through three fairs: St. Louis in 1904, China's first national exposition at Nanjing in 1910, and the Panama-Pacific fair in San Francisco in 1915. Her argument is that these elites sought control over how China was represented. At St. Louis they protested against displays arranged by the Western-staffed Qing Customs Service, including bound-foot shoes and opium pipes. At Nanjing they used the fair to promote economic nationalism and to train a 'new people', drawing on provincial displays of soap, furniture and textiles and on a hall for overseas Chinese. The San Francisco section centres on a complaint about the China Pavilion's teahouse and on 'Underground Chinatown', an attraction that the Six Companies and commissioner-general Chen Qi protested and that closed in March 1915. The chapter concludes that these disputes joined local, transnational and global concerns, and that the same dynamics recur in China's later hosting of Expo 2010.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
