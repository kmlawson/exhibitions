World's Columbian Exposition

The World's Columbian Exposition opened in Jackson Park, on Chicago's lakeshore, on 1 May 1893 and closed on 30 October (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 130). It marked the 400th anniversary of Columbus's arrival in the Americas (Samuel 2007). Its neoclassical White City stood beside the Midway Plaisance, a commercial strip of amusements and ethnological villages (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Historians use the pairing as a test case for how American fairs joined progress, nationalism, race and mass entertainment, and the Midway model spread to later fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 120; Badger 1979p. 125).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair began as a contest with New York. Dr A. W. Harlan's letter in the Chicago Times in 1882 started the Chicago proposal, and a club resolution of 7 July 1888 set up a planning committee (Badger 1979p. 44). New York launched its own bid in July 1889, after the Paris Exposition of 1889 had set a standard Americans felt they had to exceed (Badger 1979p. 46). The texts differ on how Chicago won. Badger gives a February 1890 vote on the eighth roll call, with 157 votes (Badger 1979p. 51); Greenhalgh describes a second ballot after a first in which Chicago had 115 votes to New York's 70 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 40); Rydell, Findling and Pelle date the vote to April 1890 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger dates the fair act to 28 April 1890 (Badger 1979p. 52).

Advisers urged novelty, diversity and an educational, international character (Badger 1979p. 53). G. Brown Goode of the Smithsonian planned the classification so that the fair would act as an encyclopedia of human progress (Rydell 1984). Burnham, the director of works, hoped the fair would make the city a monumental place for elites and middle classes (Gilbert 1991p. 78).

Organisation and finance

A local corporation, chartered in August 1889 with $5 million of authorised capital, ran the fair alongside a National Commission created by federal law (Badger 1979p. 49). It sold nearly 30,000 stock subscriptions, which Rydell (1984) puts at $5,407,350, from backers including Philip Armour, Marshall Field, Cyrus McCormick, George Pullman and Gustavus Swift (Rydell 1984). When Congress demanded more, the Chicago committee pledged $10 million without consulting Chicagoans (Badger 1979p. 51). Greenhalgh says the company stayed close to insolvency up to 1893 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 38).

The federal grant of $2.5 million came as souvenir coins, and without it the fair could not have opened (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 126; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 119). The Promotion Department under Moses P. Handy mailed material to some 50,000 organisations, newspapers and magazines, and R. E. A. Dorr estimated that a third of press coverage was written by the department itself (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 119).

The profit figures disagree. Allwood, Allan and Reid give $807,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207); Rydell, Findling and Pelle give $1.4 million (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger says the corporation repaid its private investors with a small surplus and collected all but 7 per cent of its stock (Badger 1979p. 109).

Site and architecture

Frederick Law Olmsted laid out the grounds, on marshland on Lake Michigan, and Burnham directed the building work (Allwood et al. 2001p. 62; Gilbert 1991p. 87). The texts give different areas: 633 acres of grounds in Whitfield (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 84), 686 acres in Rydell, Findling and Pelle (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). In Maloney's account the fairgrounds were the largest effort of their time to shape an exposition landscape out of undeveloped land (Maloney 2012p. 79). Gilbert reports that Olmsted had to accept a Japanese garden and temple on the Wooded Island, the island in the lagoon he wished to keep as a natural retreat (Gilbert 1991p. 92).

Eastern architects meeting in December 1890 adopted neoclassical styles and a common cornice height. After Root's death in January 1891, the architects painted the buildings round the Court of Honor white, which gave the fair its nickname (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Sullivan later complained that this set American architecture back fifty years (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Badger calls the White City an illusion, since its neoclassical architecture pointed to no future for American building (Badger 1979p. 127). Greenhalgh credits the fair's plaster facings with setting the model for later fairs (Greenhalgh 1988p. 46). Beaux-arts, a French academic classicism, became the pattern for public buildings across the United States (Benedict 1983p. 53), and the fair is often credited with starting the City Beautiful movement of planned, monumental cities (Maloney 2012p. 79).

Exhibits and attractions

The Midway Plaisance, a boulevard linking Jackson and Washington parks, was added after Philadelphia's 1876 fair had shown the appeal of an entertainment area (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Showmen pressed for space there, and the amusements passed to Sol Bloom, who replaced the anthropologist F. W. Putnam (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 127; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 119). The Ferris Wheel was 264 feet across in Badger's account (Badger 1979p. 108) and 250 feet in Allwood, Allan and Reid's (Allwood et al. 2001p. 68). Burris gives its cost as $350,000 (Burris 2001p. 99).

Gilbert argues that the Midway became an equal centre rather than a sideshow. Bloom's Middle Eastern area, with the Ferris Wheel at its heart, matched the White City in profit and attention, so that "two Chicago fairs coexisting in Jackson Park" resulted (Gilbert 1991p. 15; Gilbert 1991p. 111). Little Egypt's belly dancing was the sensation of the fair (Benedict 1983p. 59). Hinsley shows how the Midway sold the exotic: some 600 photograph albums were published, and entertainers earned well, Turks $200 to $300 and dancing girls at least $500 (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Peoples and colonies on display

The villages were ranked, in Snider's phrase, as a sliding scale of humanity, with German and Irish villages nearest the White City and Dahomeyans and North American Indians at the far end (Rydell 1984). Putnam and Franz Boas ran the anthropology exhibits, and the Anthropology Building was meant to define the discipline, though its space was cut by more than sixty per cent (Gosden & Knowles 2001; Karp & Lavine 1991). Boas brought fourteen Kwakiutl from British Columbia to perform for study and for the public (Karp & Lavine 1991).

The Dahomey village was intended to convince white fairgoers of their racial superiority (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Gilbert notes that Flinn's guidebook treated its residents as the lowest order of the human family, and that a sign asked visitors not to ask whether they were cannibals (Gilbert 1991p. 116). Native Americans were also displayed. Pratt refused to organise the Indian exhibit, and the Indians were settled in tepees beside Buffalo Bill's show (Greenhalgh 1988p. 100). Burris notes that the Parliament of Religions excluded all Native Americans from its call for the ten great world religions, by category (Burris 2001p. 125).

Black Americans were shut out of power. They had no seats on the central committee, state exhibits were chosen by all-white committees, and segregated exhibits were banned (Roche 2000p. 85). Douglass and Wells published a pamphlet protesting the exclusion, and the directors' Colored People's Day of 25 August 1893 drew protest (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Cooks argues that the Johnson Family cartoons in Harper's Weekly taught white readers how to recognise Black behaviour and reassured them that African American contributions were excluded (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 97).

Nations and empires had a place too. Spain's government building copied Valencia's silk exchange, and Boone argues that Spain was kept in the past by being treated as historic sites, while Cuban exhibitors claimed a nation apart from Spain (Boone 2019p. 90). Germany, which had stayed out of recent fairs, used Chicago to present itself as a world power through education and culture (Lawn 2009p. 55). Queensland's commissioner proposed a single Australian building as a sign of federation (Hoffenberg 2001p. 10).

Women, congresses and visitors

The Woman's Building, designed by Sophia Hayden, drew special attention (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 129; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 121). Congress created a Board of Lady Managers, which appointed 115 women with Bertha Palmer as chair (Ganz 2008). Weimann argues that the products shown, mostly by unmarried women, did not prove women could undertake work needing great physical or mental force (Weimann 1981p. 277). Markwyn calls it the most impressive women's building of its era, but says the Board excluded Black women (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 138).

The congresses were the fair's intellectual wing. The Congress Auxiliary, led by Charles C. Bonney, held 1,283 sessions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 121). The World's Congress of Representative Women drew more than 330 women speakers, and the Parliament of Religions met from 10 to 17 September (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 130). Burris calls the Parliament the fair's most powerful symbol of religion as an intellectual idea (Burris 2001p. 123).

Attendance figures differ. The reference works give over 21.5 million paid admissions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 130); Rydell (1984) gives 27,529,400 admissions in total (Rydell 1984); Weimann gives 27,539,000 (Weimann 1981p. 277). Chicago Day, 9 October, drew 716,881 (Badger 1979p. 109).

Aftermath and legacy

After the closing, the South Park Commission could neither dismantle nor maintain the buildings, and through the winter of 1893-94 the White City became a city of the poor, with vagrants and tramps (Badger 1979p. 130). Gilbert dates the fires that destroyed most of the White City to 8 January and 5 July 1894 (Gilbert 1991p. 209). Mayor Carter Harrison was shot on 28 October (Badger 1979p. 129; Allwood et al. 2001p. 68), though Gilbert dates the shooting to 9 October (Gilbert 1991p. 210). The Field Museum grew out of the fair's collections, which Putnam wanted kept rather than dispersed (Gosden & Knowles 2001). Weimann concludes that later expositions never matched the women's board of 1893: "That first splendid experience was never repeated" (Weimann 1981p. 597).

The Pledge of Allegiance was carried from the dedication ceremonies into the nation's schools (Rydell 1984; Samuel 2007). Turner's frontier paper, read to historians meeting with the fair, changed the course of American historiography (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 122). Chicago's 1921 Pageant of Progress was the seedbed for the 1933 fair, which Ganz reads as a break from 1893, financed privately rather than by government (Rydell 1993p. 36; Ganz 2008).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell (1984) reads the fair as an ideological project. The White City and the Midway together taught a racial hierarchy of progress, and anthropological science gave it legitimacy (Rydell 1984). For Rydell, "The White City was a utopian construct built upon racist assumptions" (Rydell 1984). Badger reads the fair as a cultural document whose symbols pulled in opposite directions, and whose vision of unity offered reassurance rather than a solution (Badger 1979p. 125). Gilbert sees two fairs in one, and presents their balance as an early form of eclectic, postmodern culture (Gilbert 1991p. 15). Gilbert also argues that the harmony the fair promised could not survive the city's class divisions (Gilbert 1991p. 210).

Hinsley reads the fair as a commercial event in which anthropology was pushed to the periphery, and argues that public curiosity produced "an early form of touristic consumption" (Karp & Lavine 1991). Burris treats the Parliament as an instance of exposition strategies applied to religion, and so less a landmark in its own right (Burris 2001p. 124). Greenhalgh reads the fair as commerce, entertainment and imperial racial display (Greenhalgh 1988p. 46). Whitfield reads the fair through six frontiers and treats the Midway as the start of mass culture (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 83). Crompton argues that the closing of the frontier turned the national gaze inward (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 113).

The texts agree on the paired White City and Midway, and on the exclusion of African Americans. They differ on whether the fair's symbols reassured or revealed. Badger and Gilbert stress the balance or the illusion, while Rydell and Hinsley place commercial and racial interests at its core. Geppert accepts Rydell's claim that Chicago became the standard for later fairs, but only within an American context (Geppert 2010p. 289).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan had the largest Asian presence. Its national building, the Hôôden (Phoenix Hall, also spelled Ho-o-den), stood on the Wooded Island in the lagoon, where Kuru Masamichi reshaped the Byodo-in temple at Uji into a Beaux-Arts building (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 92). The texts give four different costs: the Diet authorised 630,000 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212); Rydell (1984) gives more than $630,000 (Rydell 1984); Burris gives one million dollars (Burris 2001p. 105); and Hur gives over $100,000 (Hurp. 44). The Empress paid from her private purse for a women's committee and its exhibit, run by Princess Yasumori (Weimann 1981p. 138); Hur says the funding was alleged to be private (Hurp. 44). The Hôôden was given to the city and burned in 1943 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 105). The Midway section had a bazaar and a teahouse but no village (Burris 2001p. 114).

Ambassador Gozo Tateno described the Japanese section as Japan's formal introduction to the West (Kaiserp. 48). Kaiser reads this as acceptance of colonial divisions, and the exhibits as a cultural shield against imperial intrusion (Kaiserp. 48). Hotta-Lister says American respect for Japan grew, though with caution (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 211). Rydell argues that Japan was welcomed on subordinate terms (Rydell 1984).

China

China had no official pavilion. Burris links this to a boycott of the exclusion acts (Burris 2001p. 113); Rydell links it to the Geary Law (Rydell 1984). Hur places a Chinese exhibit in the General Building (Hurp. 44), while Rydell places a privately leased one on the Midway (Rydell 1984). Palmer reported that Lord Li Hongzhang proposed a semi-official committee of women, but none appears to have arrived (Weimann 1981p. 138).

Korea

Park calls the 1893 pavilion the second wave of Korean participation in world's fairs, and says it drew little attention and was hardly known to the Korean public (Parkp. 5). Hotta-Lister says Korea took part for the first time, among 72 other countries, with umbrellas and baskets (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212).

India, Ceylon and Siam

Weimann names only three official Asian exhibits: Japanese, Ceylonese and Siamese. The Indian, Persian, Turkish, Syrian and Chinese displays were privately organised (Weimann 1981p. 274). Indian merchants financed a pavilion modelled roughly on the Taj Mahal, whose attendants spoke impeccable English (Burris 2001p. 104). The Calcutta committee staffed the Indian tea house with servants in red and gold uniforms (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). Varma's ten paintings won two gold medals but were placed in the ethnographic section (Mathur 2007p. 105). The Siamese pavilion reproduced a Buddhist temple, with royal heirlooms valued at $58,000 (Weimann 1981p. 276). Japan and Siam were among the countries that originally withdrew from the awards (Giberti 2002). The texts do not say who paid for the Ceylonese or Siamese displays.

Southeast Asia

A Javanese village stood on the Midway among the villages linked to empires (Greenhalgh 1988p. 97); Benedict and Gilbert also list it (Benedict 1983p. 58; Gilbert 1991p. 115). The texts do not say who organised or paid for it.

Interpretation

Burris argues that India and Japan were the most conspicuous Asian cultures inside White City, while the Midway ranked Asian and Islamic peoples lower (Burris 2001p. 104). Hotta-Lister argues that Japan presented itself as an alternative culture rather than a supplement to the West (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 212).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commerce and entertainment — the Midway's concessions and villages earned money, and the texts treat that commercial logic as central to how the fair displayed other peoples (Badger 1979p. 120; Karp & Lavine 1991).
  • Race and hierarchy — the Midway ranked peoples from the White City outward, and the exclusion of African Americans is the charge most texts share (Rydell 1984; Roche 2000p. 85).
  • Nationalism and empire — Japan, Spain and Germany used the fair to claim standing among the powers, while colonial displays served imperial aims (Boone 2019p. 90; Lawn 2009p. 55; Hoffenberg 2001p. 10).
  • Gender and the Woman's Building — the women's exhibits are read as both progressive and limited by class and race (Weimann 1981p. 277; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 138).
  • Anthropology and museums — the fair's ethnological displays fed later museum collections and shaped their display methods (Gosden & Knowles 2001; Bennett 2004p. 133).
  • Architecture and planning — the White City set a model for public building and for the City Beautiful movement (Greenhalgh 1988p. 46; Maloney 2012p. 79).
  • Civic identity and aftermath — the fair gave Chicago a new image, and its end in ruin and unemployment marks its limits (Badger 1979p. 125; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 123).

Questions for study

  1. Was the Midway the fair's real message or a side show? Gilbert treats it as an equal centre, while Burris places it in a hierarchy of peoples.
  2. Did the White City reassure visitors, as Badger argues, or expose the tensions of American society, as Rydell argues?
  3. Why do the reference works, the monographs and the contemporary reports give such different figures for attendance, profit and the Ferris Wheel? What does this tell students about fair statistics?
  4. Was the Woman's Building a step forward for women, given that Weimann and Markwyn read it in different ways?
  5. Did Japan's display change American views of Asia, or did it confirm the hierarchy that the Midway set up?

Badger, a cultural historian, uses the 1893 Chicago fair as a test case for what world's fairs revealed about Victorian America. He argues that fairs wedded progress and nationalism, giving the nineteenth century a popular answer to rapid change, and that the Columbian Exposition was a self-conscious national event whose symbols pulled in opposite directions: the neoclassical White City and the Midway with its Ferris Wheel and ethnic villages. For Badger the resulting 'confusion of symbols' was answered by a 'vision of unity' that offered reassurance rather than a real solution, so that the White City was an illusion staged for an anxious nation. He reads the fair's end as well, from the assassination of Mayor Carter Harrison to the winter of unemployment that followed, and presents the whole event as a cultural document rather than only an architectural or commercial one.

Burris, a historian of religion, traces how international expositions from London's Great Exhibition of 1851 to Chicago's 1893 fair helped create a distinct academic field of religion. In his chapters on Chicago he argues that the White City, the Midway's ethnic villages and the Congress Auxiliary formed a hierarchy of cultures, and that the World's Parliament of Religions reflected rather than challenged colonial myths: the 'ten great world religions' excluded Native Americans by category and admitted African Americans only as converted Christians. He argues that social evolutionism let organisers rank peoples and religions together, so that religion became the principal tool for interpreting foreign cultures at the fair. For Burris the Parliament is best seen as an epiphenomenon of exposition strategies rather than a landmark in its own right, though it was the fair's most powerful symbol of the new idea of religion.

Wikipedia

The World's Columbian Exposition, also known as the Chicago World's Fair, was a world's fair held in Chicago from May 1 to October 30, 1893, to celebrate the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus's arrival in the New World in 1492. The centerpiece of the fair, held in Jackson Park, was a large water pool representing the voyage that Columbus took to the New World. Chicago won the right to host the fair over several competing cities, including New York City, Washington, D.C., and St. Louis. The exposition was an influential social and cultural event and had a profound effect on American architecture, the arts, American industrial optimism, and Chicago's image. A highlight was that Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse showcased their alternating current (AC) system, effectively winning the war of the currents against Thomas Edison's direct current (DC).
