Dallas · 1936–1937

Texas Centennial Exposition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Texas Centennial Exposition was a regional fair that opened at Dallas on 6 June 1936 to mark a hundred years of Texas independence from Mexico (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 171). Control passed to a triumvirate of bankers (Rydell 1993p. 121). Its federally funded Hall of Negro Life, which Rydell treats as an accommodationist compromise (Rydell 1993p. 176), was the only building torn down, and the fair reopened in 1937 as the Pan-American Exposition (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell uses the fair to show how Black leaders contested representation at Depression-era fairs (Rydell 1993p. 175).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Plans for a Texas centennial celebration began in the 1920s, and by the early 1930s a large fair for Dallas, the home of the annual state fair, was in planning (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell says that civic and business leaders in Dallas, having heard how Chicago's fair had employed 22,000 people, lifted Chicago retail sales by 19 percent and returned a $160,000 profit, resolved to match or surpass it (Rydell 1993p. 121). The texts treat the Dallas fair as regional, not international: neither it nor Cleveland's 1936-37 fair had enough foreign participation to qualify as a world's fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Organisers and finance

Control passed to a triumvirate of bankers: Nathan Adams, Fred F. Florence and Robert Lee Thornton, president of the Mercantile National Bank (Rydell 1993p. 121). Rydell dates this control to 1935, although the fair opened in 1936 (Rydell 1993p. 121; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Dallas officials took advice from Lenox Lohr and Louis Skidmore (Rydell 1993p. 127). Roosevelt visited Dallas to lend his personal endorsement to the fair (Rydell 1993p. 147). The federal budget was $3 million, and $100,000 of it was designated for the Hall of Negro Life (Rydell 1993p. 175; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Site and buildings

The fairgrounds combined modernism with Spanish mission, Aztec and Mayan motifs (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A tower topped by a star stood at one end of a long reflecting pool, the Esplanade of Texas, with the Hall of Communications and the Ford, General Motors and Chrysler pavilions nearby (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell says the design centred on the State of Texas Building, the Federal Building, the Esplanade of States and corporate exhibition halls (Rydell 1993p. 127). The Hall of Negro Life was a one-story, L-shaped building of nearly 14,000 square feet, and the first exhibition building completed at the fair (Rydell 1993p. 177). The fair's architect in chief, George Dahl, designed it after the exposition refused to let John Blount, who had first proposed the Hall, design it (Rydell 1993p. 176; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Hall was largely hidden behind shrubbery (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Empire and colony

The texts describe Texas as a state of the United States, not as a colony. Greenhalgh says that Texas presented itself at its centenary as a separate nation inside the United States with an imperial past, although it had no empire (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78). Governor James V. Allred's guidebook welcomed visitors to "Empire on Parade" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 79). Greenhalgh concludes that empire was largely illusory at Dallas, while the obsession with money was real (Greenhalgh 1988p. 79).

Rydell records that Dallas officials studied Daniel Burnham, Jr.'s report on the 1931 Paris colonial fair before designing their buildings in a "coloniale moderne" style (Rydell 1993p. 127). The passages read do not describe colonial exhibitors or colonised people on display. The African American exhibits in the Hall came from thirty-two states (Rydell 1993p. 178). In 1937 a Latin American building replaced the Hall (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 181). Greenhalgh gives the Pan-American fair type an imperial meaning, describing it as standing for empire (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76), but he does not discuss the Dallas reopening under that name.

The Hall of Negro Life

A. Maceo Smith, head of the Dallas Negro Chamber of Commerce, joined John L. Blount to seek state money for African American exhibits, in return for supporting the Dallas bid (Rydell 1993p. 171). Another text calls Smith's body the Dallas Black Chamber of Commerce (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The 1934 legislature deadlocked (Rydell 1993p. 171). Then a by-election for a Texas House seat split the coalition. The African American candidate, A. S. Wells, lost, and the promoters refused to raise the African American exhibit with the 1935 legislature (Rydell 1993p. 172). Smith offered to help sell fair bonds if some proceeds paid for African American exhibits, but Black buyers would not take the speculative risk (Rydell 1993p. 173).

Eugene Kinckle Jones, adviser to Secretary of Commerce Daniel Roper, proposed a Negro Building to the federal government (Rydell 1993p. 173p. 174). Rydell reads Roper's motive as securing African American loyalty to the government and encouraging consumer spending (Rydell 1993p. 174p. 175). Jesse O. Thomas managed the Hall (Rydell 1993p. 175); another text calls him Jesse D. Thomas (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell names the outcome the "Dallas Compromise", a separate hall offered in exchange for accommodation (Rydell 1993p. 176p. 182). Fair managers had the building walled off with shrubbery, and a white contractor chose its colours until committee members protested (Rydell 1993p. 176). Thomas had the segregation signs removed from the Hall's restrooms, which made it the only fair building with integrated restrooms (Rydell 1993p. 177; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and he secured seating for African American passengers on the sightseeing buses (Rydell 1993p. 177; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Greenhalgh writes that the Hall treated coloured people as a useful economic resource rather than as a culture with a part to play in American development (Greenhalgh 1988p. 100). He calls the racism in the pavilion vulgar, and says the Centennial was never likely to be an arena for debate about equal rights (Greenhalgh 1988p. 100).

Exhibits and attractions

All three major automakers had halls at Dallas. Chrysler and General Motors used earlier buildings, updated for the event, while Ford built a new pavilion designed by Albert Kahn (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188). Walter Dorwin Teague designed the exhibits and interiors for the Ford pavilions at San Diego and Dallas, and as in Chicago, the displays emphasised process rather than finished products (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188). Ford moved many of its exhibits from San Diego to Dallas (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 189) and presented a third version of its road attraction there (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190).

At the 1936 fair a Department of Labor exhibit used a talking robot that presented machines as benefactors of workers (Rydell 1993p. 151). Dallas promoters, alarmed that Fort Worth had signed Sally Rand, settled for a Streets of Paris concession of their own, featuring women performers (Rydell 1993p. 137). The Texas guide described an English Village followed by an Old Globe Theatre with Shakespearean players, close to the General Motors Auditorium (Greenhalgh 1988p. 125). A pageant, Cavalcade of Texas, played on an outdoor stage (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). An abstract nude statue of pioneer women had caused controversy before the opening, but it had blown over by the time the gates opened on June 6 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Hall's lobby held four murals by Aaron Douglas and a pamphlet by W. E. B. DuBois (Rydell 1993p. 178).

Visitors and reception

The Hall attracted about 400,000 visitors, about 60 per cent of them white (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 177). Its dedication drew a disappointing 7,000, but Negro Education Day drew over 60,000 fairgoers, half of whom visited the Hall (Rydell 1993p. 180). Many white visitors refused to believe that an African American artist had painted the murals (Rydell 1993p. 178). The Dallas Morning News ran ridiculing coverage, including of the Juneteenth celebration (Rydell 1993p. 180). Some African American newspapers urged a boycott (Rydell 1993p. 180).

Aftermath and legacy

The decision not to continue the Hall was announced in early January 1937 (Rydell 1993p. 181). Rydell says that the Hall was the only building demolished after the 1936 fair (Rydell 1993p. 181). Senator Morris Sheppard's inquiry found no fault with the white management and gave three reasons: little African American use, the need for space for Latin American exhibits, and insufficient federal funds (Rydell 1993p. 181). Thomas rejected the space argument and blamed the federal commission's executive secretary, W. B. Yeager, who had close ties to Dallas officials (Rydell 1993p. 181). Smith then built NAACP chapters across Texas (Rydell 1993p. 182). The reference work Findling and Pelle list the 1937 reopening as the Greater Texas and Pan-American Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427).

Its place in the series

Rydell says the Chicago model of modernistic architecture reappeared, in variants, at each later fair, including Dallas (Rydell 1993p. 127). Rydell, Findling and Pelle place the Dallas and Cleveland fairs of 1936-37 among the interwar fairs that showed science, corporate involvement and racial discrimination (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Samuel names Dallas (1936) among 1930s American fairs with similar consumerist themes, though he gives the 1939-40 New York fair the most enthusiastic celebration of that outlook (Samuel 2007). After Dallas, African American activists returned to the models of Philadelphia and Chicago for the New York fair of 1939-40 (Rydell 1993p. 182).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell, writing on African Americans and the fairs, reads the Dallas Hall as a compromise that later activists rejected (Rydell 1993p. 182). Rydell, Findling and Pelle treat the Texas Centennial as an example of the fairs' reliance on science, corporate involvement and racial discrimination (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh reads the fair as an imperial show and as a regressive spectacle, calling its racism vulgar (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78p. 100). Schrenk reads the Dallas pavilions as part of the automakers' rivalry at the fairs (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188). Rydell also notes that the 1936 Texas Centennial is the only depression-era fair to have been the subject of a historian's monograph, and cites Kenneth B. Ragsdale's study of the Dallas fair (Rydell 1993p. 8p. 225).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Accommodation and segregation — The Hall of Negro Life was a federal building walled off by shrubbery, and Rydell reads its terms as the Dallas Compromise (Rydell 1993p. 176; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Imperial display without an empire — Greenhalgh argues that Texas staged an imperial show as a state that had no empire (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78).
  • Corporate pavilions — Ford, General Motors and Chrysler all had halls, and Ford's new building by Albert Kahn used process displays (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188).
  • New Deal consumer politics — Roper and Jones saw African American spending and loyalty as gains for the government (Rydell 1993p. 174p. 175).
  • Entertainment and profit — The Streets of Paris concession and the Cavalcade of Texas pageant show the fair's appetite for paying crowds (Rydell 1993p. 137; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • National profile — The Texas guide's English Village and Old Globe Theatre presented a simplified version of British heritage (Greenhalgh 1988p. 125).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the Dallas organisers and federal officials accept a separate hall on terms that Rydell calls accommodation, and what did African American leaders gain by doing so? (Rydell 1993p. 176p. 182)
  2. Greenhalgh argues that an imperial theme could be staged without an empire. What does the Dallas evidence add to that argument, and what does it leave out? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78p. 79)
  3. Sheppard's inquiry and Thomas gave different reasons for the 1937 demolition. Which account do the texts support better, and why? (Rydell 1993p. 181)
  4. Ford, the Department of Labor and the Streets of Paris concession all used the fair to show modern machines or pleasure. What did each group want visitors to take away? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 188; Rydell 1993p. 151; Rydell 1993p. 137)

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
3.3Fordism, Corporate Display, and the American Expositions of the 1930sLisa D. Schrenkp. 181

Schrenk examines how American automotive companies changed their displays at world's fairs in the 1930s, when exhibits that taught visitors about production gave way to pavilions built to reinforce brand recognition. Ford and General Motors competed for visitors through increasingly theatrical buildings, often designed by architects such as Albert Kahn and by industrial designers such as Walter Dorwin Teague. The chapter follows the rivalry through the 1933-34 Century of Progress in Chicago, where GM's Chevrolet assembly line and Ford's 1934 rotunda, with its photomurals, dioramas and rotating globes, were the main attractions, and through Ford's pavilions at San Diego in 1935 and Dallas in 1936, which reused Chicago's displays. It then turns to the 1939 New York fair, where GM's Futurama, designed by Norman Bel Geddes, outdid Ford's Cyclorama of Production. The chapter concludes that the factory had become entertainment by the 1930s, and that the rivalry between the two companies carried on at later fairs.

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
SixAfrican Americans in the World of Tomorrowp. 157

Rydell examines how African Americans tried to turn the white supremacist world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s into arenas for social change. He argues that each struggle over representation reflected shifting class and political alignments within the African-American community, as older Republican elites gave way to labour organisers and radical intellectuals. The chapter moves through five fairs: Philadelphia's 1926 Sesquicentennial, where A. Philip Randolph gave an opening-day address amid protests over a planned Ku Klux Klan presence; Chicago's 1933-34 Century of Progress, where the NAACP and a state anti-discrimination law confronted concessionaires; Dallas's 1936 Texas Centennial, where a federally funded Hall of Negro Life embodied an accommodationist compromise and was later demolished; New York's 1939-40 World's Fair, where picket lines met official denials of discrimination; and the 1940 Chicago American Negro Exposition, which federal agencies helped stage. Evidence comes from newspapers, official correspondence and interviews. Rydell concludes that the fairs were battlegrounds rather than mere showcases, though sponsors repeatedly steered reform towards accommodation.

3Fairs between the World Warsp. 72

Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that fairs between the world wars turned to science, corporate pavilions and the Depression economy, and that they kept African Americans at the margins. The chapter opens with the 1920s boom and Philadelphia's Sesqui-Centennial of 1926, a historical fair that opened unfinished in the rain, and with Long Beach's 1928 fair, built around a North African theme. Its central case is Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, where modernist buildings, Sally Rand's fan dance and a Hall of Science drew crowds, while demeaning attractions and a poorly attended Negro Day marked its treatment of black visitors. The authors then cover San Diego in 1935-36, the regional fairs in Dallas and Cleveland in 1936, where the Dallas Hall of Negro Life was hidden behind shrubbery, and the New York and Golden Gate fairs of 1939-40, with General Motors' Futurama and the Trylon and Perisphere. The evidence comes from fair publicity, press reports and official histories. The chapter concludes that these fairs offered hope in hard times, and suggests that they may help explain why the United States avoided political revolution in the 1930s.

Rydell's chapter on African American challenges to the 1930s fairs gives the Dallas fair about twelve printed pages, the longest treatment in the set. It shows how A. Maceo Smith and John L. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Asian participation as exhibitors is marginal. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. The Indian Village at Chicago in 1933 falls outside the question.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. These were displays of Filipinos organised by American officials, promoters and anthropologists, not Filipino participation. China appears in a Chinese Village at Omaha in 1898, which the authors call little more than a souvenir shop, and in the Chinese village at San Francisco in 1915, which framed China as ripe for American investment. Japan appears through the Fair Japan concession at St. Louis, with its Ainu display, and through Japanese exhibits sent to Portland, which were hampered by reported discrimination against Asians at St. Louis. Elsewhere coverage is slight. The interwar chapter notes the Asian ornamental elements and Pacific Basin focus of San Francisco's 1939-40 fair, with elephant towers modelled on Cambodia's Angkor Wat. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction and conclusion name Osaka 1970 and Aichi 2005 as later Japanese fairs. No passage mentions Thailand, Indochina or the Dutch East Indies.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. Samuel's book is a history of the 1964–65 New York World's Fair, from Robert Kopple's 1958 proposal through the planning under Robert Moses, the two seasons of 1964 and 1965, and the sale of the pavilions afterwards. Its argument is that the Fair's theme of 'peace through understanding' was contradicted by its practice: commercial sponsors shaped nearly every pavilion, civil rights and Vietnam protests intruded on the grounds, and the corporation's finances collapsed. The book has two parts, 'Peace Through Understanding' (chapters 1 to 3, the planning and the two seasons) and 'Tomorrow Begins Today' (chapters 4 to 6, corporate exhibits, the International Area and science), then a conclusion on the Fair's popular memory.

Wikipedia

The Texas Centennial Exposition was a world's fair presented from June 6 to November 29, 1936, at Fair Park, Dallas, Texas. A celebration of the 100th anniversary of Texas's independence from Mexico in 1836, it also celebrated Texas and Western American culture. More than 50 buildings were constructed for the exposition, and many remain today as notable examples of Art Deco architecture. Attracting more than six million people including US President Franklin Roosevelt, the exposition was credited with buffering Dallas from the Great Depression.
