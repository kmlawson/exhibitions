In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The kyōshinkai form

Park explains that the Japanese term kyōshinkai (共進會, industrial exhibition) first meant a small competitive show for one kind of agricultural product. It later came to mean a large exposition that also staged spectacular and entertaining events in Japan (Parkp. 7). Park does not say which of these forms the Fukuoka event took, and the texts give no description of its scale, buildings or events (Parkp. 7).

Korean press coverage

Park's only reference to the Fukuoka event is in note 22, which is attached to a sentence on local exhibitions in Korea (Parkp. 7). The note refers to "industrial exhibitions, such as the Fukuoka Kyōshinkai of 1909" (Parkp. 7). It cites two Hwangsŏng shinmun reports, dated 23 November 1909 and 9 February 1910, and does not say which report covers which exhibition (Parkp. 7). The Fukuoka event is named with the Nagoya Kyōshinkai of 1910 in the same note (Parkp. 7).

Local exhibitions in Korea

Park states that after Japanese industrial exhibitions were introduced into Korea, local governments began to plan kongjinhoe there (Parkp. 7). Park describes these local shows as "important precursors of the 1915 exhibition" (Parkp. 6). The texts do not present the Fukuoka event as a model for any Korean show. Its only recorded link with Korea in these texts is the newspaper coverage (Parkp. 7).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Japanese kyōshinkai as a form — Park defines the term and says the form reached Korea, where local governments began to plan kongjinhoe (Parkp. 7).
  • Korean press coverage — the only evidence the texts give for this exhibition is Park's note on Korean newspaper reports of the Fukuoka and Nagoya kyōshinkai (Parkp. 7).
  • Precursors of the 1915 exhibition — Park calls the earlier local industrial exhibitions in Korea precursors of the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915 (Parkp. 6).

Questions for study

  1. Park cites two newspaper reports for the coverage of Japanese kyōshinkai. What did Korean readers learn from reports of Japanese provincial exhibitions, and how did the reports address Korean audiences?
  2. Park calls the local Korean exhibitions precursors of the 1915 exhibition. Which Japanese kyōshinkai, if any, shaped those Korean shows, and what evidence would show it?
  3. The texts give no organisers, exhibits or attendance for the Fukuoka Kyōshinkai. Where could a student find these details, and would they change the reading of its place in the press?

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
FourThe Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, the Last Show of the Qing Dynastyp. 124Asia

Hur turns to the Nanyang Industrial Exposition, held in Nanjing from June to November 1910, the first national exposition hosted by China and the last of the Qing dynasty. The chapter traces the project to Duanfang, the Manchu governor-general who proposed it in 1908 and modeled its regulations on Japan's domestic exhibitions, and to his successor Zhang Renjun after Duanfang's transfer in 1909. Hur describes the preparations, including a site of about 165 acres, pavilions for twenty-two provinces, foreign samples buildings, and the security measures taken amid fears of unrest in Nanjing. The main argument concerns the Chinese press, which, unlike its coverage of Osaka and St. Louis, was mostly descriptive or positive, a shift Hur ties to the constitutional movement and to newspapers and organizers who backed it. The chapter also covers the large Japanese visiting groups and the overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia. It concludes that the exposition was a momentary display of an integrated, constitutional China that faded with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
7The Expositions in Portland and Seattle: “To Celebrate the Past and to Exploit the Future”Asia

Rydell argues that the Lewis and Clark Centennial in Portland (1905) and the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition in Seattle (1909) were built to link the Pacific Northwest to Asian markets and an American commercial empire, and that organized labor contested both. The chapter traces the closed-shop dispute, the referendum drive and the open-shop hiring that led to the Labor Day protest at Woodland Park. Its evidence is the exhibits and villages: the Igorot village on Portland's Trail and Seattle's Pay Streak, the Smithsonian and War Department displays of the Pacific possessions, the Oriental Exhibits Building, and the Japanese delegation's visit to Seattle under the slogan hands across the sea. Rydell also follows the concessionaires Felder and Schneidewind and their Igorot troupe through amusement parks, where a complaint that the performers wanted to go home was answered with higher wages. The chapter ends with the fairs' legacies in Portland's Guild Lake industrial district and the University of Washington campus.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
