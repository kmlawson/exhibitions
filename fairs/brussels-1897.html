Brussels International Exhibition

Exposition Internationale de Bruxelles

The Exposition Internationale de Bruxelles, which the texts call Brussels 1897, was Brussels' first universal exhibition. It ran at the Parc du Cinquantenaire from 10 May to 8 November 1897 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143). About six million visitors came (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143). Its colonial section, the Exposition coloniale 1897 at Tervuren, opened on 24 April, and by Stanard's account held the fair's most striking exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Adrien d'Outremont was Commissioner-General (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419). The colonial section catalysed the later Musée du Congo Belge (Findling & Pelle 2008).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Depelchin lists seven international exhibitions in Belgium over 28 years, Brussels 1897 among them, and says they shared the aim of exceeding national interests while needing financiers (Filipová 2015p. 185; Filipová 2015p. 184; Filipová 2015p. 184). The economic case was Belgium's own: Belgium wanted to prove that it could compete with the great powers, and around 1900 it exported one-third of its industrial production (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142).

Organisers and finance

The texts name the Commissioner-General and several executive members: Emile Demot, Maurice Lemonnier, Nigor Thys and Senator Georges Dupret (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419). Mandell writes that the Comité français des expositions à l'étranger, a group of businessmen, organised almost all the French participants (Mandell 1967p. 38). The French section was the largest, with over 3,000 exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143). There was concern that the fair might not succeed, since it preceded Paris 1900, and torrential rain pushed the opening from 24 April to 10 May (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142). Stanard's entry gives a second reason for the delay: the opening was put back out of respect for the Duchess d'Alençon, who died in a fire in Paris on 4 May (Findling & Pelle 2008). The statistics table in Allwood gives 6,000,000 visitors, but its other cells are garbled in the extraction (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Site and buildings

Pavilions for 26 nations and buildings for 10,668 exhibitors covered 100,000 square metres around the lawn of the Parc du Cinquantenaire (Findling & Pelle 2008). Stanard calls the architecture unremarkable and says it did little to change Brussels' urban landscape (Findling & Pelle 2008). The best-known structure was the Bruxelles-Kermesse, a reconstructed old quarter inside a fourteenth-century wall, designed by Paul Saintenoy, with a belfry 37 metres high (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142). The organisers took the 'Old Antwerp' section of 1894 as their model (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Exhibits and crowds

Among the most popular exhibits was the Congo display: "One of the most popular exhibits was that representing the Independent State of the Congo, Belgium’s proud possession in Africa." (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142). A bicycle race at the Tervueren velodrome on 30 May drew 20,000 spectators (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143). The government offered 300,000 francs in prizes for answers to 408 questions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143).

Empire and colony

Roche says Belgium colonised the Congo from 1878 and staged four international expos with colonial themes before 1914, Brussels 1897 among them, as a claim to recognition as an imperial nation (Roche 2000p. 58; Roche 2000p. 58). Greenhalgh says the Brussels fair of 1897 had extensive imperial sections, and that Belgian imperialism belonged mainly to the late nineteenth century, when Leopold II took part in the scramble for Africa (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71; Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). The Congo was Leopold II's domain: the Tervuren exhibit was meant to be permanent because he was sovereign of the Independent State of the Congo (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143). At Tervuren the centrepiece was the Palais des Colonies, with rooms on ethnography, imports, transport and exports, and a hall showing coffee, cocoa and tobacco (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). The main draw was the village nègres, where one observer compared the Africans to animals in a competition (Findling & Pelle 2008). Over 260 Congolese were present, counting Force publique soldiers (Findling & Pelle 2008). The organisers put up a sign: Do not feed the blacks. They are already being fed. (Findling & Pelle 2008). The colonial section drew between 1.2 and 1.8 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008). Seven Africans housed above local stables died of poor housing and living conditions, and their tombs remain at Tervuren (Findling & Pelle 2008). Roche also reports that "pygmies from Belgian King Leopold’s recently acquired Congolese colony were displayed" at the 1897 fair (Roche 2000p. 84). One Belgian reported from the colony that a returning Congolese's account of the fair was nothing but positive (Findling & Pelle 2008). The Tervuren section was also the first Belgian attempt to use film for colonial propaganda (Findling & Pelle 2008). Its Palais des Colonies reopened in 1898 as the Musée du Congo, and a larger building, begun in 1904, became the Musée du Congo Belge (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Geppert's appendix table records the same Palais and a processional avenue between Brussels and Tervuren (Geppert 2010p. 257).

How historians have interpreted it

Stanard treats the Tervuren section, not the main fair, as the fair's most striking part and as the catalyst for the Musée du Congo Belge, and notes that little has been written on the main international section (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Roche reads the Belgian colonial expos as an imperial claim (Roche 2000p. 58). Greenhalgh places the 1897 fair among Belgian exhibitions with extensive imperial sections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Depelchin reads the Belgian series through national and financial motives and reconstructed historic scenery (Filipová 2015p. 184; Filipová 2015p. 190). Geppert calls Brussels an under-researched exhibition hub (Geppert 2010p. 13).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Colonial section at Tervuren — the texts place the fair's imperial content outside Brussels, at the Palais des Colonies (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Human display — Congolese people were shown in the villages and sign warned visitors against feeding them; the Congo exhibit in Brussels was a popular attraction (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142).
  • Imperial claim — Roche reads the four Belgian colonial expos as a bid for recognition as an imperial nation (Roche 2000p. 58).
  • Historical reconstruction — the Bruxelles-Kermesse was a model that Ghent 1913 later followed in its 'Old Flanders' (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Filipová 2015p. 190).
  • National economy — Belgium presented the fair as proof of economic strength among the great powers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142).
  • Museums and legacy — the colonial section led to the Musée du Congo Belge (Findling & Pelle 2008; Geppert 2010p. 257).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the colonial section at Tervuren draw more attention in the texts than the main fair in Brussels? (Findling & Pelle 2008)
  2. The texts give different reasons for the postponed opening. Which reason is better supported, and why? (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 142; Findling & Pelle 2008)
  3. Texts give 6 million visitors, and one footnote gives 6.2 million. How much does that difference matter for judging the fair? (Filipová 2015p. 197; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 143)
  4. How did the display of Congolese people fit Belgium's claim to be an imperial nation? (Roche 2000p. 58; Findling & Pelle 2008)
  5. What did the Musée du Congo Belge keep from the 1897 colonial section, and what did it leave out? (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008)

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Ch. 2, 'Two: Origins and Opposition', p. 38 (l. 1884-1904); ch. 3, 'Three: Preparations', p. 54 (l. 2553-2572)

Mandell's history of the Paris 1900 exposition mentions the 1897 Brussels fair twice. He notes that the Comite francais des expositions a l'etranger, a body of businessmen, organised almost all French participants at a large exhibition in Brussels in 1897. He also notes that the Bruxelles-Kermesse, a reconstruction of medieval Brussels, was a successful principal attraction there, and that Paris planned a similar 'Vieux Paris' for 1900. His argument concerns French politics and commerce around 1900, and Brussels appears as a comparison.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Mandell's book is a study of the Paris Exposition of 1900 set against the history of nineteenth-century world's fairs. Its argument is that the fair, planned as a display of French confidence, was shaped by politics: the state controlled it, opponents such as the Nancy editor Goulette campaigned against it, and the Dreyfus Affair was quietly suspended while it ran. The early chapters cover the French industrial exhibitions from 1798, the London exhibition of 1851 and the Paris expositions of 1855 to 1889. The rest follows the planning and opposition, a tour of the grounds, the political background, the verdict on success and the end of the era of great French expositions. The scope is Paris and France, with attention to national pavilions, decorative arts and finance.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention in this book, which mentions them in some 47 of its 7,866 lines, most of them in Chapter Four, 'At the Fair', where the coverage is substantial. Japan is named most often: its art made a sensation in Paris in 1867 and Vienna in 1873 (Chapter One), its request for more space was repeatedly refused and its delegation found the space insufficient (Chapter Three), and de Vogue judged that Japan surpassed France in taste (Chapter Six). China appears mainly in passing, such as a Chinese boy serving tea at the Asiatic Russia pavilion (Chapter Four). Chapter Four treats Asia as material on display, organised by others. The colonial section at the Trocadero held the pavilion of Indo-China, which Mandell says was grander than those of several sovereign states, and the Dutch East Indies; the Tour du Monde offered dioramas including Angkor Wat, and Javanese dancers and the Japanese performer Sadda Yakko drew crowds. Korean and Siamese pavilions appear only in plate captions. Siam is among the minor states that declined the invitation (Chapter Two). Southeast Asia beyond Indo-China, Java and Siam is not discussed, and no Asian visitors or observers are described.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Wikipedia

The Brussels International Exposition (French: Exposition internationale de Bruxelles; Dutch: Wereldtentoonstelling van Brussel) of 1897 was a world's fair held in Brussels, Belgium, from 10 May 1897 through 8 November 1897. There were 27 participating countries, and an estimated attendance of 7.8 million people. The main venues of the fair were the Parc du Cinquantenaire/Jubelpark, as well as a colonial section in the suburb of Tervuren, showcasing King Leopold II's personal property: the Congo Free State. The two exposition sites were linked by a purpose-built tramway.
