In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The one source

Henry's chapter on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds treats the expositions of 1907, 1915 and 1929 in detail (Henry 2014p. 93p. 97). The 1912 proposal does not appear in its main text. It is attached only to a sentence about finance in the early colonial state (Henry 2014p. 97p. 242).

Financial obstacles

Henry writes that "Although financial limitations continued to hamper the early colonial state" the annexation removed the impediment the protectorate government had faced in working with the Great Han Empire (Henry 2014p. 97). Endnote 27 cites the 1912 report on financial obstacles, and also a settler-led initiative reported on 5 June 1913 (Henry 2014p. 242). The text does not describe the 1913 initiative or say whether it concerned the same exhibition.

Earlier and later exhibitions

Henry calls the Keijo Exhibition of 1907, which opened on 1 September, a small-scale precursor to the expositions of 1915 and 1929 (Henry 2014p. 93). In the summer of 1908 its director met Korean and Japanese officials and business leaders to plan a Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion, with a budget of five hundred thousand yen (Henry 2014p. 96). The Korean government failed to muster the necessary funds, and the organisers postponed the event indefinitely (Henry 2014p. 97). This 1908 plan is a different event from the 1912 proposal.

Henry says that the colonial state "held its first exposition" at a juncture around 1915 (Henry 2014p. 15). He calls the Industrial Exhibition of 1915 "the first major spectacle of its kind in colonial Korea" (Henry 2014p. 34). The texts therefore place no colonial exhibition before 1915 in the sequence, though Henry does not state that the 1912 plan was never held.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Financial limitations — Henry ties the early colonial state's money problems to the 1912 proposal in one endnote and gives no further detail (Henry 2014p. 97p. 242).
  • Postponed exhibitions — the 1908 Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion is the earlier plan the text describes, and it failed for want of funds (Henry 2014p. 97).
  • Exhibition series — Henry presents 1907, 1915 and 1929 as one sequence, with 1915 as the state's first exposition (Henry 2014p. 93p. 15).
  • Settler initiatives — a settler-led initiative of 1913 appears only in the endnote, undescribed (Henry 2014p. 242).

Questions for study

  1. What did the 1912 proposal involve, and who promoted it? The texts give no answer, so the cited 1912 newspaper report would be the place to check.
  2. Does Henry mean the 1912 financial obstacles and the 1913 settler initiative to refer to one project, or to two separate efforts? The endnote does not say.
  3. How far did money shape which colonial exhibitions were held before 1915, given the 1908 plan failed for lack of funds (Henry 2014p. 97)?
  4. Why did the organisers of 1908 consider the palace grounds, and what did a palace site signal to Korean visitors (Henry 2014p. 96)?

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
