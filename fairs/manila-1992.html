Manila 1992 (recognised exposition)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

Manila 1992 was a planned recognized exposition for Manila in 1992. The 1988 BIE protocol allowed one short recognized event between registered expositions, and the Philippines applied for it (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The texts say the Philippines won the right to hold it, but a new government in Manila declined to fund it and the Philippines withdrew, so it was never held (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). Its significance lies in the hope that a developing country could host a world exposition, a hope the text says it thwarted (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). Only one text in the collection discusses it.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and the bid

The text describes the Philippines as a developing nation with a record as a participant at many world expositions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The 1988 protocol limited registered expositions to five-year intervals but left an opening for one recognized exposition between them. That event was to last no more than three months, occupy no more than 25 hectares, and suit a small host other than the traditional major countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The Philippines applied for such an event for Manila in 1992 and prepared creative, thematic, business and site plans of high professional quality (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).

Competition and cost

Australia then decided to challenge for 1992, and the Manila organisers found themselves in an expensive and at times intimidating contest (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The Philippine government put much effort and money into dealing with BIE delegations and into major presentations and hospitality in Paris. This drew media criticism and official alarm at home (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). A later passage describes the same kind of bidding as the force that undermined the Philippines' aspiration to be the first developing host, because the five-year rule encouraged rivalry in every fifth year (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). That passage does not name Manila or 1992, so the link is the essay's inference from the matching account of competition.

Withdrawal and its meaning

The Philippines won the right to hold a recognized exposition in 1992, yet a new government in Manila declined to continue the financial support needed to mount it, and the Philippines withdrew (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The text concludes that the hoped-for chance for a developing country to host a world exposition was thwarted. It also says the problem of helping less wealthy nations gain economic and social benefit from hosting a major exposition was left unresolved (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171). The text gives no theme, site, dates, budget or visitor figures for the 1992 plan (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171).

Its place in the series

The same text links the Manila bid to a later attempt. It says the first truly authentic registered exposition "would have been in Manila in 2002", and that losing that opportunity would have repercussions for the BIE and the medium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). The appendix of recognized expositions lists that 2002 Manila event as cancelled, and it lists no 1992 Manila entry (Allwood et al. 2001p. 211). The 2002 bid is a separate event and should not be confused with the 1992 one. The text also says the 1988 recognized category made no mention of developing countries, so any nation could use it. Lisbon in 1998 was the first European nation to do so (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). The text says that France's move to claim a recognized exposition in 2004 would occupy the period the Philippines had vacated (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197). The Chicago 1992 event listed in the same appendix is a different exposition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 210).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Developing countries as hosts — the bid is treated as a test of whether a developing nation could host a world exposition, and the text says that test was thwarted (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171p. 197)
  • Bidding and competition — the contest with Australia cost the Philippine government heavily and drew criticism at home (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171)
  • The BIE rules after 1988 — the recognized category was meant for small hosts, but the text says it did not mention developing countries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171p. 197)
  • Money and withdrawal — a new government declined to continue financial support, and the Philippines withdrew (Allwood et al. 2001p. 171)
  • Manila 2002 as a successor bid — the text sets the 1992 failure beside a later Manila bid that was not held (Allwood et al. 2001p. 197p. 211)

Questions for study

  1. The text presents a "recognized" event as suited to a small host, yet it also says the rules did not restrict the category to developing countries. What did the 1988 rules fail to protect?
  2. The text gives the change of government as the cause of withdrawal, and it describes the cost of the contest with Australia. How far were these two causes linked?
  3. What would the 1992 plans have looked like? Which sources, outside this text, would document its site, theme and finance?
  4. Does the claim that the 2002 Manila bid would have been the first truly authentic registered exposition change how the 1992 failure should be read?

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
14The Eastern Hemispherep. 161Asia

Allwood's chapter covers the exhibitions held in Japan, Australia and Korea between 1975 and 1999, and argues that Japan turned the medium into a state-led tool of regional and economic development, which its neighbours then copied. It opens with Okinawa 1975, where an ocean theme and the Aquapolis floating platform were set on a former battlefield, and moves through Tsukuba 1985, which used computers, robots and audio-visual display to promote a science city. Its main case is Osaka 1990, a garden exposition that the organisers turned into a hybrid expo within the BIE's rules, with 82 nations and a record attendance. The chapter then surveys Brisbane 1988, whose shaded riverside site and state pavilions are described, Taejon 1993, where Korean corporate pavilions overshadowed the international exhibitors, and the Kunming 1999 garden expo. It ends with the Philippines' withdrawal from a recognised exposition for Manila in 1992, and concludes that developing nations were still denied a realistic route to hosting.

In the contents of this work

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
EAppendix E: Fairs That Never Werep. 428

This appendix to the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions lists expositions that were planned but never held, from New York 1913 and Baltimore 1914 to Calgary 2005, each as a short history. It does not argue a thesis; it records how each proposal was made, which sites and themes were chosen, and where it stopped. The causes recur: world wars and the Great Depression, economic slumps, doubts that a city could afford the cost, governments and mayors withdrawing support, and rival bids that won the BIE vote. Several entries are detailed, notably Philadelphia 1976, Los Angeles 1981, Paris 1989, Chicago 1992, Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, which trace the bidding, financing and political fights. Some entries show a fair's afterlife, such as Tokyo 1940 tickets honored at Osaka 1970. The appendix ends with Calgary's defeat by Aichi for 2005.

Allwood, Allan and Reid give Manila 1992 a few paragraphs in the Eastern Hemisphere chapter, and return to it briefly in the post-2000 discussion and in the appendix of recognized expositions. The authors say the Philippines won the right to hold a recognized exposition in 1992 after an expensive contest with Australia, but a new government in Manila withdrew financial support and the Philippines withdrew, so it was never held. Their argument is that the 1988 rules, which allowed one short recognized event between registered ones, were meant to let a developing country host a world exposition, and that the Manila failure thwarted that hope. They also show that the rules did not restrict the category to developing hosts, so that a European city, Lisbon in 1998, could use it. The text gives no theme, site, dates, budget or visitor figures for the 1992 plan.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.
