Calcutta · 1883–1884

Calcutta International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Calcutta International Exhibition ran from December 1883 to March 1884 in Calcutta, capital of British India, and was India's first international exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75). Its promoter, Jules Joubert, was a French-born Australian entrepreneur who had run exhibitions in Australia and New Zealand before it (Filipová 2015p. 253; Allwood et al. 2001p. 54). The texts put attendance at about a million or more (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). Historians use the fair to show how exhibitions produced imperial knowledge, shaped social relations, and left collections in Indian museums (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12; Hoffenberg 2001p. 13).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Joubert, a French-born naturalised citizen of New South Wales, initiated the fair and managed it as general manager (Filipová 2015p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). In December 1882 he proposed the plan to Augustus Rivers Thompson, lieutenant-governor of Bengal (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). Thompson's main hope was to encourage trade by advertising Indian products, particularly in the Australian colonies (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). Prasch adds that Joubert's central aims were trade between Australia and India, along with personal profit, and that these aims suited the British governors of India, who wanted to boost Indian commerce and revive Indian craftsmanship (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76).

Organisation, finance and Indian experts

The Bengal government agreed to provide Rs 50,000 for buying and displaying provincial products, and the Public Works Department built extra buildings at Joubert's expense (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82; Hoffenberg 2001p. 88). Stall holders paid a weekly fee and could sell only duplicates of exhibits or goods made in the exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 81). Hoffenberg reports that Joubert was accused of organising the fair for his own gain and of ill-treating South Asian visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 91). The officers were Thompson as president of the executive committee, S. T. Trevor as vice-president and Joubert as general manager (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 384).

Indian experts did much of the practical work. Trilokya Nath Mukharji, from Calcutta, was the most influential South Asian official at the shows, and an assistant curator in the Indian Museum (Hoffenberg 2001p. 50; Hoffenberg 2001p. 53). He prepared an Indian raw produce collection for Calcutta and wrote official guidebooks for Calcutta and Amsterdam (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54; Hoffenberg 2001p. 111).

Site, buildings and exhibits

The fair covered almost 22 acres, about 7 of them enclosed, and drew 2,500 exhibitors who showed more than 100,000 articles (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 10 acres, a figure the other texts do not share (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). The India courts were the centre of the fair, with human displays of India's races and craftsmen at work (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). Their centrepiece, the Gwalior Gateway, was carved by about a thousand stone carvers in four months under Major J. B. Keith (Hoffenberg 2001p. 154; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77). Prasch argues that it was "assembled to say “India” to the world" rather than a copy of any Indian tradition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77). Machines were demonstrated in motion, including steam ploughs, but a Times correspondent doubted they suited Indian needs (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189; Hoffenberg 2001p. 190).

Peoples on display and participating nations

The Royal Asiatic Society in Calcutta had proposed ethnographic displays of wild subjects as early as the 1860s, and in 1883–84 fair officials invited governments to send living specimens of the aboriginal tribes of India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 220; Hoffenberg 2001p. 221). George Watt oversaw lifelike models, which were either kept by the city museum or exchanged (Hoffenberg 2001p. 222). Hoffenberg argues that such displays separated wild from civilised subjects within South Asian society (Hoffenberg 2001p. 221).

Participants included European and North American countries, Japan and the Australian colonies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). Powell lists Turkey, Japan and the Indian provinces among the exhibitors, and says only Austria-Hungary sent an official delegation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). The Japanese court drew attention to the artistic skill of "that remarkable people" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 83). The texts say nothing about Ceylon, Burma or China as exhibitors; Chinese workers appear only as carpenters in Kipling's packing rooms (Hoffenberg 2001p. 35).

Dr Fayrer proposed that living people be seated in booths like those of theatres or Indian bazaars, classified by race and tribe, where they could be watched, photographed and cast, with interpreters to explain them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 221). The organisers also suggested that the subjects, when not on show, could be set to work tidying the exhibition grounds (Hoffenberg 2001p. 221).

Visitors, reception and controversy

Hoffenberg says the fair was open over four months, while Powell and Allwood give three (Hoffenberg 2001p. 2; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82; Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). Joubert set aside Sunday afternoons for women in purdah, the seclusion of women from public view, in an attempt to break "the impenetrable barrier of the zenana", the women's quarters of a household (Hoffenberg 2001p. 16). Several thousand Indian women attended, and some princes bought exclusive hours for their wives and mothers (Hoffenberg 2001p. 216). The official report noted "the large number of native females of all ranks" among the visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 216). Hoffenberg argues that the women became exhibitors as well as exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 218).

The Ilbert Bill, which Prasch describes as giving Indian courts and juries jurisdiction over white settlers, provoked a boycott by much of the Anglo-Indian community (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75). The texts disagree on its status: Prasch calls it recently passed, while Hoffenberg calls it proposed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Hoffenberg 2001p. 265). Hoffenberg says European guests refused to sit on the podium with the viceroy and visiting princes (Hoffenberg 2001p. 265). The Nizam of Hyderabad's visit caused anxiety, since he seemed to claim greater authority (Hoffenberg 2001p. 239). Electric lights failed at the opening: Prasch calls it a technical failure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75), while Hoffenberg says the wires were too small for the current, not sabotaged (Hoffenberg 2001p. 265). Powell reports that the Indian and Anglo-Indian press took little interest, seeing the fair as entertainment (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 83).

Joubert's visits were set apart on Sunday afternoons behind screens at the entrance gates, and all male attendants were removed (Hoffenberg 2001p. 216). He told the local press that the visits were run by an Indian, not a European (Hoffenberg 2001p. 216). The proposal was argued out in Calcutta's streets and press. Modernisers, including many South Asians, saw it as a step towards national progress, and one Indian visitor wrote that Joubert had done more in three months to break down conservatism among women than many philanthropists had done in fifty years (Hoffenberg 2001p. 218). Many officers of the Raj feared that admitting women revived the Liberal reforms they blamed for the Mutiny, and Anglo-Indian and South Asian traditionalists both wanted women kept in the zenana (Hoffenberg 2001p. 218).

Aftermath and legacy

Local officials bought exhibits after the fair with public funds for the city museum, and the show had provided the core collection of a revived public museum (Hoffenberg 2001p. 41; Hoffenberg 2001p. 13). The Gwalior Gateway travelled to London, where it again served as the gateway to Indian courts at the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77). The Kew indigo factory model was a replica of one made for the Calcutta fair and was lent to a London store in 1910 (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 140). In the Dutch East Indies, the Semarang exhibition of 1914 was modelled more directly on colonial exhibitions in British India and Australia than on European fairs (Cotép. 3).

Money, sales and losses

Hoffenberg says the invitation to women in purdah was partly a scheme to draw paying visitors from Calcutta's South Asian population (Hoffenberg 2001p. 216). Attendance fell well below expectations despite extensive advertising, and the shortfall left Joubert and his company in serious financial difficulty (Hoffenberg 2001p. 217). The newspaper debate over the zenana days in 1884 raised a number of admissions that had been disappointing until then (Hoffenberg 2001p. 16).

The accounts disagree about who gained. Allwood, Allan and Reid's table records a loss of Rs 1,390 (£96) (Allwood et al. 2001p. 206). Powell names Joubert as one of the main beneficiaries, along with a few tradesmen and the local hotel and boarding-house trade (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 83). Hoffenberg quotes Lockwood Kipling, who wrote to his wife asking why Joubert should be in the exhibition "to take the money only" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 91). Local newspapers argued that the state should run the next show, since its accounts would at least be public (Hoffenberg 2001p. 91). Prasch records that exhibitors paid two shillings a foot for display space, with a five-shilling surcharge for frontage on the main avenues (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76).

The grounds and the layout

Prasch places the displays of foreign nations and colonies in a permanent brick building joined to the India Museum, on one side of Chowringhee Road (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). Powell gives a different occupant for the permanent building beside the museum, which he says housed an art gallery and jewellery court on the east side of the road (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). The two texts therefore disagree about what stood beside the museum. Powell puts the Indian Court (488 by 120 feet) and the Machinery Court (410 by 144 feet) on the west side (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82), with smaller buildings reusing designs Joubert had used in Christchurch (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82). Constance Fletcher, writing for the Art-Journal, described a bridge over the road that carried visitors from the consumer goods displays "to an enchanted land", the India courts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76).

The Gwalior Gateway and the India courts

Hoffenberg says the gateway was not a copy of a conventional entrance, as some of the written references suggested, but an attempt to show carving from many periods at once, which collapsed time (Hoffenberg 2001p. 154). One of the organisers claimed that the Gwalior forms were "not dead but merely in abeyance" and would revive under patronage (Hoffenberg 2001p. 154). Major J. B. Keith, who superintended the construction of the gateway, wanted a museum for archaeological fragments and a register of decayed craftsmen, and forwarded a carved stone to Calcutta to win public support for that work (Hoffenberg 2001p. 156).

Tea and trade with Australia

Thomas Carritt, agent of the Indian Tea Districts Association, organised tea displays at the Amsterdam and Calcutta exhibitions in the early 1880s, with samples, working models and gardens where visitors could sit and drink tea (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114). He contacted the Australian commissioners at Calcutta and lobbied for trade with their colonies (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114). Exports to Australasia rose sharply in the mid-1880s (Hoffenberg 2001p. 115). Joubert returned to South Australia from his first official visit to Calcutta with seventy-seven large packages of purchases (Hoffenberg 2001p. 119). The Anglo-Indian community called the Australian exhibits the basis of a future trade between the two countries, and one local reviewer judged wines and preserved meats the most promising colonial exports, since refrigeration cut costs (Hoffenberg 2001p. 119). H. J. Scott, South Australia's agent at Calcutta, visited the colony's works likely to trade with India before the show opened (Hoffenberg 2001p. 120). Victoria alone sent 2,300 items (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). An 1884 dinner in Calcutta for the Australian commissioners was praised in the Anglo-Indian press (Hoffenberg 2001p. 255).

Police and security

Police precautions at Calcutta included asking for detectives from Scotland Yard, but routine guard duties fell to the local Calcutta Police (Hoffenberg 2001p. 235). Thefts and vandalism still occurred, the worst being the breaking off of part of a gold throne exhibited by one of the princes (Hoffenberg 2001p. 235).

How historians have interpreted it

Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions were sites where commissioners produced imperial knowledge and social order, and that their effects outlasted the shows in Indian museums and public culture (Hoffenberg 2001p. 31; Hoffenberg 2001p. 12). Hoffenberg reads the zenana episode as turning women from passive objects into observers and sometimes exhibitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 218). Hoffenberg also sees the machinery displays as showing the limits of imperial spectacle, since suspicion of the technology and differing soils and climates slowed its spread (Hoffenberg 2001p. 190; Hoffenberg 2001p. 190).

Prasch argues that the fair belonged to a wider exhibition tradition in which imperial themes and displays of imperial subjects were becoming central (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76). Powell argues that the fair mattered more for introducing Indians to non-Indian and non-British influences than for trade, and that Joubert and a few traders profited (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 83; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 83). The two differ on success. Prasch holds that the disputes did not undermine the overall success (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76), while Powell records Thompson's proclamation of an almost unqualified success (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82).

Coté treats Calcutta as the scale that Dutch colonial exhibitions had to match (Cotép. 3). The volume edited by Finnegan and Wright shows a Calcutta model travelling beyond the exhibition hall (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 140). The volume edited by Filipová places the fair among colonial fairs that displayed British dominance (Filipová 2015p. 14). Roche quotes MacKenzie's view that such shows celebrated the transplantation of European-style societies overseas (Roche 2000p. 58). Geppert and Lawn mention the fair only in chronologies (Geppert 2010p. 251; Lawn 2009p. 13), and Mandell calls it a small, so-called international exhibition (Mandell 1967p. 16).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial spectacle and its limits — The texts treat the fair as an imperial showcase, but Hoffenberg shows that machinery displays did not easily spread into practice in India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 190; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76).
  • Women and purdah — The zenana days were the most disputed episode, read as a challenge to seclusion and as a political flashpoint (Hoffenberg 2001p. 16; Hoffenberg 2001p. 216; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 76).
  • Ethnological displays — Calcutta officials proposed living displays of aboriginal and tribal people, and their models shaped museum collections (Hoffenberg 2001p. 221; Hoffenberg 2001p. 222).
  • Indian experts and princes — Mukharji and other South Asian officials ran exhibits, princes attended the opening, and the Nizam's visit caused anxiety (Hoffenberg 2001p. 50; Hoffenberg 2001p. 206; Hoffenberg 2001p. 239).
  • Finance and private promotion — Bengal's Rs 50,000 and Joubert's private role framed the fair's economics (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 82; Hoffenberg 2001p. 81).
  • Ilbert Bill politics — The Ilbert Bill dispute shaped the boycott and press coverage, and the texts disagree on its status (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 75; Hoffenberg 2001p. 265).
  • Museums and collections — Fair purchases and models moved into Calcutta's museum and beyond (Hoffenberg 2001p. 41; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 77).

Questions for study

  1. Did the zenana days open a public role for Indian women, or did they mainly serve Joubert's promotion of the fair? What evidence in the texts would decide it?
  2. Why did machinery demonstrations work as spectacle in Australia yet fail to change practice in India? Which explanation do Hoffenberg's examples support best?
  3. Whose fair was Calcutta: Joubert's private venture, the Bengal government's exhibition, or the work of Indian experts such as Mukharji?
  4. How should students weigh the conflicting figures for the site (10 or 22 acres), the length of the fair (three or four months) and the status of the Ilbert Bill?
  5. What does the Gwalior Gateway, built to say India rather than to copy a tradition, reveal about how colonial exhibitions represented India?
  6. Did the women's days chiefly bring in paying admissions, as the shortfall suggests, or did they serve the reform aims Joubert claimed? Which figures would settle it?
  7. Powell counts Joubert among the main beneficiaries, while Allwood records a loss. What would a student need to know about how each source counted costs and receipts?
  8. Prasch and Powell give different occupants for the building beside the India Museum. Which description fits the layout better, and which plan or map would decide it?

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
1Exhibitions and the New Imperialismp. 1Asia

Hoffenberg argues that the popular exhibitions held in London and in colonial cities such as Sydney, Melbourne and Calcutta between 1851 and 1914 deserve as much study as the Great Exhibition that overshadows them. The chapter shows how colonial commissioners used these shows to present Australia as a progressive settler society and India as a restored tradition. Its evidence includes the Doulton 'Impasto' panels at the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition, the Zenana Days in Calcutta, and Aboriginal Australians displayed at Sydney and Adelaide, cases in which visitors and exhibits sometimes returned the colonial gaze. The chapter also traces the afterlife of the shows in museum collections, catalogues and the classifications used to organise them. It argues that the exhibitions promoted an imperial market and a federated commonwealth, while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. It closes by claiming that exhibitions shaped social order through participation rather than coercion, and that their ideas about imperial federation carried into the Commonwealth.

2The Exhibition Wallahs: Commissioners and the Production of Imperial Knowledgep. 31Asia

Hoffenberg's chapter examines the commissioners who organized Britain's and its colonies' exhibitions in the Victorian and Edwardian eras, and how their work turned exhibitions into networks of imperial and colonial knowledge. The argument is that these cultural bureaucrats, who were often scientists, art experts, botanists and museum officials, created and circulated the categories by which the empire and its colonies were known, and that their exchanges of objects and information strengthened ties between metropole and periphery. Hoffenberg works through South Kensington first: Henry Cole, Cunliffe-Owen and Purdon Clarke, whose handbooks, purchases and loans linked London to Australian and Indian museums. The bulk of the chapter then compares Australian commissioners such as Redmond Barry, William Macarthur, Edward Combes and Ferdinand von Mueller with Anglo-Indian and South Asian ones, above all Buck, Watt, Lockwood Kipling and Trilokya Nath Mukharji, whose catalogues and overseas travels form the chapter's fullest case study. The conclusion argues that this participation was not simply hegemonic: colonial experts became nationalist intellectuals, so that imperial knowledge could also undermine imperial rule.

In the contents of this work

3Commissioners and the State: Power and Controversy at the Exhibitionsp. 63Asia

This chapter asks how exhibition commissioners justified their authority as a new cultural elite, and why that authority drew criticism. Hoffenberg argues that commissioners presented themselves as disinterested experts serving the state, yet their work of selecting, pricing and displaying objects exposed them to charges of self-interest and commerce. The main evidence is the Crystal Palace of 1851, where the executive commissioners forbade price tags, and the London International of 1862, which radical papers denounced as a jobbery run by a 'Science and Art clique'. Colonial cases, including the Melbourne Centennial Exhibition, the Adelaide Jubilee show of 1887 and the sales rules at Calcutta in 1883-84, show how on-site selling set commissioners against local merchants. Hoffenberg then compares state funding in Australia and India, which was direct and generous, with England's ad hoc royal commissions, and traces critics from Babbage to the radical press who attacked the commissioners from opposite sides. The chapter ends by treating exhibitions as contests for public opinion, in which the shows revealed the tensions they were meant to conceal.

In the contents of this work

4Consumers, Producers, and Markets: The Political Economy of Imperial Federationp. 99Asia

Hoffenberg examines how Australian and Indian commissioners, merchants and critics used international exhibitions to argue about the economics of empire. The displays of wool, coal, wine, cotton, silk and tea are read as political economies in their own right rather than neutral reflections of trade. Hoffenberg follows the Australian and Indian commodity courts from the Great Exhibition of 1851 through the London International Exhibition of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, with further material from Sydney in 1879 and Calcutta in 1883. Tea provides the chapter's clearest case: the Indian Tea Districts Association sold samples in London and then in Australia and North America, and the chapter traces how James Inglis and Jules Joubert encouraged direct trade between Australia and India without English middlemen. The argument is that colonial producers pursued independent commerce within an imperial framework, and that critics such as Carlyle and the radical press rejected the market ideals the shows celebrated. The chapter ends by noting that the displays both reinforced imperial economic unity and exposed its strains, which opens onto the question of colonial trade policy.

In the contents of this work

5Terrae Nullius?: Australia and India at Overseas Exhibitionsp. 129Asia

Hoffenberg asks how Australian and Indian commissioners represented their colonies at overseas exhibitions, from the Paris Universal Exposition of 1855 to the London shows of 1862 and 1886. The chapter argues that the displays drew on a contrast between settler Australia, shown through mining machinery, wool, photographs, maps and emigrant literature, and an Oriental India, shown through artware, village bazaars, ethnographic models and casts of ancient monuments. Its evidence comes mainly from commissioners' letters, official catalogues and guidebooks, and from objects such as the working quartz-crushing battery of the Queensland court at South Kensington in 1886. Hoffenberg shows how Australian commissioners tried to correct an image of frontier violence and rough labour by exhibiting paintings, books and pictures of towns, and how both colonies courted emigrants, with Canada as the main rival. In India, commissioners built a traditionalist past through casts of the Sanchi Tope, the Gwalior Gateway and reconstructed bazaars where artisans worked and sold goods to visitors. The chapter concludes that these exhibits reinforced imperial authority over the past, yet also supplied colonial nationalists with the materials they later used to challenge it.

In the contents of this work

7Imperial and National Taxonomies: Entertaining and Policing Exhibition Visitorsp. 203Asia

Hoffenberg turns from exhibits to the people who visited exhibitions and to the ways commissioners classified, entertained and policed them. Visitors to the Great Exhibition of 1851, the London International of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 are treated as a visible social mass that organisers sorted by nation, race, class and gender. The evidence includes reduced fares for London workers, the appeal to guilds from neighbouring colonies for the Melbourne Intercolonial of 1866 to 1867, and the controversial zenana day at the Calcutta International of 1883 to 1884, when women in purdah were admitted to the grounds. Maori and Fijian performers at Sydney in 1879, and Aboriginal visitors from mission stations at Adelaide in 1887, are set against the static ethnographic models shown elsewhere, and the separate entrances at Nagpore in 1865 show how race and rank were policed at the door. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions offered social integration and imperial federation in economic and cultural terms, a citizenship that was real but not political. The chapter closes by linking these practices of ordering visitors to later management of migration in the twentieth century.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
Calcutta entry by Thomas Prasch, pp. 75–77; fair officials in Appendix C, p. 418

Prasch's encyclopedia entry presents the Calcutta International Exhibition of 1883–84 as India's first international exhibition and the first held in any non-settler colony. It covers the opening, the boycott by much of the Anglo-Indian community over the Ilbert Bill, the million-plus visitors and the countries that exhibited, including Japan and the Australian colonies. Prasch places the fair in a wider exhibition tradition in which imperial themes and displays of imperial subjects were becoming central. In Prasch's account the India courts, with their human displays and the Gwalior Gateway, were assembled to say “India” to the world rather than to reproduce any one Indian tradition, and the revival of Indian crafts there was a hybrid of preservation and commerce.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
introduction, p. 3; notes 7–8, pp. 36–37

Coté uses the Calcutta International Exhibition of 1883 as the scale against which Dutch colonial exhibitions were measured. Coté argues that the Semarang International Colonial Exhibition of 1914 was the first Dutch East Indies show with pretensions to be international, and that it was modelled on colonial exhibitions in British India and Australia rather than on European fairs. Coté notes that its organisers sought information from British Indian exhibition organisers. The article's treatment of Calcutta is brief and confined to the text and notes.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.

This volume's discussion of colonial fairs (p. 14) lists the Calcutta International Exhibition of 1883–84 among fairs organised in British India to promote settlement, investment and development. Anne Neale's chapter on Tasmania identifies Jules Joubert as the overall manager of the Calcutta show, placing it within the career of an exhibition impresario who went on to run later colonial fairs.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. The text uses the Calcutta exhibition to trace the circulation of a display object. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume brings together eleven case studies, grouped as encounters and identity, collection and display, and circulation and translation, on how knowledge was made and moved across empires in the long nineteenth century. Its introduction questions what 'global knowledge' meant and warns against easy claims of globality, while the afterword by Withers proposes connections, institutions and languages as ways of testing those claims. The sites are varied: fur-trade posts in Rupert's Land, ships at sea, a Cyprus excavation, a Belfast museum, Kew's Museum of Economic Botany, Malthus's Essay on Population, a Haitian imperial court, a Bihar mission and Livingstone's French and German editions. Exhibitions recur in only a few chapters, mainly the Kew and Haitian ones.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial but concentrated presence, carried by two chapters. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Mandell's book is a study of the Paris Exposition of 1900 set against the history of nineteenth-century world's fairs. Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government.
