New Zealand Centennial Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The New Zealand Centennial Exhibition was held at Wellington and opened on 8 November 1939 by the governor-general, Lord Galway (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). It marked the centenary of New Zealand's foundation as a British colony and the Treaty of Waitangi of 1840 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). A private company organised it, with considerable government support (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). The texts present it as a display of national progress. McCarthy also suggests that its Maori Court was an Indigenous attempt to find a place in modern New Zealand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Caird says the idea of an international exhibition at Wellington was first proposed in 1930 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). By 1938 its scope had been determined as imperial, as opposed to international, because an international exhibition needed official recognition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). McCarthy says the exhibition marked the hundred years since the Treaty of Waitangi was signed in 1840 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). J.W. Heenan, of the Department of Internal Affairs, wanted the exhibition to "create a national spirit" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). It was the centrepiece of events run by the National Centennial Committee, which included books, monuments, reenactments, festivals, art exhibitions and a film (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310).

Organisers and finance

Caird says the money came from selling shares in the exhibition company, with government contributions and subsidies (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). McCarthy describes a private company, led by an experienced manager, the Scotsman Charles Hainsworth, which had considerable government financial support (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). Appendix C names the Hon. D. G. Sullivan as president and T.C.A. Hislop as chairman of directors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 421; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 392). The texts do not say whether T.C.A. Hislop is the Thomas Hislop whom McCarthy names as mayor of Wellington (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). McCarthy says the company eventually lost money (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310), and Caird says the exhibition did not make a financial profit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305). Neither text gives fair statistics for it that can be read.

Site and buildings

Caird places the Rongotai site in central Wellington, chosen for its transport links and for minimal engineering difficulties (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). McCarthy puts it on the Miramar peninsula, in the Rongotai suburb, where it was accessible to the city but exposed and windswept (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). Caird gives the site as 55 acres (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). The buildings covered 14 acres, cost £50,000 and were temporary structures (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). McCarthy names the architect as Edmund Anscombe, who had worked on the Dunedin exhibition fourteen years earlier, and says the buildings were laid out on a similar axial plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). He calls the Art Deco architecture one of the most memorable features (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). The 155-foot tower (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304) became the main symbol and was reproduced on souvenirs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311). Its base carried a frieze by Alison Duff, and Maori motifs added a local flavour to the decoration (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311).

Exhibits and attractions

Caird says twenty-six departments of state contributed to the Government Court (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). The Dominion Court, representing all the provinces, replaced separate provincial exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). The Women's Section had the most striking historical display, with a North Island room of pioneering conditions and a South Island room of a later, more prosperous settler's home (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). Playland had a roller coaster, funhouse, miniature race track, magicians and Chinese acrobats (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305). McCarthy says the Cyclone roller coaster was the big hit and that 150,000 people went to the cinema (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312). He says the exhibition included no fine art apart from historical murals in the government court (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312).

Visitors and reception

A crowd of 40,000 attended the opening (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). Caird says an attendance of 4 million had been hoped for, and the total of 2,641,043 was a success given wartime conditions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305). McCarthy says the war limited participants and attractions, few overseas visitors came, and some events were cancelled (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). Caird calls the exhibition overall a great success (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305). She notes that newspaper coverage in New York and London was practically non-existent, so the Wellington dailies hold more (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305).

Empire and colony

Caird describes the centennial as marking New Zealand's foundation as a British colony (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). Its scope was set as imperial, "determined as imperial, as opposed to international" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). The texts do not say what the imperial scope changed beyond the need for official recognition. The centennial celebrated New Zealand as a British colony (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304), and the governor-general, Lord Galway, opened the exhibition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304).

Colonies and dominions took part in the displays. Caird names the Australian and Fijian pavilions, which concentrated on resources and travel attractions, and a Canadian court on industry and agriculture (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). McCarthy names an Australian pavilion in a modernist style (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311). The government court's external affairs section included Pacific Island dependencies: Samoa, Niue and the Cook Islands (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311). The texts describe no colonial products shown as such, and no "native village" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311).

The Maori Court was a Native Department display. McCarthy says official imagery could be read as celebrating progress by contrasting a Maori past with a Pakeha present, and that Apirana Ngata set out to challenge this view through the display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312). The court featured an unnamed whare runanga (council house), built in some haste, whose mix of modern and traditional elements expressed the dynamism of Maori culture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312). In the guide to the court, Ngata wrote of a display with its blend of a beautiful ancient form with modern adaptations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312). Caird's entry does not mention the Maori Court (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304).

For the organisers' aims, the texts give the national spirit that Heenan wanted (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310) and McCarthy's view that the nationalism was modest and did not shake New Zealand's unquestioning loyalty to Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). Caird says the exhibition was "a boost to national morale" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). The texts say little about trade or settlement. The government court included commerce and land development (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311), and Caird describes a settler's home room (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304), but neither text states an aim of trade or settlement.

Its place in the series

McCarthy says the fair looked more to the future than the earlier fairs at Dunedin and Christchurch, stressing material and technological progress (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 311). He notes that Anscombe had worked on the Dunedin exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310). He also places the Maori displays in a longer history of Maori exhibitions, citing his own book (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312).

How historians have interpreted it

Two authors interpret the fair: Conal McCarthy in text 35 and Felicity Caird in text 39. McCarthy reads the nationalism as modest and the Maori Court as an Indigenous attempt to find a place in modern New Zealand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312). Caird reads the fair as a tribute to progress and a boost to morale in wartime (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304). They differ on its outcome: McCarthy stresses the lost money and cancelled events (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310), while Caird calls it a great success (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial scope — Caird says that by 1938 the scope was set as imperial rather than international, because an international exhibition needed official recognition (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304).
  • Indigenous representation — McCarthy presents the Maori Court, run through the Native Department, as Ngata's challenge to official imagery that set a Maori past against a Pakeha present (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 312).
  • Nationalism and loyalty to Britain — McCarthy calls the nationalism modest and says it did not shake loyalty to Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310); Caird calls the fair a boost to national morale (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 304).
  • Wartime — McCarthy says the war limited participants and attractions and few overseas visitors came (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310); Caird calls the attendance a success given wartime conditions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 305).
  • Modern design — McCarthy describes the Art Deco architecture and the tower as among the fair's most memorable features (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 310).

Questions for study

  1. Caird says the scope became imperial because official recognition was needed for an international exhibition. What did being imperial change in practice, and which sources could show it?
  2. McCarthy reads the Maori Court as an Indigenous attempt to find a place in modern New Zealand. How far does the Native Department's role in the display qualify that reading?
  3. The war limited participants and visitors. Which exhibits and events would the organisers have planned without it?
  4. Caird calls the fair a great success, while McCarthy stresses the lost money. What measure of success does each author use?
  5. Why did a New Zealand fair include Samoa, Niue and the Cook Islands in its government court, and what does that suggest about New Zealand's own position in the empire?

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

On the web

Wikipedia

The New Zealand Centennial Exhibition took place over six months from Wednesday 8 November 1939 until 4 May 1940. It celebrated one hundred years since the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840 and the subsequent mass European settlement of New Zealand. 2,641,043 (2.6 million) visitors attended the exhibition. The New Zealand Government staged the exhibition with assistance from local government, New Zealand industry and the New Zealand public. The exhibition received support from the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Fiji and other Pacific islands who either constructed their own pavilions on site or had displays in one of the exhibition buildings.
