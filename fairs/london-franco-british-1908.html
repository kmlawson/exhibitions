Franco-British Exhibition

The Franco-British Exhibition ran at the White City, Shepherd’s Bush, in west London, from 14 May to 31 October 1908 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 198). Imre Kiralfy organised it for an Executive Committee that commissioned his company, and the site was split evenly between French and British displays to mark the Entente cordiale (the 1904 Anglo-French agreement) (Geppert 2010p. 111; Geppert 2010p. 112; Geppert 2010p. 115). The texts give it weight: Geppert calls it the first British imperial exposition that was consistently organized as spectacle (Geppert 2010p. 118), and Greenhalgh reads its villages, women’s palace and fine art as evidence of a Liberal Britain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 184).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Planning began in 1902, when two members of the British Empire League, Gerard Smith and Sir John Cockburn, proposed an imperial exhibition for London (Geppert 2010p. 109). The two encyclopedias date the planning to 1905, when the French Chamber of Commerce in London and the British Empire League worked with Kiralfy, an established exhibition entrepreneur (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 203; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 198). Geppert argues that the commercial prosperity of the two nations was the main objective from the start, whatever the official rhetoric said (Geppert 2010p. 110). At a meeting in July 1906 the organisers decided that any profits would eventually go to a public purpose (Geppert 2010p. 110).

Organisation and finance

The Executive Committee commissioned Kiralfy’s company, which Geppert names Shepherds Bush Exhibitions Ltd, and Kiralfy appeared in public as the sole organiser (Geppert 2010p. 111). Greenhalgh names the operating body the International and Commercial Company Ltd and says the fair followed the American company model (Greenhalgh 1988p. 40). The British government gave a guarantee fund and no pavilion, while the French government and the Chamber of Commerce subsidised the exhibition with more than £80,000 through the Comité, the French official body for foreign exhibitions (Geppert 2010p. 110; Geppert 2010p. 110). Receipts are reported in three different ways. The two encyclopedias give over £420,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 205), Badger’s appendix gives £798,771 (Badger 1979p. 131), and Geppert gives £1.2 million and a profit of £38,902 (Geppert 2010p. 111). The texts do not reconcile these figures.

Kiralfy called the fair, with 8.4 million visitors, the "summit of my life’s achievements" (Geppert 2010p. 105). Badger's appendix gives 8,396,673 visitors for the same fair (Badger 1979p. 131). The same row gives receipts of £798,771 and a cost of £783,521 (Badger 1979p. 131). On my reading of Badger's column headings, that leaves a surplus of £15,250, well short of the £38,902 profit that Geppert gives on receipts of £1.2 million (Geppert 2010p. 111). The texts do not reconcile these figures.

The fair also brought lawsuits. Australia's Agent-General sued the Franco-British authorities, Kiralfy among them, over lighting, space and advertising, and an Australian commissioner wrote that Kiralfy was "very niggardly of performance" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 91). Greenhalgh describes a different dispute with the Australian organisers. A new building partly blocked the frontal facade of their pavilion, Kiralfy did not appear to answer the complaint, and the case was settled with a partial refund of rent (Greenhalgh 1988p. 63).

Site and architecture

Geppert gives the site as 57 hectares, planned by Kiralfy with the architects John Belcher and Marius Toudoire (Geppert 2010p. 111; Geppert 2010p. 111). The encyclopedias describe it as about 140 acres of agricultural land in Shepherd’s Bush (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 198; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 203). The White City was built to last, with steel frames and concrete, and its Court of Honour and name were borrowed from Chicago’s 1893 fair (Geppert 2010p. 109; Geppert 2010p. 108; Geppert 2010p. 114). Greenhalgh shows that the plaster facades covered a steel framework, so that only the Flip-Flap and the stadium displayed engineering openly (Greenhalgh 1988p. 159).

Kiralfy allocated the sites himself before the architects were consulted (Geppert 2010p. 114). The grounds held 400 structures, among them twenty pavilions for British and French industry and 120 other buildings (Geppert 2010p. 114). The Machinery Hall was the largest building of its kind yet put up in Britain (Geppert 2010p. 112). Four stations on five railway lines made it possible to move 70,000 to 100,000 visitors an hour (Geppert 2010p. 111). Badger's appendix gives the area as 140 acres (Badger 1979p. 131). Geppert's 57 hectares is about 141 acres (Geppert 2010p. 111), so the two sources describe the same ground. Greenhalgh finds the Court of Honour a mixture of India and Britain, with minarets, onion domes and Indian tracery set on Edwardian baroque facades, and says that Kiralfy, as architect, fused the two countries in its design (Greenhalgh 1988p. 61). A critic at the time thought the style had little to do with the Entente theme and compared the site to a village of steel and stucco palaces (Greenhalgh 1988p. 60).

Attractions and the Flip-Flap

Merryland, the amusement section, was dominated by the Flip-Flap, a ride whose two steel arms swung carriages through the air (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 203). The texts disagree about its size. Geppert gives two arms 46 metres long, each carrying up to 70 passengers, and more than 1.1 million riders (Geppert 2010p. 119; Geppert 2010p. 119). The encyclopedia by Findling and Pelle gives a 150-foot lift (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 199), and the dictionary gives 200 feet (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 203). The 1908 Olympic Games were held on the site, with over 2,000 competitors from twenty-two nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 203). Kiralfy agreed to build the stadium after Rome withdrew as Olympic host, and Geppert says the Games were a sideshow with low attendance because of high admission prices and bad weather (Geppert 2010p. 108; Roche 2000p. 90). Roche reports 90,000 spectators on the final day (Roche 2000p. 90).

The sources also disagree about the Flip-Flap's capacity. Geppert gives up to seventy passengers in each arm (Geppert 2010p. 119), while Benedict says each arm carried forty-eight people (Benedict 1983p. 55). The ride was the site's only tall structure because the planned imperial tower was abandoned (Geppert 2010p. 119; Geppert 2010p. 228). Riders paid sixpence, and over 1.1 million passengers rode it (Geppert 2010p. 119).

The Olympic Games were the most disputed attraction. Geppert says that Rome gave up the Games after the eruption of Vesuvius in 1906, that Kiralfy took them on as a sideshow, and that high admission prices and bad weather kept attendance low (Geppert 2010p. 108). The stadium was the first venue built expressly for the Olympics (Geppert 2010p. 108). Roche records low early attendance but 90,000 people on the final day (Roche 2000p. 90). Greenhalgh is more emphatic: the stadium was full throughout the Games, bringing the organisers "over one hundred thousand fee-paying customers" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 45). That figure contradicts the low-attendance account given above. Roche says the marathon was won by Dorando Pietri (Roche 2000p. 90), while Geppert records that Pietri was disqualified after being helped over the line (Geppert 2010p. 108). Allwood's reference work says that the Great Stadium was later named White City Stadium (Allwood et al. 2001p. 89).

Nations and colonies on show

The colonial displays were grouped in the northern part of the site, where pavilions, native villages and rides stood together. Geppert says the dominion and colonial governments protested at this placing (Geppert 2010p. 114). The French side had ten colonial pavilions, including Tunis, Algeria, French East Africa and Indochina (Geppert 2010p. 120). Benedict argues that most ethnic classification at fairs took place in colonial displays, and says the Franco-British was organised on the basis of colonial identity (Benedict 1983p. 41). Hoffenberg reports that Canada, Australia and India had their own palaces, which were integrated into one imperial message (Hoffenberg 2001p. 123).

Peoples on display

The village displays are where the texts disagree most sharply. Geppert describes the Senegalese Village, with about 150 inhabitants under contract to two Frenchmen, and the Irish village, Ballymaclinton, where almost 200 young Irish women worked, named after a soap company (Geppert 2010p. 124; Geppert 2010p. 125). Greenhalgh says the Irish village was kept for the next three White City shows, and that showing Ireland as a village was inappropriate, given Ireland’s own international exhibitions since 1853 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 106; Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). Harvey, following Coombes, reads the Irish and Scottish villages as images of nations and the African villages as evidence of racial difference (Harvey 1996p. 71). Following Coombes, Harvey finds in the guidebook descriptions of Irish and Scottish villagers as healthy, beautiful and industrious evidence of an inherent superiority over the colonised races (Harvey 1996p. 71).

Geppert gives visitor figures for the villages: about 750,000 people at the Senegalese Village in three months, more than one million at the Ceylon Village, and about two million paying visitors to the Irish village (Geppert 2010p. 121). Ballymaclinton took its name from McClinton, an Irish soap company whose best-selling product was Colleen, and the village's profits were to fund a campaign against consumption (Geppert 2010p. 125; Geppert 2010p. 126). Greenhalgh ties the Irish village to the Ulster revolt then being stirred up against a Liberal government (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107). The Times listed Irish industries as handloom weaving, lacemaking, embroidery, carpets and soaps (Greenhalgh 1988p. 107).

Geppert uses an 1896 contract to suggest what performers faced. It set a ten-hour working day, damages of Rs 5000 for leaving early, and dismissal on one week's notice (Geppert 2010p. 123). After questions in the House of Commons about conditions in 1908, the programme of the 1909 Imperial International assured the public that the native villages were well looked after (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). Greenhalgh has no full catalogue to work from, but thinks Kiralfy probably booked some villages from the French, Dutch and Belgian governments and paid for those from the British empire himself (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92).

Visitors, reception and criticism

The encyclopedias give 123,000 visitors on the opening day and 130,000 on 31 August, the busiest day (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 198). Geppert says the fair became a weekend resort for Londoners, and that postcards and visitors’ memories show its popularity (Geppert 2010p. 131). Architectural critics were less enthusiastic. Carden of the Architectural Review deplored the lack of expressive originality, and Nixon Horsfield called Kiralfy an architectural dilettante (Geppert 2010p. 130; Geppert 2010p. 130). Greenhalgh describes the Palace of Women’s Work as a building the Times judged a missed opportunity, and reports that women employed on the site worked twelve-hour shifts with one rest room (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183; Greenhalgh 1988p. 186).

Women workers on the site

The fair employed large numbers of working-class women as cleaners, ticket-sellers and booth operators, usually in preference to men (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). Within weeks of the opening, pay and conditions became an issue, and Kiralfy was accused of exploitation (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). The Women's Industrial Council wrote to The Times that women on the site had none of the legal protection on hours and rest breaks that factory workers had (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). Their shifts ran from eleven to eleven, with few breaks (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). The only rest room was built by the YWCA and served more than a thousand women in one part of the grounds (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). The Council asked for a second rest room at the other end of the site, and Greenhalgh has found no record that it was built (Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). Meanwhile the displays in the Palace of Women's Work were arranged mainly by the daughters of committee members, and its architect was a man, Maurice Lucet (Greenhalgh 1988p. 185; Greenhalgh 1988p. 186). Benedict notes that European fairs generally had no women's buildings and lists the Franco-British palace among the few exceptions (Benedict 1983p. 40).

The fine arts and the jury

Greenhalgh says the Franco-British offered the fullest retrospective display of any British exhibition, with large collections of British and French art from the Renaissance to the end of the eighteenth century (Greenhalgh 1988p. 200). The French jury, headed by Léon Bonnat, decided to show no post-impressionism and only small numbers of impressionist works (Greenhalgh 1988p. 212). Greenhalgh suggests that this was partly a guess at what British visitors would accept (Greenhalgh 1988p. 212). The Times of 19 August 1908 reported that leading French critics were angry at the exclusion (Greenhalgh 1988p. 212). British critics were less put out, and on the whole found enough impressionism for their taste, if not too much (Greenhalgh 1988p. 212). A Daily Mail writer set the dazzle of French painting against the delight of English work and put the difference down to national temperament (Greenhalgh 1988p. 211).

Afterlife of the site

The White City ran annual exhibitions until 1914, but none drew as many visitors as the Franco-British, which had more than twice the attendance of any later show (Geppert 2010p. 116; Geppert 2010p. 117). By 1911 an official German visitor thought the site had lost its appeal to Londoners (Geppert 2010p. 117). During the war the site housed troops, then a medical inspection centre, an aeroplane factory and finally a dog track (Geppert 2010p. 117). It was offered for auction in November 1922 (Geppert 2010p. 117), and a sale prospectus of that year described the medium as family-friendly and class-transcending (Geppert 2010p. 206). The London County Council acquired the site in 1945 for housing, and the stadium, the last surviving structure, stood until its demolition in 1986 (Geppert 2010p. 117). In 1908 the British press had asked for a state body like the French comité, and a branch of the Board of Trade was set up for the purpose under Ulick Wintour (Geppert 2010p. 219).

How historians have interpreted it

Geppert argues that the fair’s bi-national design turned imperial and national themes into controlled spectacle, and that its legacy stayed within the exposition medium itself (Geppert 2010p. 116; Geppert 2010p. 117). Geppert also argues that historians who stress its diplomatic success take the official rhetoric at face value (Geppert 2010p. 116). Greenhalgh reads the Palace of Women’s Work as an appeasement of suffragist demands by a Liberal government, and calls the fair perhaps the last cultural manifestation of Liberal Britain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 184; Greenhalgh 1988p. 184). Hoffenberg places the displays of India and the settler colonies within an imperial message and within colonial economic nationalism (Hoffenberg 2001p. 123; Hoffenberg 2001p. 124). Roche treats the 1908 fair as part of a London series of imperial exhibitions (expos), which were used to communicate the imperial message to the public (Roche 2000p. 57). O’Mahony, writing on Nancy, reads the English amusements there, traced to London, as a municipal reinforcement of the Entente (Filipová 2015p. 280). The texts agree on the scale and popularity of the fair, but disagree on whether it mattered more as diplomacy, commerce or entertainment.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

India and Ceylon

The Indian presence was the largest Asian display, and its size depended on the Indian government, which joined late. Its pavilion ended up much smaller than planned, because the government gave less money than the organisers expected (Geppert 2010p. 308). Greenhalgh describes a splendid building, with minarets, towers and domes, that held a large selection of produce, and entertainments meant to explain Indian life under British rule (Greenhalgh 1988p. 60).

The Indian Arena staged an outdoor show, Our Indian Empire, with more than 150 acrobats, sorcerers, wrestlers, dancers and snake charmers. They performed two or three times a day under the direction of the Hamburg impresario Carl Hagenbeck, and each show ended in a tiger hunt with elephants (Geppert 2010p. 122). Geppert found no record of the performers themselves, and used a group of 132 Indian, Ceylonese and Burmese people from Kiralfy’s 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, held under unfavourable contracts, as a guide to their conditions (Geppert 2010p. 122; Geppert 2010p. 123).

The Ceylon Village had huts, a pagoda and bazaars. Geppert calls it the only ethnic display that directly represented British overseas colonialism (Geppert 2010p. 121). It drew more than one million visitors in three months (Geppert 2010p. 121). A visitor from Ceylon complained that the women and children shown had no connection with Ceylon (Geppert 2010p. 123). The Times disapproved of importing such performers, whom it judged too poor and illiterate. Geppert, who does not clearly separate this group from the 1896 one, notes that most were Tamils, though visitors took them for Indians (Geppert 2010p. 123).

Japan

The texts record no Japanese display at this fair. Japanese officials studied it closely, however. Komura, the Japanese ambassador in London, called the Franco-British an ideal example in January 1908, when urging Tokyo to consider a joint exhibition, and cited French government funds of one million yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). Komura visited the site with Kiralfy in July 1908 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 43). In 1909 the commissioners Wada Hikojiro and Beppu Ushitaro visited the White City and took advice from those who had run the Franco-British (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 48). Hotta-Lister suggests, as a possibility, that Kiralfy turned to France after failing to win Japan’s participation in a White City show (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). A reply to the Diet, the Japanese parliament, in 1909 compared the 1910 funding with the French allotment at the Franco-British (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 54).

Southeast Asia

Geppert lists the French colonial pavilions, ten in all, which included Indochina alongside Tunis, Algeria and French East Africa (Geppert 2010p. 120). No other Southeast Asian presence appears in the passages. Greenhalgh says the Tunisian and Algerian palaces comfortably equalled the one built for India, so that visitors found the two empires hard to tell apart (Greenhalgh 1988p. 68).

Reception and sales

Hoffenberg records that more than four hundred visitors bought Indian artware after the fair closed (Hoffenberg 2001p. 81). The main text says the goods sold at about half their original price, while a note gives one-fourth (Hoffenberg 2001p. 81; Hoffenberg 2001p. 340). An Indian tea association exhibited and sold tea at the fair, and the Princess of Wales visited its display (Hoffenberg 2001p. 114).

India and Ceylon

Geppert notes that the Ceylon Village was often called the Indian Village, and that it stood behind the Ceylon Tea House, near the Indian Palace (Geppert 2010p. 121). Greenhalgh says that Our Indian Empire played to an open-air theatre holding 3,000 spectators, with hundreds of Indian and Ceylonese performers and imported animals, in a programme of two hours (Greenhalgh 1988p. 92). Geppert counts more than 150 performers (Geppert 2010p. 122). Neither source names the performers, so the two counts cannot be checked against a list.

Japan

Hotta-Lister argues that the Palace of the Orient at the 1910 show presented Japan's colonial subjects, and that it followed the self-congratulatory imperial display that Britain and France had claimed for themselves in 1908 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 84). The Korean protectorate was fully represented in the same palace (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 83). The vice-chairman of the 1910 council reported that British manufacturers had been satisfied with the results of the Franco-British, so more space had been allotted to them in the new show (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 90).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Bi-national spectacle — The texts treat the fair as a deliberately equal pairing of French and British halves, and read that design as central to its meaning (Geppert 2010; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Colonial villages and pavilions — The villages and colonial pavilions are the most discussed feature, and the texts disagree on how far they were racial displays rather than colonial ones (Greenhalgh 1988; Geppert 2010; Benedict 1983; Harvey 1996).
  • The Flip-Flap and amusements — The ride and the rides around it are treated as the fair's commercial core, and the texts give conflicting heights (Geppert 2010; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Benedict 1983).
  • The 1908 Olympic Games — The stadium linked the fair to the Olympics, and the texts describe the Games as a sideshow with mixed attendance (Geppert 2010; Findling & Pelle 1990; Roche 2000; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Women and the Palace of Women's Work — The women's palace is read as an appeasement of suffragists, and Greenhalgh sets it against the working conditions of women on the site (Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Finance and receipts — The texts give receipts of over £420,000, £798,771 and £1.2 million, and only some of them explain how the figures were reached (Geppert 2010; Badger 1979; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Japanese and Indian observers — Japanese officials used the fair as a model and consulted its organisers, and Indian sales and tea exhibits appear in several passages (Hotta-Lister 1999; Hoffenberg 2001).

Questions for study

  1. How far did the equal French and British halves of the fair reflect real diplomacy, and how far were they an official ideal that the organisers used for publicity?
  2. Why did the organisers put the colonial villages in the amusement section, and does this placing change what the villages meant to visitors?
  3. Greenhalgh and Geppert read the Palace of Women's Work and the villages in different ways; what evidence would settle between an appeasement reading and a reading centred on spectacle?
  4. The texts give receipts from £420,000 to £1.2 million; what do these different figures suggest about the sources historians use for exhibition finances?
  5. Japanese officials used the Franco-British as an ideal case for their own exhibition; what did they take from it, and how did their later criticism complicate the claim that the 1910 show was a joint venture?
  6. Harvey and Roche connect the fair to nationhood and mega-events; can the fair be studied fairly alongside the Olympic Games, and what would be lost by separating them?
  7. Greenhalgh puts the stadium at more than 100,000 paying spectators, while Geppert and Roche describe low early attendance. Which records, such as ticket sales or press counts, would settle the question?
  8. Geppert and Roche disagree about who won the 1908 marathon, and Geppert and Benedict give different capacities for the Flip-Flap. What do these disagreements suggest about how far the fair's own publicity and the later press can be trusted as sources?
  9. Hotta-Lister reads the 1910 Japanese display as a copy of British and French imperial display. What would Japanese official or press writing from 1910 need to show for that reading to hold?

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
1Introduction: How to Read an Expositionp. 1

Geppert opens with a 1896 review by Georg Simmel of the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung, which he uses as a model for reading an exposition as a medium rather than a mirror of national identity. The book studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931, among them the Exposition Universelle of 1900, the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 and the British Empire Exhibition of 1924-25. Geppert argues that these events formed overlapping transnational networks, since organizers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and 'old town' sections from one another, and that the 'identity' framework of earlier work is too simple. He sets out a method of space, time and personae, drawing on Lefebvre and Chartier, and surveys the rise of exposition studies since the 1980s. The introduction admits that its choice of cases is partial, and it closes by promising five virtual visits that compare London, Paris and the would-be world city of Berlin.

4London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibitionp. 101Asia

Geppert uses the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 at London's White City to examine Imre Kiralfy, the Budapest-born impresario who turned international exhibitions into a private commercial business. The chapter traces his earlier London ventures at Olympia and Earl's Court, and the transnational networks, from Barnum to Coubertin, through which he borrowed ideas, above all from Chicago 1893. It then analyses the 1908 fair's bi-national design, its Court of Honour, its white architecture and the commercial purpose behind the Entente cordiale rhetoric. The key evidence comprises the Flip-Flap, the Ceylon, Senegalese and Irish 'villages', Old London, the Paris pavilion, press reviews and visitors' postcards. The chapter concludes that the fair's imperial spectacle was bound up with its dual nature, that its claims to diplomatic rapprochement rested more on faith in the medium than on evidence, and that the White City's fame proved short-lived.

In the contents of this work

7Conclusion: Exhibition Fatigue, or the Rise and Fall of a Mass Mediump. 201

Geppert's concluding chapter asks why world and imperial exhibitions, at their height around 1900, also drew sustained criticism. It traces the German term Ausstellungsmüdigkeit ('exhibition fatigue') from the diaries of the engineer Max Eyth, who worked at fairs in London, Vienna and Paris, through German debates from the 1880s to similar complaints in France and Britain. It then follows the institutional response: a German permanent exhibition commission, a British Board of Trade branch, and the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions. A proposed Anglo-German exhibition at the White City, which German officials blocked, shows how these fears worked in practice. The second half turns to sites. Geppert compares the Eiffel Tower, the Ferris wheels at Earl's Court and Paris, and the Berlin Funkturm, and treats the grounds in London, Paris and Berlin as 'cities within the city' that were absorbed into urban life in different ways. The chapter closes by proposing three elements of a theory of exposition practice: transience, the movement from space to place, and the chronotope of time and space.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
2Funding, politics and societyp. 27

Greenhalgh examines who paid for exhibitions and who controlled them, arguing that the events were saturated with liberal ideology and that backers wanted to maximise attendance, publicise a political position and make a profit. The Great Exhibition of 1851 is the model case: a Royal Commission, thousands of small sponsors and over four hundred local committees raised the money, and the event is read as a counter-revolutionary display staged after 1848. The chapter then compares French state funding for the Expositions Universelles with the company-run American fairs, from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939, and with Imre Kiralfy's commercial White City shows in London. It shows how attendance came to depend on popular entertainment, from the amusement areas of Paris in 1867 to the Chicago Midway in 1893, and it weighs apparent losses against the permanent buildings the fairs left behind. The chapter concludes that empire was the theme most likely to sustain audiences, which the next chapter takes up.

3Imperial displayp. 52Asia

Greenhalgh argues that empire was the theme most fully displayed at international exhibitions, and that its presentation changed between 1851 and 1940 while its presence never slackened. The chapter opens with India, the most carefully staged possession from the Great Exhibition onward, and traces how Indian courts grew from 30,000 square feet in 1851 to the Wembley palace of 1924, which cost £180,000 for the Indian site. It then turns to the handling of the white dominions and smaller colonies, to French displays of Algeria and Tunisia from 1839 through the Paris Exposition of 1889 to the colonial exhibition at Vincennes in 1931, and to the Portuguese, Dutch and Belgian sections, especially the Antwerp exposition of 1930. Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 close the survey, with the American and Japanese displays of empire. The chapter concludes that exhibitions joined a rhetoric of progress to organised exploitation, and so embodied neither good nor evil in any simple sense.

4Human showcasesp. 82Asia

Greenhalgh examines the practice of displaying people at international exhibitions between 1889 and 1914, when colonised and other non-Western peoples were housed in reconstructed villages and watched by paying crowds. He sorts the displays into four types (imperial, educational, commercial and ambassadorial) but argues that they overlapped, and that the imperial type was the most numerous and lavish. The chapter works through the Paris 1889 and 1900 native villages, the Jardin d'Acclimatation's peopled exhibits that preceded them, Imre Kiralfy's London spectacles, and the Chicago, Buffalo and St Louis fairs, where Native American, Filipino and Dahomeyan residents were placed within a supposed evolutionary hierarchy. Commercial examples include the Cairo Street and the Indian tea-houses, and the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes, who toured under private entrepreneurs, receive detailed attention. Greenhalgh concludes that the displays did lasting damage by teaching mass audiences that non-European peoples were inferior, and that this harm was never repaired.

In the contents of this work

6The prefabricated and the mass-producedp. 142

Greenhalgh traces how the international exhibitions shaped design and architecture from 1851 to 1939, and argues that the historicist-to-modernist shift was neither linear nor complete. The Great Exhibition revealed the tension between machine production and ornament, and critics often blamed industrial methods for what was really a question of symbolic taste. The chapter then follows the buildings: Paxton's prefabricated Crystal Palace, the Model House for the Working Classes, the housing section at Paris in 1867, and the engineering halls of Paris in 1889, set against the stone-clad Grand Palais of 1900. It shows art nouveau at Paris in 1900, Turin in 1902 and London in 1908, and the arrival of full modernism at Paris in 1925, Paris in 1937 and New York in 1939. Its conclusion is that international modernism failed through its methods, losing sight of ordinary urban life, and that the earlier exhibitions had more to teach than the later ones.

7Women: exhibiting and exhibitedp. 174

Greenhalgh examines what women did at exhibitions and how they were presented there, arguing that the women's buildings were both a platform and a constraint. O’Mahony’s chapter on the 1909 Nancy exposition connects that fair’s English-style amusements and gardens with the London White City of 1908. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Wikipedia

The Franco-British Exhibition was a large public fair held in London between 14 May and 31 October 1908. It was the first in the series of the White City Exhibitions. The exhibition attracted 8 million visitors and celebrated the Entente Cordiale signed in 1904 by the United Kingdom and France. The chief architect of the buildings was John Belcher. The Exhibition was held in an area of west London near Shepherd's Bush which is now called White City: the area acquired its name from the exhibition buildings which were all painted white. The 1908 Summer Olympics fencing events were held in the district alongside the festivities.
