Empire of India Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Empire of India Exhibition was held at Earl's Court in London in 1895 (Geppert 2010p. 251). Imre Kiralfy's company, The London Exhibitions Ltd, ran the site (Geppert 2010p. 105). Qureshi calls it the first show devoted solely to India, set up by a showman in collaboration with the government (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Its displays included over five hundred relics of the East India Company and a reconstructed Bombay street (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151; Hoffenberg 2001p. 159). Texts also record a re-run in 1896 as the Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 250).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and organisers

The Hungarian impresario Imre Kiralfy began in London at Olympia, where from 1891 he organised exhibitions with Harold T. Hartley (Geppert 2010p. 103; Geppert 2010p. 103). The London Exhibitions Ltd took over Earl's Court in 1894, with Hartley as managing director and Paul Cremieu-Javal as chairman (Geppert 2010p. 105; Geppert 2010p. 105). The texts disagree on the company's start. Greenhalgh says Kiralfy formed London Exhibitions Ltd in 1895 and staged the show that year (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). Hotta-Lister says Kiralfy registered London Exhibition Ltd in 1895, with his two sons and Sir John Cockburn (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39). Qureshi says Kiralfy moved to London after the 1893 Chicago fair and bought Earl's Court, without giving a date (Qureshi 2011p. 107).

Empire and colony

Qureshi describes the show as "the first devoted solely to India and set up by a showman", made in collaboration with the government (Qureshi 2011p. 250). The passages read name no government body involved (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Geppert groups the 1895 show with Earl's Court exhibitions that displayed "typical products of single colonies" (Geppert 2010p. 105).

Qureshi says that the India melodrama in the programme was intended to promote British rule in India (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Hoffenberg says that the relics in the overseas courts built a public history of the Raj (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151). Kiralfy solicited and displayed over five hundred items under the title "Relics and Memorials of the Late Honourable East India Company" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151). Visitors saw manuscripts, seals, medals, petitions and portraits of influential Anglo-Indians (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151). Many of the exhibits were copies or photographs of the originals (Hoffenberg 2001p. 152).

The passages read do not describe a native village for this show. Qureshi says that Kiralfy custom-built an entire site for it (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Hoffenberg says that projects of this kind used a sense of Orientalism to promote consumption and trade (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159).

Exhibits and attractions

Kiralfy commissioned the British engineer Walter B. Bassett to build a Great Wheel for the restructured grounds (Geppert 2010p. 229). Geppert says it opened after delay on the occasion of the Empire of India Exhibition in July 1895 (Geppert 2010p. 229). He calls Kiralfy's wheel, a Ferris wheel, "the first of many copies" (Geppert 2010p. 230). It was over 90 metres high (Geppert 2010p. 230).

Geppert also lists a Great Wheel among the vertical structures of the 1896 Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 228). The texts therefore leave open which show had the wheel, although Geppert dates its opening to July 1895 (Geppert 2010p. 229).

Qureshi says the programme contained India, an imperial melodrama by Kiralfy that covered a thousand years of Indian history (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Greenhalgh and Geppert place Kiralfy's 1895 India spectacle at Olympia, not at Earl's Court (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Geppert 2010p. 103). The texts do not settle whether the melodrama belonged to the Earl's Court show.

Who took part

Qureshi says that Kiralfy imported South Asians to work as performers and craftsmen (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Hoffenberg says that Kiralfy's Bombay street, Benares temple and Lahore shop fronts drew on photographs, sketchbooks and woodwork from condemned houses in Poona (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159; Hoffenberg 2001p. 160). Hoffenberg also reports Clarke's point that South Asian weavers and metalworkers were brought to the shows to make goods in front of visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 160). No text gives a number for the South Asian performers in this show (Qureshi 2011p. 250).

Visitors and reception

The passages read give no attendance figure for the show (Qureshi 2011p. 250). In a letter to Birdwood, C. Purdon Clarke judged the Empire Exhibition a success for its visitors (Hoffenberg 2001p. 160). The notes date Clarke's letters to July to September 1894 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 340). Geppert says the Earl's Court shows were very popular and made Kiralfy an expert in the public's eyes (Geppert 2010p. 105).

Qureshi reports commercial failure and a bitter winter for the performers, but the sentence sits beside a reference to an 1885 Indian village in Battersea Park. The text does not clearly apply it to this show (Qureshi 2011p. 253).

Its place in the series

Qureshi and Geppert date a re-run to 1896 as the Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 250; Geppert 2010p. 105). Greenhalgh names a different 1896 show, the India, Ceylon, Borneo and Burma Show (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). Geppert records that the 1896 show employed Indian, Ceylonese and Burmese artisans (Geppert 2010p. 122). Their contracts obliged them to pay for their own return journey if they withdrew (Geppert 2010p. 122). Hotta-Lister reports that Komura assured Hayashi that the proposed Japanese exhibition would differ from the Earl's Court shows that Kiralfy had organised (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41).

How historians have interpreted it

Hoffenberg reads the relics and bazaars as displays that built a public history of the Raj and promoted consumption and trade (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151; Hoffenberg 2001p. 159). Geppert describes Kiralfy's ambition at Earl's Court as a centre of pageantry and imperial pride (Geppert 2010p. 103). Qureshi reports a view, which he quotes from another author, that the India production was significant for its scale, its authenticity and its aim of historical verisimilitude (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Greenhalgh argues that Kiralfy used the native village as a medium for his imperial beliefs, a claim he makes about Kiralfy's exhibitions in general (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

South Asian performers and craftsmen

South Asian performers and craftsmen worked in the show, and Qureshi says Kiralfy imported them for this purpose (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Hoffenberg says that Kiralfy and Birdwood recreated Indian village bazaars with artisans at exhibitions in London and European cities (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159). The relics were from the East India Company (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151). Ceylonese and Burmese artisans appear only in the 1896 show (Geppert 2010p. 122; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). The passages read name no Japanese, Chinese or Korean participation in the 1895 show (Qureshi 2011p. 250).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial spectacle — Qureshi treats the show as a theatrical argument for British rule, through the India melodrama in its programme (Qureshi 2011p. 250).
  • Relics and public history — Hoffenberg treats the East India Company relics as a way of building a public history of the Raj and naturalising an Anglo-Indian presence (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151; Hoffenberg 2001p. 151).
  • Orientalist bazaars and trade — Bazaars, artisans and goods were used to suggest authenticity and to promote consumption and trade (Hoffenberg 2001p. 159; Hoffenberg 2001p. 160).
  • Commercial entertainment — Geppert describes the Earl's Court shows as very popular, and Greenhalgh says Kiralfy aimed to entertain and propagandise rather than educate (Geppert 2010p. 105; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).
  • Technology of overview — The Great Wheel is treated as a technical attraction, and Geppert calls it the first of many copies (Geppert 2010p. 230).
  • Conflicting sources — The texts disagree on the company's start date, on the site of the India spectacle, and on which show had the Great Wheel (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 39; Geppert 2010p. 105; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Geppert 2010p. 228).

Questions for study

  1. Qureshi places the India melodrama in the Earl's Court programme, while Greenhalgh and Geppert place Kiralfy's 1895 India spectacle at Olympia (Qureshi 2011p. 250; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Geppert 2010p. 103). Were these one show or two?
  2. Clarke's letter, dated in the notes to 1894, describes the Empire Exhibition before the 1895 date given by most texts (Hoffenberg 2001p. 160; Hoffenberg 2001p. 340). What does this suggest about how the show was planned?
  3. Who chose the East India Company relics, and who saw them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 151; Hoffenberg 2001p. 151)?
  4. Qureshi says the performers were imported to work as performers and craftsmen (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Do the texts say what their working conditions were, and how Geppert's contract evidence from 1896 relates to them (Geppert 2010p. 122)?

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
5Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolisp. 134Asia

Geppert examines the British Empire Exhibition of 1924 and 1925 at Wembley, and the stadium built for it, treating the site as a suburban metropolis rather than a simple national display. The chapter opens with the 1990s plan to demolish the stadium, then recovers a forgotten layer of the ground: Watkin's Folly, an unfinished imitation of the Eiffel Tower abandoned in 1895, which ties Wembley to the London–Paris rivalry. The exhibition, revived in 1920 to answer London's lack of a monumental image as an imperial capital, is shown to order the Empire spatially through its symmetrical layout, Kipling's street names, reinforced-concrete pavilions and the Wembley lion emblem. The main cases are the Government Pavilion with its illuminated world map, the Indian pavilion, the West African 'Walled City' and a 1924 student protest over a newspaper article about it. Visitor accounts, from schoolchildren to a young Somali and German official reports, show how readily the imaginary world tour was accepted. The chapter concludes that Wembley was a self-referential celebration of Empire that also marked the beginning of its end.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
Introduction

The introduction presents world's fairs as a formative part of modern life, drawing around 100 million visitors to American fairs between 1876 and the First World War and shaping amusement parks, technologies and city design. It then sorts the scholarship on fairs into six schools, from producer-centred readings of cultural hegemony and audience-centred accounts of how visitors made their own meanings, to counter-hegemonic studies of the people displayed, Benedict's potlatch model with Susman's liminality, documentary histories of technology, and the collectors' tradition. Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, moving from the Crystal Palace model through Philadelphia in 1876 to Chicago in 1893 and Atlanta in 1895, and promoting overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The authors close by asking whether fairs, now tied to transnational corporations, will address the nuclear and ecological crises of the present or leave us whistling in the dark.

Geppert places the 1895 Empire of India Exhibition among the shows that Imre Kiralfy's company staged at Earl's Court after 1894. He groups these shows by theme: some displayed the products of a single colony, some were broad international exhibitions, and most had no official participants from abroad. In the conclusion he describes the Great Wheel that Kiralfy had built for the grounds and opened for the 1895 show, as part of his account of vertical overview technology. His argument is that Kiralfy's imperial spectacles were popular enterprises, and that the London shows helped make him an expert in the public's eyes.

“typical products of single colonies” p. 105
About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
ch. 5 'Terrae Nullius?', pp. 151-152 (l. 7901-7929) and pp. 159-160 (l. 8280-8307); notes 113, 114, 163, 164 (l. 16956-17111); bibliography (l. 19751-19762)

Hoffenberg shows that Imre Kiralfy's Empire of India Exhibition of 1895 used relics of the East India Company and reconstructed Indian streets to present India as an ancient and authentic place. He argues that these displays built a public history of the Raj and gave a sense of authenticity that promoted consumption and trade. The relics and bazaars also naturalised a creole Anglo-Indian presence. The show is one of the main examples in the chapter of how India's place in exhibitions depended on Orientalist display.

“Relics and Memorials of the Late Honourable East India Company” — exhibition's own title for the collection, as quoted in the text p. 151
About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
ch. 4 'Human showcases', p. 91 (index, l. 12469); biographical passage on Kiralfy

Greenhalgh's chapter on human showcases mentions Imre Kiralfy's Empire of India Exhibition only in a biographical passage about his career. He presents Kiralfy in 1895 as moving from spectacle toward imperial concerns, and then as staging the show at Earl's Court. In the same passage he says Kiralfy's career made empire a constant subject of his events, and he treats the native village as a dramatic medium for imperial beliefs. The passage gives no description of the show's contents.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Qureshi, Sadiah. Peoples on Parade: Exhibitions, Empire, and Anthropology in Nineteenth-Century Britain. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2011.
ch. 7 'The End of an Affair', p. 250 (l. 10784-10797); pp. 253 (l. 10864-10870)

Qureshi presents the 1895 Empire of India Exhibition as one of Imre Kiralfy's most pertinent shows: the first devoted solely to India, with a custom-built site, South Asian performers and craftsmen, and reconstructed Bombay, Lahore and Benares scenes. He says the show's India melodrama was significant for its scale, authenticity and historical aims. He places Kiralfy's show within the more lavish, commercially driven human displays of the late nineteenth century, where the aim was commercial success through visual impact.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Qureshi's book studies the display of living foreign peoples in nineteenth-century London, from the San exhibition at Exeter Hall in 1847 to Savage South Africa at Earl's Court in 1899. Its argument is that these shows were a large commercial entertainment, shaped by London's streets, printed promotion, managers and performers who consented on their own terms, and that they fed into ethnology and the scientific study of race rather than being only racist spectacle. Part One covers the streets, Part Two the promotion, the managers and the recruitment of performers, and Part Three the audiences' interpretations, the natural history court at the Crystal Palace and the learned societies, ending with the shift toward entertainment and imperial spectacle. The scope is London, with comparisons to Paris in 1889, Chicago in 1893 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present in this book as one group among many displayed peoples and as a frequent comparison, but not as a central subject. The text names them in about 74 of its 17,790 lines, mostly in Chapters Six and Seven and the notes. Chinese people appear in Chapter One as physiognomic types in Eliza Lynn's 'Passing Faces' and as settlers stranded in the London docks, and Japanese and Chinese shows are named in the introduction. Chapter Six gives the most sustained attention: Latham's natural history court at the Crystal Palace displayed Chinese, Tibetans, Malaysians and Javanese, and the guidebook's classification of the Chinese as the most civilized of the Mongolians is discussed. Chapter Seven adds Asian displays at other fairs, used as comparisons with London: the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition's 'Malay' and Straits Settlements performers and Chinese junks from Hong Kong, the Cochin-China settlement and Kampong-Javanese camp of Paris 1889, and the Chicago 1893 Midway with its Chinese and Javanese villages and Japanese bazaar. The book says little about the Asian performers themselves or their experiences in London, and South Asia, through the Empire of India exhibition of 1895, receives more attention as a contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Hotta-Lister lists the 1895 Empire of India Exhibition at Earl's Court among the imperial-themed shows that Imre Kiralfy's London Exhibition Ltd handled. The list is background to the Japanese organisers' approach to Kiralfy and to the White City.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Wikipedia

The Empire of India Exhibition took place at Earls Court in London in 1895. Indian scenery was reproduced and there were displays which reflected the country's past and present states. The overall theme was that modern India was the product of British patience and genius. It was organised by Imre Kiralfy. It featured the Great Wheel.
