Jamestown Tercentenary Exposition

The Jamestown Tercentenary Exposition opened on 26 April 1907 on Hampton Roads, near Norfolk, Virginia, and closed on 30 November 1907 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). It marked three hundred years since the first permanent English settlement in the New World, and its promoters sold it on the region's historical character (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193). The texts treat it as a display of American naval power, a booster project for Virginia and a financial failure (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The idea of a fair for the 300th anniversary of the settlement arose around 1900, and Lyon G. Tyler, president of the College of William and Mary, was among those who proposed it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). By early 1901 the Virginia legislature had endorsed a national tercentennial for 1907 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). Richmond was first chosen as the site, but Hampton Roads was picked (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). The committee saw the fair as a moneymaking enterprise and a way to advertise the state (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). Bowers says the fair was promoted on the region's historical character, yet the exposition "was transformed into a showcase for the nation’s new militarism and imperialism" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193).

Organisation and finance

Fitzhugh Lee was first chosen to raise money and barely reached the required million dollars (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). He asked Congress for $3 million; Congress complied but required that the exhibition celebrate the navy, army and marines (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). Maloney instead cites a Virginian-Pilot headline announcing that Congress had finally voted $1,325,000 in June 1906 (Maloney 2012p. 119). The texts do not reconcile the two sums. Bowers describes a feud between the president and the director general over hosting visiting dignitaries, which led James Barr to resign (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). Hoover says Henry St. George replaced Lee after Lee's death in 1905 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200), whereas Bowers names Harry St. George Tucker as left in charge (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196).

The fair closed with a loss put at about $2.5 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201); the Great Exhibitions table gives $2,450,330 (£510,485) (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Rydell (1993) reports that Seattle's planners found the Jamestown corporation owed $500,000 more than had been announced, and that the Seattle managers decided the cause was advertising (Rydell 1993p. 24; Rydell 1993p. 24). The texts do not reconcile that debt with the loss at closing.

Site, architecture and landscape

The texts give different site sizes: Bowers gives 340 acres at Sewall's Point (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193), while Hoover gives a 5,000-acre frontage (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). The ground was flat and badly drained, and of twenty planned major exhibit buildings only about half were finished by the opening (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). Hoover calls the architecture Greek revival (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200); Bowers says Colonial Revival was prominent (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). Warren H. Manning designed the grounds, using about a million native plants, which he said cost less than at any earlier exposition (Maloney 2012p. 118; Maloney 2012p. 121; Maloney 2012p. 126).

Before building began, the land lay among isolated farms and one hotel, the Pine Beach Ocean Resort Hotel (Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119). Maloney says the Exposition Landscape Department drained 1,000 acres, half of which was to go to the fairgrounds (Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119). That implies about 500 acres, a third figure beside Bowers's 340 and Hoover's 5,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200). Lake Sanford, a hundred-acre lake distant from the site, was also drained to ward off mosquitoes (Maloney 2012p. 226; Maloney 2012p. 226; Maloney 2012p. 226).

Manning's crew built a living fence two years before the opening: an 8-foot woven wire fence with two feet of barbed wire on top, hidden under Japanese evergreen honeysuckle and thousands of crimson rambler roses (Maloney 2012p. 120; Maloney 2012p. 120; Maloney 2012p. 120). Isaac Hicks & Sons of New York moved more than three thousand large trees, some over 30 inches across (Maloney 2012p. 121; Maloney 2012p. 121). The Powhatan Oak was kept. It was a live oak 4 to 5 feet across, with a canopy of more than 70 feet, and was reputed to be nearly a thousand years old (Maloney 2012p. 125; Maloney 2012p. 125).

The fair had no horticulture palace. A single States Exhibit Building, the U-shaped States Palace of about 2.5 acres on the east side of Lee's Parade, held the agriculture, horticulture and forestry displays (Maloney 2012p. 127; Maloney 2012p. 127; Maloney 2012p. 127). Maloney also describes the Colonial architecture mandated for the exposition buildings, which sides with Bowers against Hoover's Greek revival (Maloney 2012p. 124; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200).

Military display and the navy

Roosevelt came by private yacht to open the fair, and his first act was to review the Great White Fleet (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193). The fleet left Hampton Roads on 16 December 1907 for its voyage around the world (Maloney 2012p. 120). Bowers counts 52 ships, 43 of them American, and nearly 14,000 troops (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). "Militarism definitely played a central role in the exposition," Bowers writes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). Maloney adds that Roosevelt suggested the name War Path for the amusement area, and that the Advisory Board protested the fair's militarism (Maloney 2012p. 120; Maloney 2012p. 120). Navies from Great Britain, France, Germany, Austria, Brazil and the Netherlands took part (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201).

The main naval feature was a mixed concrete and wood pier, built for $400,000. Its two walkways each ran more than 2,500 feet into Hampton Roads and met at a single concrete span, the largest in the nation, which formed a lagoon for naval demonstrations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). Bowers adds that the fleet was already outdated and that many of its vessels were due to be decommissioned soon after their return (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). He also reports a great debate over how far the military and its war machines pervaded the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196).

The texts disagree on when the navy took over the site. Hoover says it became part of the Norfolk Naval Base ten years after the closing (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Bowers says "Several years later" the government bought the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197). Maloney dates the navy's purchase to after 1917 (Maloney 2012p. 129; Maloney 2012p. 129).

Exhibits, attractions and peoples on show

State, city and territorial buildings, and exhibits from seventeen nations, filled the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). Georgia built a replica of Bulloch Hall, and Massachusetts a replica of its old statehouse (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). The midway offered Wild West and animal shows, and Bowers calls a re-enactment of the Monitor and Merrimack battle the most popular show there (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). African Americans secured a substantial exhibit built under the Negro Development and Exposition Company (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). Hoover records a $100,000 grant from the Virginia General Assembly for it, and says Black people planned and executed it and that Roosevelt visited it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Greenhalgh describes a Negro Building designed by W. Sidney Pittman, paid for and run by the federal government, which stressed segregation rather than recognition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99; Greenhalgh 1988p. 99). The texts disagree on who paid. Bowers reports that many Black people encouraged a boycott, because Virginia had recently passed Jim Crow transport laws (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). Booker T. Washington visited the exhibit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197).

Visitors and reception

Hoover gives total attendance as 2,850,735, of whom only half paid (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Bowers gives 2.85 million visitors, of whom 1.25 million paid (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197); Rydell, Findling and Pelle say nearly three million, about half admitted free (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Opening-day crowds found the exhibits unfinished, and the adverse press followed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Hoover blames the small regional population, the adverse publicity and the inaccessibility of Norfolk (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Bowers adds complaints of price gouging by hotels and transport firms (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197). King Edward VII spent several days on site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196).

Aftermath and legacy

The company went into receivership less than a week after the fair closed on 30 November 1907 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Bowers says the government bought the grounds, minus the debt, and made them part of the Norfolk Naval Base (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197); Maloney dates the navy's purchase to after 1917 (Maloney 2012p. 129). Some state buildings survive on the National Historic Register (Maloney 2012p. 129). Maloney notes that Jamestown was the first and only American exposition whose site was destined to become a residential development (Maloney 2012p. 119), and argues that the City Beautiful movement took root there (Maloney 2012p. 129).

Money, contracts and land

Maloney says Manning worked to a severely constrained and constantly dwindling budget (Maloney 2012p. 120; Maloney 2012p. 120). Bowers records delays from labour scarcity, cost overruns, bad weather, site complications and poor transport (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193). He adds that the fair hoped to draw 3.5 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197). Many people complained of the costs, including national leaders who had lent the company large sums (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 197).

Real estate ran alongside the fair. Abbott Morris & Co., a Norfolk firm, took a half-page advertisement in the Virginian-Pilot that declared the exposition a certainty and advertised lots on Exposition avenue (Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119). Maloney notes that investors knew the grounds would become housing, so the outlying areas received infrastructure upgrades (Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 119).

Disorder on the War Path

Bowers says that "There were actually two War Paths," one inside the grounds and one outside them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). In July some 400 National Guardsmen, who felt that several showmen had exploited them, marched onto the midway, took it over and shut the venues they judged dishonest (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). After a melee and a few non-fatal injuries, the exposition police restored order (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196).

In late July a fire swept away several dozen hotels and War Path venues. Only one life was lost, that of an African American man, but Bowers says the main damage was economic and to the fair's reputation in the press (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). Hoover says the unfinished exhibits on opening day brought adverse publicity, which gave the fair the nickname "Jamestown Imposition" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201).

Women at the fair

Weimann lists the Jamestown Tercentennial among the fairs that housed a woman's building, which was a plantation house built of wood (Weimann 1981p. 598; Weimann 1981p. 598). At St Louis in 1904 the twenty-three Lady Managers met in a building that later became the Hall of Physics at Washington University (Weimann 1981p. 598; Weimann 1981p. 598). Hollengreen et al. list Jamestown among the fairs that showed replicas of historic landmarks (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129). They argue that those who organised such displays were usually upper or upper-middle class white women active in preservation at home (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 129).

How historians have interpreted it

The readings differ more in emphasis than in facts. Bowers treats the fair as a showcase for the nation's new militarism and imperialism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 193). Hoover reads it as a booster project that failed financially but succeeded in promoting Virginia, and argues that its military emphasis exemplified the jingoism of the American nation at the time (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Hoover also judges that the fair "improved as time passed but never was a complete success" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Rydell, Findling and Pelle place Jamestown among the fairs that reworked the nationalising synthesis first shown at Chicago in 1893 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell (1984) reads the southern expositions, Jamestown among them, as presenting a New South imbued with progress and patriotism (Rydell 1984). Greenhalgh reads the fair through race, as a publicity exercise that stressed segregation (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99). Maloney says the fair is often treated "as a footnote in world’s fair literature," but argues that its landscape is a fine example of urban planning (Maloney 2012p. 118; Maloney 2012p. 119).

The texts agree that the fair lost money and suffered from weak publicity and poor access (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). They disagree on site size, architectural style, attendance and paid-admission figures, the director's name, and who paid for the Black exhibit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Hoover and Bowers, who read the fair as an expression of national militarism, differ from Maloney, who reads it as a landscape and planning project.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The Philippine village

The Asian presence the texts record is almost entirely Filipino. Bowers describes a five-acre Philippine settlement with five tribes, including the Bagobos, but does not say who organised it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). Rydell says that Felder and Schneidewind, who had earlier run an Igorot village at the Portland exposition, held the Philippine Village concession on the midway, called the Warpath (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). The concession was a private venture, and the texts report no government role in running it. Rydell reports that the Bureau of Insular Affairs was told that forty new Filipinos bound for the fair were quartered in steerage, poorly clothed and fed, but that the government took no action (Rydell 1984).

Other Asian displays

Bowers mentions several displays from the Far East without naming a country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). The midway also had two burlesque shows, The Far East and The Orient, which Bowers says were in fact one show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196). The texts name no Japanese, Chinese or Korean display at Jamestown (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). The navies the texts name, from Great Britain, France, Germany, Austria, Brazil and the Netherlands, include no Asian power (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201). Bowers says the anthropological displays fitted a pattern created at earlier fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194). The texts say nothing about Asian commissioners, visitors or observers at Jamestown. Among the texts, only Rydell (1984) describes how the Filipino performers were treated, and he stresses the concessionaires' self-interest and the government's inaction (Rydell 1984).

The Igorot concessionaires before Jamestown

Rydell says that Felder and Schneidewind, who ran the Philippine Village, had raised the Igorots' wages, which the concessionaires held in trust, to prevent a mutiny in the village before the show went to Chicago (Rydell 1984). When Truman K. Hunt's Igorot company moved into a cross-town park, Felder and Schneidewind asked for an inspection of his operation. Hunt was in a Memphis jail the following year on charges of embezzling the Igorots' wages (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). For the time being, Rydell writes, the pair were "more careful, but not necessarily much better" (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). In 1913 Schneidewind was accused of embezzlement while the village toured Europe (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Militarism and naval display — the texts treat the Great White Fleet, the War Path and Congress's navy, army and marines proviso as the fair's defining feature, read as national militarism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 196; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Maloney 2012p. 120)
  • Boosterism and the New South — the fair promoted Virginia's history and the state's image, and Rydell (1984) reads it as one of the New South expositions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 200; Rydell 1984)
  • Segregation and the Black exhibit — a separate Negro exhibit with disputed funding; Greenhalgh reads it as a stress on segregation rather than recognition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201)
  • Finance and publicity — a loss of about $2.5 million, blamed on weak publicity, poor access and a small local population (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 201; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 24)
  • Landscape and planning — Manning's native planting and a site meant to become a residential town, which Maloney treats as the fair's lasting interest (Maloney 2012p. 119; Maloney 2012p. 129)
  • Peoples on show — Filipino performers and anthropological displays on the midway, with the concessionaires' self-interest in the foreground (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Rydell 1984)

Questions for study

  1. Why did a fair promoted as a celebration of Virginia's history end up dominated by naval and military display, and what does that suggest about national feeling in 1907?
  2. The texts give different figures for federal funding, site size and attendance; how should students weigh reference works against newspaper-based accounts?
  3. Who should be credited for the Black exhibit: the federal government, the state, a Black company, or the Black planners and organisers?
  4. Maloney calls the fair a footnote in world's fair literature; does the landscape deserve more attention than the fair's financial failure?
  5. How did the midway's commercial shows, and the anthropological displays of Filipinos and others, shape the way visitors saw non-white peoples?
  6. Was the loss mainly a publicity problem, as Rydell (1993) suggests, or were the fair's location and military theme the deeper causes?
  7. If the Bureau of Insular Affairs was told that the Filipinos were quartered in steerage, why did the government take no action, and what powers did it have over the concessionaires?
  8. Weimann places the Jamestown woman's building in a plantation house; what did a building of that style say about the women who used it, and who chose it?
  9. Hollengreen et al. link replica exhibits with preservation-minded women; did women's groups have any hand in the replicas of Bulloch Hall and the Massachusetts statehouse, as Bowers describes them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 194)?

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Jamestown Exposition, Jamestown, 1907: Native Plantings Frame City Planningp. 117

This chapter examines the landscape of the 1907 Jamestown Exposition in Virginia, a fair that never captured the nation's attention and is often treated as a footnote. Its central claim is that Warren Manning's design is a fine example of urban planning, since the fairground was intended to become a residential town after the fair. The chapter sets out the fair's financial problems, its military and maritime emphasis and a budget that kept shrinking. It then shows how Manning drained the swampy site, relied on native plants, had more than three thousand large trees moved and built a living fence of honeysuckle and roses. The evidence includes the street plan and its signature tree species, the Powhatan Oak, Flirtation Walk and the Canoe Trail, and the States Palace, which stood in place of a separate horticulture building. Manning's 1907 essay 'Civic Horticulture and Improvement' supplies the principles that the chapter applies to the plantings. It ends by arguing that the fair's small-town character, its native plantings and its waterfront design shaped later exposition planning.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
ch. 6, 'Native Plantings Frame City Planning' (Jamestown Exposition), pp. 117-131

Maloney gives the Jamestown Exposition a whole chapter, treating it as a landscape designed by Warren H. Manning. Maloney argues that the fair is a fine example of urban planning, because its grounds were planned to become a residential town after the fair and because native planting was used to cut costs and frame the city plan. Maloney places the fair within the City Beautiful movement and treats its military and naval emphasis as the setting Manning had to work within. The chapter also says the fair is often treated as a footnote in world's fair literature.

Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.

Rydell places Jamestown among the international fairs that spanned the South between 1885 and 1907, each presenting an image of a New South imbued with progress and patriotism. In a passage on the Filipino village concessions, Rydell follows Felder and Schneidewind, who held the Philippine Village on the Jamestown midway. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.

Rydell uses the Jamestown fair as a cautionary case of weak publicity. When the Seattle planners of 1909 consulted the Jamestown directors, they found the corporation's debt was $500,000, more than had been announced, and they decided the cause was advertising. Rydell sets this against the more extensive advertising of Buffalo's 1901 fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's book examines the American world's fairs of the 1920s and 1930s, the century-of-progress expositions, and argues that they were theatres of power offering abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy during the Depression. He places them in continuity with the Victorian fairs, tracing ethnological villages, eugenics displays, colonial exhibitions in Europe, scientists' exhibits and African American protests from Philadelphia in 1926 through Chicago, New York, Dallas and San Francisco to Brussels in 1958. The first part, An Exhibitionary Culture, covers forerunners and eugenics exhibitions; the second, The Century-of-Progress Expositions, moves through colonial modernism, science, future-oriented displays and racial politics. A closing chapter argues that the fairs helped shore up faith in government and the economic order.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, though the centre of gravity is the American fairs themselves. The material is concentrated in Chapter One, 'Forerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositions', and Chapter Three, 'Coloniale Moderne'. The Philippines is the main Southeast Asian case: the 1904 St Louis Philippines Reservation is read as a display of Filipinos as commodities of American empire, Igorot and Filipino villages at Seattle and Buffalo are ethnological displays organised by others, and the American annex at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is set against the Filipino and Puerto Rican protests that the official display erased. French Indochina recurs through the Angkor Wat replica at Paris in 1931 and the Indochina pavilion at San Francisco in 1939, where the Dutch East Indies and Johore also had pavilions in the Pacific Basin. Living displays of Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese at Wembley in 1924, and Chinese from a village concession used to promote the Nashville fair in 1897, show Asians as displayed peoples. Japan appears among the foreign pavilions at the 1926 Philadelphia Sesquicentennial and Japanese restaurants on the San Francisco Gayway are passing mentions. Asian visitors and Asian-held exhibitions are not discussed. The Brussels chapter and the science and African American chapters say nothing about the region. Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.

Rydell, Findling and Pelle place Jamestown among the fairs of 1895 to 1909 that they treat as threads in one web of expositions, each reworking the nationalizing synthesis shown at Chicago in 1893. In their survey of Southern fairs they describe the Jamestown fair's military and naval displays, staged at Roosevelt's urging, and its financial failure, which they put down to early negative publicity, a small local population and poor access. They also note that most Southern fairs of the period relegated African American exhibits to a separate Negro Building, as at Jamestown.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. Their argument is that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, and that business elites used them to promote overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. A Philippine Reservation at Buffalo in 1901, with Negrito and Igorot villages and Philippine Scouts, presented the islands as stages of American uplift. The federally backed Filipino village at St. Louis in 1904, the Igorot village at Portland in 1905 and the Filipino building at Seattle in 1909 followed. These were displays of Filipinos organised by American officials, promoters and anthropologists, not Filipino participation. China appears in a Chinese Village at Omaha in 1898, which the authors call little more than a souvenir shop, and in the Chinese village at San Francisco in 1915, which framed China as ripe for American investment. Japan appears through the Fair Japan concession at St. Louis, with its Ainu display, and through Japanese exhibits sent to Portland, which were hampered by reported discrimination against Asians at St. Louis. The atomic-age chapter mentions exhibits from newly independent Asian nations at New York in 1964-65, China's relics at Knoxville in 1982 and Korea at Taejon in 1993. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. Wikipedia

The Jamestown Exposition, also known as the Jamestown Ter-Centennial Exposition of 1907, was one of the many world's fairs and expositions that were popular in the United States in the early part of the 20th century. Commemorating the 300th anniversary of the founding of Jamestown in the Virginia Colony, it was held from April 26 to December 1, 1907, at Sewell's Point on Hampton Roads, in Norfolk, Virginia. It celebrated the first permanent English settlement in the present United States. In 1975, the 20 remaining exposition buildings were included on the National Register of Historic Places as a national historic district.
