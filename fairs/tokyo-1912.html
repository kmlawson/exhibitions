Colonial Exposition

Takushoku hakurankai

The Colonial Exposition (Takushoku hakurankai) was a Tokyo exhibition dated to 1912, and the timeline records it as held. Only one text names it, and it takes the name and date from Yamaji (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The texts give no organisers, site, dates or visitors for it (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). Hotta-Lister describe a different Tokyo exhibition planned for 1912, the Grand Japanese Exhibition, which was postponed and then abandoned (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The texts do not say whether the two were one event.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The exhibition in the texts

Shepherdson-Scott mentions the exhibition once, in a note to her study of Japanese displays of Manchuria at the Chicago World's Fair of 1933-34 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 538; Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). Citing Yamaji, she calls it the first appearance of a Manchuria exhibit in a Japanese exhibition context (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). Its Manchurian Reference Pavilion (Manshū sankōkan) commemorated the war dead and the 203 battle sites on the continent (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). The display reportedly "appealed to the hearts of the Japanese people" (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546), for whom the Russo-Japanese War was still fresh (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546).

The text gives no account of the pavilion's design, visitors or organisers (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). Shepherdson-Scott's wider argument turns on a Manchuria pavilion at the 1915 Kyoto enthronement exposition, which drew on continental architecture (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546; Shepherdson-Scottp. 547).

The Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1912

Hotta-Lister describe a Tokyo exhibition planned for 1912 under the name Nippon Dai Hakuran Kai, the Grand Japanese Exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). It was proposed in April 1907 for 1 April to 31 October 1912, on a 250-acre site in Tokyo, with a budget of 20 million yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). Kaneko Kentaro was elected chairman of its committee in August 1907 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). In early 1908 the government postponed it to 1917 because of money problems after the war with Russia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). In 1912 it was postponed again, this time indefinitely, and it was abandoned once the Great War began in 1914 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Neither text says whether this exhibition and the Colonial Exposition were one event. Shepherdson-Scott places the Colonial Exposition in Tokyo in 1912 (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546). If the two were one, Hotta-Lister's account would contradict the timeline record that it was held (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). A source that names both would settle the question.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Manchuria as a memorial display — the pavilion is said to commemorate the war dead and 203 battle sites on the continent (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546).
  • War memory and public appeal — Yamaji's claim is that the display moved Japanese visitors because the Russo-Japanese War was still recent (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546).
  • Precedent for later Manchuria displays — Shepherdson-Scott cites it as the first such display in a Japanese exhibition context, and discusses earlier continental designs for Manchuria pavilions (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546; Shepherdson-Scottp. 546).
  • Postponement and abandonment — the Grand Japanese Exhibition was postponed twice, the first time for money, and then dropped (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Questions for study

  1. Was the 1912 Colonial Exposition a separate event from the Grand Japanese Exhibition that Hotta-Lister describe as postponed and dropped, and which sources would settle it? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217)
  2. What did a display built around war dead and battle sites ask Japanese visitors to feel about Manchuria, and what evidence beyond Yamaji's reported view would show it? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546)
  3. Why did the cost of the Grand Japanese Exhibition exceed its first estimate, and how did its financing compare with the London exhibition? (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 189)
  4. How far does a single footnote about a 1912 pavilion support Shepherdson-Scott's account of continental design in Manchuria pavilions? (Shepherdson-Scottp. 546; Shepherdson-Scottp. 546)

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
2Preparationsp. 38Asia

Hotta-Lister shows how deeply the Japanese government committed itself to the London exhibition and how far the two sides differed in approach. The idea came from the entrepreneur Imre Kiralfy, who had staged imperial spectacles and organised the White City site; Komura took it up in Tokyo, while Kato, Mutsu Hirokichi and the commissioners Wada and Beppu handled it in London. The chapter reconstructs the earlier rejections of Kiralfy's plan and the rivalry with possible American and Russian exhibitions. It then covers the 1.8 million yen budget, the exhibitors' association, the selection of 19,943 articles from 1,286 exhibitors, and the gardens, models and entertainers shipped from Japan. British responses were lukewarm: the Board of Trade and Grey gave little official support, while a General Committee under the Duke of Norfolk formed and the Japan Society was given its own space. It ends with the opening on 14 May 1910, two days late after the death of Edward VII.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 4

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
Untitled opening section (no heading in text)p. 1Asia

Coté opens by placing the Semarang International Colonial Exhibition of 1914 within the history of world's fairs, which he presents as stages for a discourse of progress and a pedagogy of mass education. Colonial exhibitions, he argues, carried the same didactic aims as European ones but faced a particular problem: could native audiences read them correctly? Drawing on Prakash's account of British India and on Dutch participation in world exhibitions from London in 1851 to Paris in 1931, he shows how the Netherlands used such displays to present itself abroad. The Semarang exhibition, planned in 1912, postponed from 1913 and staged by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, is described through its scale, its architecture, its electric lighting and the foreign pavilions of Japan, China and two Australian states. Coté concludes that the exhibition, opening as war began in Europe, was a settler-led showcase whose meanings could not be fixed, and that it marked the beginning of the end of the imperial future it celebrated.

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
4From the Opening Day Until the End of the Exhibitionp. 100Asia

This chapter narrates the Exhibition from its opening on 14 May 1910 to the receipt of the official reports in 1912. Hotta-Lister estimate attendance at over eight million and describe the evening illuminations, fireworks and package tours that drew crowds. Most of the chapter is a dated record of visits and events: Prince Fushimi's attendance at King Edward's funeral, banquets for British and Japanese dignitaries, Japan Day, and visits by Kitchener, Marie Stopes, the Swedish royal couple and the Brazilian president-elect. It also records a private viewing of retrospective Japanese art for about 350 European experts. The authors then trace the afterlife of the exhibits, which were shipped home, given to British and European institutions, or sent to exhibitions in Budapest, Dresden and Turin. Little is argued here; the chapter is a record of ceremony and diplomacy that frames the reactions discussed next.

In the contents of this work

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Panama-Pacific International Exposition, San Francisco, 1915: East Meets West in the Gardenp. 133Asia

This chapter examines the landscape of the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco, which it presents as a fair that joined Eastern and Western garden traditions on land made from the bay. It explains how the Harbor View site was created by dredging, and how Edward Bennett's block plan of courtyards was undermined when he resigned in 1912 after accusing the city's Department of Works of pernicious influence. Most of the chapter concerns John McLaren, the local landscape designer whose plantings are described in detail: the ice plant hedge that imitated an aged wall, the palm and cypress avenues, and the courts that the chapter treats as early small-space gardens. Its other principal cases are the Palace of Fine Arts and its lagoon, the Japanese garden, and the Horticulture Palace, whose exhibits moved from single fruit specimens to commercial processes. The chapter also covers the quarantine inspections that imported plants required. It ends by tracing the fair's legacy through the trees planted in Golden Gate Park and the de Sabla garden at El Cerrito.

In the contents of this work

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
2The Master of the Formp. 39Asia

Peterson argues that by 1915 Japan had become the master of the international exhibition format, and that its self-representation at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco was built on staged encounters rather than on objects alone. He starts with diplomacy: the 1912 groundbreaking, the gold 'Loving Cup' and the public performances of Japanese and American officials that presented the two countries as peaceful partners on the Pacific. The first and longest case is the Japanese government site, with its temple-like pavilion and garden, where Peterson reads photographs of kimono-clad women as evidence of how fairgoers performed an emotional attachment to the place. He then turns to the commercial 'Japan Beautiful' concession in the Joy Zone, with its miniature Asakusa-style village, geisha and Mount Fuji tea house, then to the trade halls selling ceramics, lacquer and silk, and to the fine arts galleries, where San Francisco critics praised traditional work and dismissed Western-style painting. The chapter ends by arguing that Japan knew what America wanted to see, a pre-modern and refined nation, and supplied it.

Hotta-Lister's book is about the 1910 Japan-British Exhibition in London. Its passages on Tokyo concern a different planned event, the Grand Japanese Exhibition (Nippon Dai Hakuran Kai), which was to run in Tokyo from 1 April to 31 October 1912. The authors describe its postponement to 1917 for financial reasons, its second postponement, and its abandonment when the Great War began, and they argue that the London exhibition was meant partly to advertise the 1917 plan. The text never names the Takushoku hakurankai and does not say whether the two events were one, so these pages bear on this fair only as a possible conflict with the timeline record that it was held.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

Brief discussion 1

Shepherdson-Scott, Kari. “Conflicting Politics and Contesting Borders: Exhibiting (Japanese) Manchuria at the Chicago World’s Fair, 1933–34.” Journal article.
section 'Selling Japanese Manchuria', note 11 (printed p. 546)

Shepherdson-Scott's article is about Japanese displays of Manchuria at the Chicago World's Fair of 1933-34, and mentions the 1912 Tokyo colonial exposition once, in a note that cites Yamaji. The note says the exposition's 'Manchurian Reference Pavilion' was the first Manchuria exhibit in a Japanese exhibition context and that it commemorated the war dead and 203 battle sites. The author uses this as background to her argument that the Chicago pavilion distanced Manchuria from China and presented Manchukuo as a sovereign nation, in contrast with earlier pavilions that drew on continental motifs.

“appealed to the hearts of the Japanese people” — quoted by the author from Yamaji 2008, 151 p. 546
About the work as a whole Asia: central

Shepherdson-Scott examines the Manchuria pavilion that the South Manchuria Railway Company and the Manchukuo government sponsored at Chicago's A Century of Progress fair in 1933 and 1934, months after Manchukuo was founded and Japan left the League of Nations. The argument is that this small building, which drew about two percent of fairgoers, was a diplomatic instrument aimed at American opinion, and that Mantetsu let the exhibit present Manchuria as an economic project rather than a political one. The paper also treats the American-sponsored Golden Temple of Jehol, whose site and authenticity contested the pavilion's maps, which placed the recently occupied province of Jehol inside Manchukuo. It concludes that the fair never sold a single idea of Manchuria.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Manchuria is the subject of the whole article, so East Asia is central, but the coverage is confined to a few states. Japan is the organiser: the government, the Kwantung Army, Mantetsu and the exhibition committees shape the pavilion, which chapter 2 reads against Japan's own Kyoto Manchuria pavilion of 1915 and the Qing-inspired architecture it dropped. Manchukuo is treated as a new state whose sovereignty the exhibit had to perform. China enters as the party whose planned display prompted Japan to participate, and chapter 3 is concentrated on the Golden Temple of Jehol, a replica built in Beijing by Chinese craftsmen and sited beside the China and Japan exhibits. Mongolia appears through Jehol and the Sino-Mongolian culture Bendix claimed to conserve. Korea appears only as a Japanese colony shown on Mantetsu's map, its border with Manchukuo blurred. Southeast Asia is absent. None of its countries, colonies or peoples is named in the text, and it discusses no exhibition held in Southeast Asia. Its Asian comparisons are confined to Japan, China, Korea and Manchuria, and the wider 'Asia' language is used for the Japanese imperial project in Northeast Asia.
