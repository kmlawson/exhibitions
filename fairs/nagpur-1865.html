Nagpur Exhibition of Arts, Manufactures and Produce

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Nagpore Exhibition of Arts, Manufactures and Produce was held in 1865 at Nagpore, a city in the Central Provinces of British India, and its report is dated December 1865 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104p. 322). Sir Richard Temple, Chief Commissioner of the Central Provinces, is named as its organiser (Hoffenberg 2001p. 284). Hoffenberg uses the show to examine the hope that exhibitions would sell European goods and spread new machinery, and to show how visitors were sorted by race at the door (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104p. 187p. 237). Coté mentions it only in a footnote, spelled Nagapur (Cotép. 36).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Empire and colony

John Forbes Royle, an early advocate of exhibitions, wanted colonial shows to test, compare and sell colonial products and so enhance imperial trade links (Hoffenberg 2001p. 103). Local Indian exhibitions at Nagpore and Agra were to serve the same purpose, and Royle and other Anglo-Indian officials struggled there to introduce European consumer items (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104). The hope was not always met. After the close of the 1865 show, Nagpore officials were left with mountains of unsold, "unredeemable" European goods (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104).

Government money supported the show. Government funds contributed to exhibitions at Nagpore and elsewhere, and local authorities gave public land for Indian shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 88). Hoffenberg sets this colonial official support against popular perceptions of the English government, whose uncertain role weakened English exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 88). Temple is named as the organiser of the Nagpore show in an appendix of exhibition commissioners (Hoffenberg 2001p. 284).

The passages describe no colonised people displayed as exhibits at Nagpore. They describe South Asian visitors, artisans and princes (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189p. 237). Officers of the Royal Asiatic Society in Calcutta proposed showing typical examples of the races of the old world at the shows in Calcutta, Jubbulpore and Nagpore, but the passage does not say whether this was done at Nagpore (Hoffenberg 2001p. 220).

Machines and demonstrations

Commissioners at Nagpore in 1865 built two special sheds with galvanized iron roofs for English and European technology. Cotton gins, steam engines and pumps were run in them, and the iron-processing and threshing machines took more space than the poultry, horses, minerals and forest exhibits (Hoffenberg 2001p. 187). Hoffenberg argues that Temple and Royle expected far less immediate purchase than Australian commissioners did, and that the displays could not by themselves spread the machines, because of deeper divisions in Indian and English societies and in the imperial relationships of the Raj (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189).

The secretary to the chief commissioner reported that cotton gins, threshers and steam engines drew large numbers of South Asian visitors. The chairman of the Machinery Subcommittee hoped that European and English technology would give the native workman a fairer place in competition with overseas workers (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189). Frustrated commissioners then proposed that the Nagpore School of Design buy European machines for long-term display. The secretary wrote that "natives cannot understand a new thing, unless it be held up before their eyes with something of continuous performance" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 190). Temple agreed that the practical gains would show only with nearly permanent display in museums and shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 190).

Anglo-Indian officials had earlier doubted that machines shown at Nagpore and Bombay could be sold for local use. One influential commissioner thought sales would follow only if the local Resident forced landlords and manufacturers to buy them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 169).

Visitors and policing

In 1865 the Nagpore commissioners provided a force of over three hundred police to protect the exhibits and control the expected crowds, and sentries lined the building that held jewels and other valuable property (Hoffenberg 2001p. 235). Hoffenberg writes that at Nagpore "organizers created two entrances to distinguish European from non-European visitors" (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237). Officers and European visitors entered from the West, while princes, zamindars and native artisans from the city used the East Door (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237). Hoffenberg reads this as an attempt to sort visitors by race and political authority, but once inside, he argues, the visitors formed a more complex colonial society (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237).

Local visitors appeared awed by the European and English cotton gins and steam engines. The exhibition secretary warned that one show, never repeated, would be like a flash of light that left everything as dark as before (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193).

Aftermath

Hoffenberg writes that museums, technical and design schools naturalised the new machinery shown at exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193). Anglo-Indian officials argued that demonstrations would have to be repeated and that models should be kept at government model farms, public museums and schools (Hoffenberg 2001p. 193). After the Colonial and Indian Exhibition at South Kensington in 1886, figures of Indian peoples cast at the Calcutta School of Art were sent to several institutions, among them the Nagpore Museum (Hoffenberg 2001p. 150).

Its place in the series

In a footnote to his article on Semarang, Coté lists the Nagapur show of 1865 among similar colonial exhibitions regularly held in India, with Fureedpur in 1873, Calcutta in 1883 and Allahabad in 1911, citing Prakash (1992) (Cotép. 36). The footnote gives no further detail on the Nagapur show (Cotép. 36).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial trade — Royle and Anglo-Indian officials saw Indian shows as a way to sell European goods and promote colonial produce, and the Nagpore goods were left unsold (Hoffenberg 2001p. 103).
  • Machines and progress — two sheds of European machinery were shown as progress, but Hoffenberg argues the displays alone could not spread them (Hoffenberg 2001p. 187p. 189).
  • Race and entrances — separate doors and a large police force sorted visitors by race and rank (Hoffenberg 2001p. 235p. 237).
  • Local visitors — large numbers of South Asian visitors watched the gins and engines, and the secretary doubted that a single show would have lasting effect (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189p. 193).
  • Museums and permanence — repeated displays and museum models were seen as the way to make new technology lasting (Hoffenberg 2001p. 190p. 193p. 150).

Questions for study

  1. Why did officials expect a local Indian exhibition to sell European goods, and what did the unsold stock show about that expectation? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 104)
  2. Hoffenberg says displays could not spread machines alone. What other conditions do the passages name, and how far do they explain the limited diffusion? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 189)
  3. Separate entrances were meant to sort visitors by race and rank, yet visitors mixed once inside. What does this suggest about the limits of colonial order at exhibitions? (Hoffenberg 2001p. 237)
  4. Coté lists the Nagapur show with three other colonial exhibitions in India. What would a comparison of these shows need to show, given that the footnote gives no detail? (Cotép. 36)

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
7Imperial and National Taxonomies: Entertaining and Policing Exhibition Visitorsp. 203Asia

Hoffenberg turns from exhibits to the people who visited exhibitions and to the ways commissioners classified, entertained and policed them. Visitors to the Great Exhibition of 1851, the London International of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 are treated as a visible social mass that organisers sorted by nation, race, class and gender. The evidence includes reduced fares for London workers, the appeal to guilds from neighbouring colonies for the Melbourne Intercolonial of 1866 to 1867, and the controversial zenana day at the Calcutta International of 1883 to 1884, when women in purdah were admitted to the grounds. Maori and Fijian performers at Sydney in 1879, and Aboriginal visitors from mission stations at Adelaide in 1887, are set against the static ethnographic models shown elsewhere, and the separate entrances at Nagpore in 1865 show how race and rank were policed at the door. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions offered social integration and imperial federation in economic and cultural terms, a citizenship that was real but not political. The chapter closes by linking these practices of ordering visitors to later management of migration in the twentieth century.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Coté's article examines the International Colonial Exhibition held in Semarang, Dutch East Indies, in 1914. It was organised by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, and it opened as war broke out in Europe. Coté argues that colonial exhibitions shared the didactic aims of European world's fairs but faced a particular problem: the colonised visitor was both audience and exhibit, so the pedagogy of progress could not be fixed in meaning. He reads the plan, architecture, native section, thematic layout, women's pavilion and poor attendance as expressions of a settler vision of colonial modernity. The article moves from an opening survey through chapters on the exhibition's visible and invisible meanings, its design, its ordering of visitors, the spectator, the women's pavilion and outcomes, to a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Southeast Asia is the whole subject: the exhibition was held in Semarang, Java, and the article concerns the Dutch East Indies throughout. Its Javanese and colonial materials are read closely in chapter 1 (visible and invisible meanings), chapter 3 (Defining a colonial utopia, on the native section, the regional houses and the temple), chapter 4 (Establishing proper order, on the native agriculture and housing displays) and chapter 7 (Outcomes, which analyses attendance of 306,795 Javanese against 370,472 Europeans). The Javanese spectator and the nationalist reading of 'tradition' are the subject of chapter 5 and the conclusion, which mentions Minangkabau architecture. No other Southeast Asian territory is discussed. The comparison with British India and Australia marks South Asia as a contrast only. East Asia enters as foreign participation and as a political reference point. The opening section names the official Japanese and Chinese displays and reads them against the European war and suspected Japanese ambitions in the region, describing the Japanese buildings as impressive. Chapter 4 treats the Chinese pavilion, financed by Oei Tiong Ham, as the one community that escaped European control, and the women's pavilion in chapter 6 includes a Chinese girls' school. The conclusion calls the Japanese and Chinese displays 'provocative models for an autonomous Asian future'. These passages are brief and comparative: neither country is examined as an exhibitor in its own right, and no exhibition held in East Asia is discussed.
