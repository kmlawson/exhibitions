British Empire Exhibition (1915 plan)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

The British Empire Exhibition planned for London in 1915 was never held. Its promoters, Lord Strathcona, the impresario Imre Kiralfy and Sir Pieter Stewart-Bam, meant it to coincide with the coming of age of the Prince of Wales (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). Organisational problems and then the First World War delayed it, and Allwood, Allan and Reid say the plans were discarded in 1915 and revived in 1919 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231; Allwood et al. 2001p. 96). The revived scheme became the Wembley exhibition of 1924-25 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 230).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, in Alexander C. T. Geppert's entry on Wembley, traces the plan to earlier proposals. The British Empire League and similar organisations first proposed an imperial exposition in 1902 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). In 1910 Lord Strathcona, Imre Kiralfy and Sir Pieter Stewart-Bam put the scheme forward again, and Stewart-Bam had chaired the General Executive of the 1907 South African Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). The project was then listed as the Imperial Exhibition and "was intended to be held in 1915" to coincide with the coming of age of the Prince of Wales (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231).

Text 50 says a privately organised Festival of Empire had been held in 1911 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 96). Text 35 describes the pavilions of that festival at Sydenham as built for a largely unsuccessful event (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231).

Empire and colony

The texts describe an imperial aim for the plan. Allwood, Allan and Reid say the revived exhibition would display the countries of the Empire and show that the British Empire was still very much a power to be reckoned with (Allwood et al. 2001p. 96). They note that all international exhibitions held in London since 1851 had included exhibits from the Empire's member countries and colonies, and that after the 1911 exhibition many people felt a nationally organised exhibition of the Empire on a full international scale was appropriate (Allwood et al. 2001p. 97).

Geppert says the revival was meant to secure the empire's stability after the war, to help postwar demobilisation and to counter wide-scale unemployment and the threatening economic decline (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). He says the vast majority of colonies and dominions had unanimously agreed to take part with their own displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). The texts do not name the colonies that were to take part in the 1915 plan, and they describe no colonised peoples or native villages for it.

Organisers and finance

After the war a provisional committee was appointed, and the Prince of Wales, later Edward VIII, launched the project at a meeting at the Mansion House under the Lord Mayor of London on 7 June 1920 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). More than a year later a second meeting, with Winston Churchill as colonial secretary, followed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). The government contributed £100,000 to the guarantee fund and gave no direct subsidy at first. A special Act of 23 December 1920 facilitated private financing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231).

Why it was not held

Geppert's entry says the 1915 plan was delayed first by organisational difficulties and then by the First World War, though similar proposals continued (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). Allwood, Allan and Reid say the plans were discarded in 1915 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 96). The two accounts differ on whether the 1915 date was abandoned or deferred, and the texts do not say who took that decision.

Aftermath and the 1924-25 exhibition

The revived plan became the British Empire Exhibition, held in 1924 and repeated a year later (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 230). Wembley was chosen in 1921 mainly for its size and accessibility by rail (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231). Geppert argues that a show planned for a pre-war setting was not altogether appropriate when it came about, after an almost decade-long time-lag and the first global war (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 235). He calls its significance ambiguous: it renewed the importance of empire but also marked the symbolic beginning of the empire's end (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 235).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial exhibitions before 1914 — the plan was one of a series of pre-war proposals, from 1902 and again in 1910, for an imperial exposition in London (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231)
  • Wartime delay — Geppert describes the 1915 date as deferred by organisational problems and the war, while Allwood, Allan and Reid describe the plans as discarded (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231; Allwood et al. 2001p. 96)
  • Postwar motives — the revived project was meant to secure imperial stability, aid demobilisation and counter unemployment and economic decline (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231)
  • Empire as display — colonies and dominions were to show their own displays, and the exhibition was to show British imperial power (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231; Allwood et al. 2001p. 96)
  • Finance and the state — the government gave a £100,000 guarantee and no direct subsidy at first (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 231)

Questions for study

  2. The government gave no direct subsidy at first and later took a more active part. How might that change have affected what the exhibition could say about empire?
  3. Geppert calls the show out of step with its pre-war origins. Which aims of the 1915 plan survived into the postwar version, according to these texts?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
8The Louisiana Purchase Exposition and Two Great Firesp. 83

Allwood sets two American fairs against the difficulties of organizing them. The St Louis Louisiana Purchase Exposition of 1904 is described as bigger than any previous exhibition, spread over 1,272 acres with a walk of thirty-five miles around it. Its white Ivory City, the Festival Hall and the Pike amusement area are compared with Chicago, and the automobile, wireless and aeronautics displays are used to argue that St Louis excelled Chicago in showing science in motion. The living ethnological displays and the Olympic Games are also covered. The second half concerns the Panama-Pacific International Exposition of 1915 in San Francisco, which the author presents as a success against earthquake, fire, war and depression. Its colour scheme by Jules Guerin, the Tower of Jewels, Bernard Maybeck's Palace of Fine Arts and the Stella attraction are described. The chapter closes with San Diego's exhibition and the Rio centenary of 1922-23, and points forward to London's Empire exhibition.

In the contents of this work

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
The Anthropology of World's FairsBurton Benedictp. 1

Benedict argues that world's fairs are modern rituals of competition, comparable to the potlatch, in which nations, companies and cities compete through ever larger displays of goods, towers and allegorical symbols. He shows how classification schemes from the 1851 Great Exhibition onward ordered the world, and how the display of colonised and Native peoples ranged from trophies and curiosities to evolutionary villages at St. Louis in 1904. He treats the amusement zone as a parody of the main fair. His examples come chiefly from London 1851, Paris 1889, Chicago 1893, St. Louis 1904, San Francisco 1915 and New York 1939, and he takes the Panama Pacific exposition as a case study. He concludes that the New York fair of 1939 marked a watershed after which world's fairs became trade shows, and that the 1915 San Francisco exposition was the last collective outburst of nineteenth-century optimism.

In the contents of this work

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
Sites of the Future: comparing and ordering new educational actualitiesMartin Lawnp. 15

Lawn argues that world exhibitions from London in 1851 onward created the standards and classifications that became 'technologies of rule' in modern education, and that these spread through museums, models and periodicals. His starting point is Michael Sadler's 1902 memorandum urging a government-organised British education exhibit at the St Louis fair of 1904. Most of the chapter examines the 1851 Exhibition's classification scheme, which applied to the British exhibits alone, and the South Kensington museums built from its proceeds and salvaged materials. Lawn then traces colonial museums in New South Wales, which bought classroom models and object-lesson cards in 1885 on the same Empire-wide classification, and the Harvard psychology laboratory exhibit at St Louis. Cassell's illustrated periodicals and the Montessori classroom at the 1915 Panama-Pacific fair show how display became pedagogy. The conclusion holds that education became a field of national competition, and that comparison produced similar teaching materials across many countries.

In the contents of this work

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
Introduction: communities real and imagined: world’s fairs and political meaningsDavid Raizman and Ethan Robeyp. 1

Raizman and Robey introduce the volume by asking how the world's fairs of 1851 to 1915 used manufactured goods to express national identity. They argue that earlier design history treated fairs as a foil to modernism, and that the fairs were networks of exchange rather than single, unified displays. Pevsner's dismissal of the Crystal Palace is challenged by later critics, from Banham and Sparke to Greenhalgh, Bennett and Mitchell, the last three reading the fairs as imperial spectacle. The editors use Anderson's 'imagined communities' to tie national identity to material culture. Their evidence is the Albert Memorial, Owen Jones's interior and Grammar of Ornament, and the contributors' cases: the 1888 Copenhagen Nordic Exhibition, the Nanjing and South American centenaries of 1910, Italy at Brussels and Antwerp, and the Finnish pavilion at Paris in 1900. They end with the outbreak of World War I, when the ideal of 'peaceful competition' broke down.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
OneForerunners of the Century-of-Progress Expositionsp. 15Asia

Rydell traces the roots of the interwar fairs to the Victorian expositions from London in 1851 to San Francisco in 1915, arguing that these fairs fused a culture of abundance with a vision of empire. Exposition authorities organised themselves like corporations, ran publicity departments and turned to trained scientists to classify exhibits, and at nearly every American fair after 1893 ethnological villages placed people along a scale from savagery to civilisation. The 1904 St Louis fair, with its Philippines Reservation, is the central case, where Filipinos were displayed as commodities of the American empire. Rydell then follows the publicity machinery at Chicago in 1893 and St Louis, and the Montana state board, whose silver statue of Justice drew the state into the fair's imperial vision before the silver was melted down. The Philadelphia Commercial Museum, founded from Chicago's exhibits to promote foreign trade, is presented as a permanent fair. The last pages cover postwar plans for fairs in Philadelphia and Chicago, and the 1920s 'fitter family' displays that carried eugenics into the New York fair.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

chapter 9, 'Between the Wars', opening pages, before the account of the 1924 British Empire Exhibition

Allwood's chapter on the inter-war fairs gives the 1915 plan only a few sentences before turning to the Wembley exhibition of 1924. It says the plans for a British Empire Exhibition were discarded in 1915 and revived in 1919, when the show was to display the countries of the Empire and demonstrate that the British Empire was still a power to be reckoned with. The authors set the idea in a longer line of Empire exhibitions: after the 1911 exhibition many people thought a full international exhibition of the Empire appropriate, and the First World War overtook these plans. The passage does not say who abandoned the 1915 plan.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.
