Glasgow International Exhibition

International Exhibition of Industry, Science and Art
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Glasgow International Exhibition ran in Kelvingrove Park from 8 May to 10 November 1888 and drew 5,748,379 visitors, the largest British exhibition held outside London up to that date (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). It was Glasgow's first international exhibition and opened a series that our sources link to later fairs in 1901, 1911 and 1938 (Filipová 2015p. 211; Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 188). Historians value it because its organisers sought to answer the city's reputation for poverty and crime, and because its displays, from Scottish industry to Indian goods, show how an exhibition presented a city and an empire (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 187).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

A group of politicians, businessmen and cultural leaders began planning the fair on 21 October 1886, under the formal title International Exhibition of Industry, Science and Art (Lawn 2009p. 184; Lawn 2009p. 184). Limond argues that the usual explanation, civic rivalry with Edinburgh's 1886 exhibition, is largely a media construct and greatly exaggerated; Glasgow's elite compared their city with London, Paris and New York rather than with Edinburgh (Lawn 2009p. 184; Lawn 2009p. 184). The more pressing concern was reputation. Glasgow was widely seen as poor, overcrowded, diseased and crime-ridden, and a successful display of industry and taste might counter that image at home and abroad (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185). Limond suggests that attracting trade was probably the main purpose, but that managing the city's reputation was also at issue, and compares Glasgow in 1888 with Dublin's exhibition of 1853 (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185).

Organisation and finance

Sir Archibald Campbell was president, Sir James King chaired the Executive Council, Henry Anthony Hedley managed the exposition and Alfred Brown was treasurer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419). The sources disagree about the profit. Findling and Pelle’s encyclopedia gives £41,700 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96); their historical dictionary gives a surplus of £41,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104); and Allwood, Allan and Reid give £47,000, for buildings only (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). The profit was used for a permanent museum. Their encyclopedia says it went towards the Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum, opened at the 1901 exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). Their historical dictionary names the Glasgow City Museum and Art Gallery (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104), and Allwood, Allan and Reid add that an Art Gallery Building Fund appeal contributed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Special railway and steamship fares encouraged visitors from across Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104).

Site and architecture

Kelvingrove Park, which Joseph Paxton had laid out in 1853, was chosen as the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). An architectural competition announced in January 1887 was won by James Sellars (Lawn 2009p. 185). Findling and Pelle’s historical dictionary says that Sellars, with James Barr, built a Moorish structure with a dome 150 feet high and four minarets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103). Their encyclopedia describes a design that combined Moorish, Byzantine and Indian influences (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). The buildings covered 11.5 of the nearly 60 acres, and the main building had 474,500 square feet of floor area and a central dome 170 feet high (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). A machinery annex at the west end gave the largest display space in Britain since 1862 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). Limond adds a 160,000 square foot annex for heavy industry, which housed 350 exhibitors (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 187). Work began on 2 May 1887, and the fair opened almost a year later (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 186). Outside, a terracotta fountain by Doulton, 85 feet high, was claimed to be the largest terracotta structure ever built (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96).

Exhibits and attractions

Exhibits were arranged in twenty-one classes, from agriculture and natural resources to engineering, chemistry, education and the fine and decorative arts (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103). The fine art display held more than 2,500 items (Lawn 2009p. 187). The women's section stood at the east end of the main building, and Greenhalgh describes the Pavilion of Women's Industries as a somewhat surprising choice (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103; Greenhalgh 1988p. 174). Entertainments included a switchback railway, a shooting gallery, a curling rink, boating on the Kelvin and gondolas brought from Venice (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103). A reconstructed bishop's palace in painted canvas served as a museum of Scottish antiquities, holding relics such as Neil Gow's fiddle and walking stick (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Lawn 2009p. 187). The Daily Programme for 16 October 1888 announced a welding exhibition, and the fair also staged a working dairy and a diamond-cutting demonstration (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104).

Nations, colonies and peoples on show

Foreign exhibitors were few, so the fair was technically international, but Scottish products, processes and culture predominated (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103). Two-thirds of exhibitors were from Scotland (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). The texts name no colonial pavilions for 1888. India is the one colony whose goods are recorded. The fair gave considerable space to Indian goods, which Limond argues, following Kinchin and Kinchin, depicted an imagined country and catered to European ideas of India, as the Great Exhibition of 1851 had done (Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187). Two groups of working people appear: dairymaids from Switzerland, Holland, Ireland and Scotland who worked in the dairy, and Indian artisans shown at work (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104).

Visitors and reception

Visitors numbered 5,748,379, and many came from other parts of Britain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104). Queen Victoria made a state visit on 22 August, her second to the city, and John Lavery painted the visit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). Before the opening, The Times praised the site and the Moorish main building (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186). Limond says the fair was generally considered a success, with favourable publicity and a boost to trade (Lawn 2009p. 187). Limond sees its educational aim as autodidactic, meaning self-taught: visitors were expected to improve themselves through their own diligent effort, in the spirit of Samuel Smiles (Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187).

Legacies

The profit paid for a permanent museum, the Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum, which opened in time for the 1901 exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Allwood et al. 2001p. 81). Limond calls this museum an icon of civic pride, paid for in part from the proceeds of the 1888 event (Lawn 2009p. 187). Neale describes the Doulton fountain as a legacy of the 1888 exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 257). The 1901 fair was the second Glasgow International Exhibition (Filipová 2015p. 211).

Photographs and the city's image

Limond argues that Glasgow's bad name rested on "the perception as much as the reality that shaped the image of a corrosive, crime-ridden community" (Lawn 2009p. 185). One thing that fixed the image was a set of photographs of the worst slums, taken by Thomas Annan between 1868 and 1871 and ironically already earmarked for clearance; Limond says they did much to fix the popular impression (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185). A second source was local. Because the city had a strong evangelical Protestant tradition, there was a tendency to talk up social problems as a prelude to calling for a return to godly ways (Lawn 2009p. 185). Limond says that Glasgow's first international exhibition therefore had a great deal riding on its outcome (Lawn 2009p. 185; Lawn 2009p. 185).

Inside the Bishop's Castle

The Times described the replica as standing on the north bank of the Kelvin and recalled that the original had stood, until a hundred years before, on the site of the Royal Infirmary (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186). Limond judges its contents to be "more an eclectic storehouse of bric-a-brac than a museum" (Lawn 2009p. 187). They included the corporate seal of Dundee, dating to 1555; the university maces of St Andrews and Glasgow; original volumes of the Waverley novels; a contemporary copy of the proclamation that authorised the Glencoe massacre of 1692; and relics of Mary Stewart (Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187). Limond's list comes from Hunter's 1990 study of the exhibition (Lawn 2009p. 187).

Press and publicity

The Times, which praised the site in the passage quoted above, also contrasted the park with the unsavoury precincts of the Broomielaw and the Cowcaddens, and wrote with only a hint of metropolitan condescension (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186). Its account said the fairy fountain would throw up about twenty jets of water, lit in colours after dark (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186). The North British Daily Mail issued a run of Exhibition Extra supplements, numbers 1 to 3 dated 19 April, 26 April and 3 May 1888, and number 12 dated 14 November 1888 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104). The Builder printed a series of articles on the building and its design, some in 1887 and several in the months of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104). The Illustrated London News, The Pictorial World and The Graphic are useful for images of the exhibition, and T. Raffles Davison's Pen and Ink Notes at the Glasgow Exhibition has further pictures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96).

Heavy industry in the annex

Limond says that technical considerations made it hard to show the ships' engines and locomotives, on whose building the city's economy depended, and that a 160,000 square foot annex was devoted to heavy industry (Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186; Lawn 2009p. 186). Some of the pieces were so heavy that a crane able to lift up to 20 tons was needed to put them in place (Lawn 2009p. 187).

The royal visit and the gifts

Alongside Lavery's painting of the visit, the Glasgow Museum and Art Galleries holds many of his 150-odd portrait studies of people in the audience (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104). Among the additional attractions was a display of the Queen's Jubilee presents, 799 in number (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 103). The encyclopedia says the presents were also put on display in the nearby Kelvingrove Mansion Museum (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96).

Refreshments and the School of Cookery

Many dining rooms and tea rooms served refreshments, and the Glasgow School of Cookery ran a tearoom that made enough profit to cover the school's running costs for a number of years (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96).

How historians have interpreted it

The authors differ on the fair's purpose. Limond argues that it belonged to a laissez-faire phase (liberal non-intervention), in which organisers trusted visitors to reach their own conclusions, unlike the more hectoring events of 1938 and 1951 (Lawn 2009p. 196; Lawn 2009p. 196). On this reading, the fair was about reputation and self-improvement, not about rivalry with Edinburgh (Lawn 2009p. 184). Wilson Smith suggests that the alternating Edinburgh and Glasgow series might express a continuing rivalry, and that Glasgow won it convincingly (Filipová 2015p. 211; Filipová 2015p. 211). Filipová places the Glasgow fair among provincial shows that used displays to build a local identity (Filipová 2015p. 137). Greenhalgh sees the women's pavilion of 1888 as an idiosyncratic step before the women's buildings of Chicago in 1893 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 174). Hoffenberg looks at the Indian displays through the catalogues of Mukharji, arguing that they shaped the accepted knowledge of Indian art (Hoffenberg 2001p. 55). Roche places the fair in a British imperial version of the exposition genre (Roche 2000p. 42). The reference works agree on the basic facts, but not on the profit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104).

The texts say little about Asian displays at the Glasgow fair. None records East Asian or Southeast Asian displays, visitors or commissioners at the 1888 fair, and the Indian presence is the only one they describe.

India

Trailokya Nath Mukharji, a South Asian expert, wrote the official catalogues that accompanied India's displays at international exhibitions, including Amsterdam in 1883 and Glasgow in 1888 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53). These catalogues described artware and commercial products and gave their local names, places of origin, uses and prices (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53). Hoffenberg records Mukharji's Art-Manufactures of India, which was specially compiled for the Glasgow exhibition and printed in Calcutta in 1888 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 307). Cornish cites the same book in a footnote and describes Mukharji as one of several Indian experts employed by the government department, and unusual in travelling overseas as an official representative of the Indian government (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 128; Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 128). Who paid for these displays the texts do not say beyond this official framing.

Limond gives the Indian goods a different reading. The fair reserved considerable space for goods from India, and Limond argues that the display depicted an imagined country for the European consumer, as the 1851 Great Exhibition had done (Lawn 2009p. 187; Lawn 2009p. 187). Limond draws on Kinchin and Kinchin for this point (Lawn 2009p. 187).

Hoffenberg is more interested in what happened to Mukharji's work afterwards. His commentary and catalogues became part of the accepted canon on traditional Indian art, and the analyses prepared for Glasgow and Amsterdam became common reference points for later European and Anglo-Indian art experts (Hoffenberg 2001p. 55; Hoffenberg 2001p. 55). Hoffenberg's chapter conclusion asks whether this was imperial knowledge or local power (Hoffenberg 2001p. 55). Cornish argues that Mukharji's knowledge was as much imperial and global as local and national (Finnegan & Wright 2015p. 129).

Indian tea also appeared at Glasgow. The Indian tea association, which promoted Indian machine-processed tea against hand-rolled Chinese leaves, displayed and sold tea at Glasgow and other exhibitions in the following decade (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116; Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).

Indian artisans were listed among the attractions in the Daily Programme of 16 October 1888, but Findling and Pelle’s historical dictionary does not say who sent them (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 104). Findling and Pelle’s encyclopedia lists an Indian-themed amusement among the entertainments and says nothing about its makers (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 96). The texts record no Japanese, Chinese or Southeast Asian presence at the fair.

Indian staff and their catalogues

Hoffenberg says that the Revenue and Agriculture Department employed South Asians to collect, organise and publicise material for official exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 52; Hoffenberg 2001p. 52). Mukharji, the most senior of them, rose only to the rank of class II assistant or clerk, although he wrote the catalogues for the India displays at Amsterdam in 1883 and Glasgow in 1888 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 53; Hoffenberg 2001p. 53). Writing about Amsterdam, Mukharji gave the object of his catalogue as interesting European visitors in the old Indian arts, in Hoffenberg's quotation (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54; Hoffenberg 2001p. 54). For the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886, Mukharji arranged the art manufactures by region of origin rather than by class (Hoffenberg 2001p. 54; Hoffenberg 2001p. 54). The texts do not say whether the Glasgow catalogue used the same arrangement.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Civic reputation — The texts agree that the fair was meant to change how Glasgow was seen, but differ on how far rivalry with Edinburgh drove it. (Lawn 2009; Filipová 2015)
  • Education and self-culture — Limond treats the fair as a site of self-improvement, where visitors were left to reach their own conclusions. (Lawn 2009)
  • Local identity and regional exhibitions — Filipová places the fair among provincial shows that used displays to construct local identity. (Filipová 2015)
  • Indian goods and knowledge — Limond reads the Indian goods as an imagined country, while Hoffenberg traces how Mukharji's catalogues became accepted knowledge. (Lawn 2009; Hoffenberg 2001; Finnegan & Wright 2015)
  • Finance and museum legacy — Profits built a permanent museum, but the reference works give three different profit figures. (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • Women's displays — Greenhalgh treats the 1888 women's pavilion as an idiosyncratic precursor of the Chicago women's buildings. (Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Imperial exposition genre — Roche places the Glasgow fair in the British imperial strand of the exposition genre. (Roche 2000)

Questions for study

  1. If the fair aimed at self-culture, as Limond argues, how could historians find out what visitors actually learned?
  2. How far should the rivalry with Edinburgh be treated as central, given Limond's dismissal of it and Wilson Smith's suggestion that it may explain the alternating series?
  3. Did the displays of Indian goods present India as the organisers saw it, or as the visitors wanted it to be seen? Compare the readings of Limond and Hoffenberg.
  4. Why do the reference works give different profit figures for the same fair, and what does that imply for using such works in research?
  5. Was the women's pavilion of 1888 a real step for women at exhibitions, or a curiosity, as Greenhalgh's wording suggests?
  6. How does the use of a fair's profit for a permanent museum shape the way a city later remembers the event?
  7. If the fair's displays were meant to answer both outside photographs and local moral campaigns, which of the 1888 exhibits would have spoken to each audience, and what evidence would show a visitor reading them that way?
  8. The Bishop's Castle relics were chosen to tell a Scottish story; who chose them, and how far does the 1990 study Limond relies on show that the choice was deliberate?
  9. If the school's tearoom paid for the school's running costs, what does that suggest about whether the fair's catering was planned as a source of income or as a service to visitors?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Lawn, Martin, ed. Modelling the Future: Exhibitions and the Materiality of Education. Oxford: Symposium Books, 2009.
More of a school than a show? Events in Glasgow, 1888-1988David Limondp. 183

Limond traces how the educational aims of Glasgow's exhibitions changed over a century, using the 1888 International Exhibition, the 1938 Empire Exhibition, the 1951 Exhibition of Industrial Power and the 1988 Garden Festival. The argument is that the nineteenth-century event relied on self-improvement and left visitors to draw their own conclusions, that the 1938 and 1951 events were openly didactic and modernist, and that by 1988 the organisers had abandoned educational purpose, offering school parties a 'day out'. Evidence comes from contemporary press reports, guide-catalogues and an interview with the 1988 principal architect. The 1888 event is read as a response to Glasgow's reputation for poverty and violence, the 1938 event as an imperial showcase whose schoolchildren the city paid to attend, and the 1951 show as a story of uninterrupted technological progress. The chapter ends by arguing that the earlier aspirations were sincere, if paternalistic, and that their loss reflects a wider retreat from exhibitions as teaching.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

David Limond's chapter “More of a school than a show?”, section “Glasgow, 1888”, pp. 184-187

David Limond's chapter treats the 1888 Glasgow International Exhibition as the first of a century of Glasgow events, and argues that its educational aim was “autodidactic”: visitors were expected to improve themselves rather than be taught. Limond sets the fair against Glasgow's poor reputation, not against Edinburgh, and rejects the common claim that civic rivalry with Edinburgh prompted it. Limond reads the Indian goods as an imagined country made for European consumers and the relics in the Bishop’s palace as an eclectic storehouse of bric-a-brac. The fair is then contrasted with the later Glasgow events, which Limond argues were more explicitly educational.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Wikipedia

The International Exhibition of Science, Art and Industry was the first of 4 international exhibitions held in Glasgow, Scotland, during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It took place at Kelvingrove Park between May and November 1888. The main aim of the exhibition was to draw international attention to the city's achievements in applied sciences, industry and the arts during the Industrial Revolution. However, it was also hoped the Exhibition would raise enough money for a museum, art gallery and school of art in the city. The exhibition was opened by the Prince of Wales, as honorary president of the exhibition, on 8 May 1888. It was the greatest exhibition held outside London and the largest ever in Scotland during the 19th century.
