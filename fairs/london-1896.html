Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition

The Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition was held at Earl's Court in London in 1896, in the series of shows that Imre Kiralfy staged there (Geppert 2010p. 251; Geppert 2010p. 105). Qureshi describes it as a second run of Kiralfy's 1895 Empire of India Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Geppert counts it among Earl's Court shows of single colonies, and the texts record South Asian performers and craftsmen in it (Geppert 2010p. 105; Qureshi 2011p. 250). None of the passages gives opening or closing dates (Geppert 2010p. 251; Qureshi 2011p. 250).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Organisers and names

Geppert says that Kiralfy's company, The London Exhibitions Ltd, took over Earl's Court in 1894 and staged nine expositions there over the next nine years (Geppert 2010p. 105). Greenhalgh dates the company's formation to 1895 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). Qureshi and Geppert call the 1896 show the Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition (Qureshi 2011p. 250; Geppert 2010p. 228). Greenhalgh names a show that Kiralfy organised in 1896 'India, Ceylon, Borneo and Burma Show', without a venue (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

Empire and colony

Qureshi says that the 1895 show, held again in 1896, was the first devoted solely to India and was set up by a showman, albeit in collaboration with the government (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Geppert places the 1896 show among Earl's Court exhibitions of "typical products of single colonies" (Geppert 2010p. 105). Most of that series, he writes, had no official participants from abroad (Geppert 2010p. 105). Greenhalgh says that under Kiralfy empire became a constant subject for events in Britain (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91). No passage says which colonial authorities supplied the goods (Qureshi 2011p. 250).

Geppert describes the 1896 show as a smaller fair with three vertical structures: a captive balloon rising to 300 metres, a Great Wheel and the Belvedere Tower, which he calls a "diminutive Eiffel tower" of 60 metres (Geppert 2010p. 228). He dates the opening of Kiralfy's Great Wheel to July 1895, at the Empire of India Exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 229; Geppert 2010p. 230). The texts therefore do not clearly show which edition had the wheel.

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

South Asian performers and craftsmen

Qureshi says that Kiralfy custom-built a site, imported South Asians to work as performers and craftsmen, and recreated a Bombay street, Lahore storefronts and a Benares temple (Qureshi 2011p. 250). Geppert writes that Indian, Ceylonese and Burmese artisans "participated in Kiralfy’s 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition" (Geppert 2010p. 122). He says little is known of the Indian Arena performers of the Franco-British show, so he draws on this group, presumed to have worked under similar conditions, and on a contract (Geppert 2010p. 122). The contract required the company to convey the workers to England and back, board them and pay monthly wages, and required a Rs. 5000 payment from any worker who left England before the close of the exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 122).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Kiralfy's Earl's Court series — the 1896 show is one of a run of shows that Kiralfy's company staged on one site, and Greenhalgh and Geppert both present Kiralfy as its organiser (Greenhalgh 1988p. 91; Geppert 2010p. 105).
  • Single-colony displays — Geppert lists the 1896 show among typical products of single colonies (Geppert 2010p. 105).
  • South Asian performers and labour — the performers and craftsmen, and the contract Geppert uses to infer their conditions (Qureshi 2011p. 250; Geppert 2010p. 122).
  • Vertical structures — the balloon, Great Wheel and Belvedere Tower, which Geppert counts among the features of all the expositions he studies (Geppert 2010p. 228).
  • Editions and names — Qureshi's re-run of the 1895 show and Greenhalgh's different title for a 1896 show (Qureshi 2011p. 250; Greenhalgh 1988p. 91).

Questions for study

  1. Did the Great Wheel belong to the 1895 show, the 1896 show, or both, and what evidence would settle it?
  2. Is Greenhalgh's 'India, Ceylon, Borneo and Burma Show' the same event as the Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition, a different show, or another name for it?
  3. How far can the working conditions set out in one performers' contract stand for those of performers in another show?
  4. What did the government gain from working with a private showman on an exhibition of this kind?

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
1Introduction: How to Read an Expositionp. 1

Geppert opens with a 1896 review by Georg Simmel of the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung, which he uses as a model for reading an exposition as a medium rather than a mirror of national identity. The book studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931, among them the Exposition Universelle of 1900, the Franco-British Exhibition of 1908 and the British Empire Exhibition of 1924-25. Geppert argues that these events formed overlapping transnational networks, since organizers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and 'old town' sections from one another, and that the 'identity' framework of earlier work is too simple. He sets out a method of space, time and personae, drawing on Lefebvre and Chartier, and surveys the rise of exposition studies since the 1980s. The introduction admits that its choice of cases is partial, and it closes by promising five virtual visits that compare London, Paris and the would-be world city of Berlin.

In the contents of this work

2Berlin 1896: Wilhelm II, Georg Simmel and the Berliner Gewerbeausstellungp. 16

Geppert asks why Germany never hosted an international exposition like those in London, Paris or Vienna, and what the Berliner Gewerbeausstellung of 1896 was in their place. He traces three failed phases of the 'exhibition question' (1878-82, 1891-92, 1907-10), in which businessmen's associations and government debated and dropped plans, and attributes the final failure largely to Wilhelm II's personal opposition, with cost and site problems behind it. The chapter then turns to the privately financed fair: its layout, the colonial section with its 'native villages', the Egyptian quarter Kairo and Alt-Berlin. Reading Georg Simmel's 1896 review beside the Kaiser's letters and the press, Geppert argues that the fair tested Berlin's claim to be a world city and found it wanting next to Paris. It concludes that the fair left few urban traces but was publicised as a rehearsal for a later world exhibition.

Brief discussion 3

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
ch. 4 'London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition', pp. 105, 122-23; ch. 7 'Conclusion', pp. 228, 230; appendix p. 251; bibliography p. 340

Geppert mentions the 1896 show in four places, each briefly. In chapter 4 it is one of the Earl's Court shows listed as typical products of single colonies, and a passage uses its Indian, Ceylonese and Burmese artisans as evidence for the working conditions of performers in another show. In the conclusion it is a 'smaller fair' with a captive balloon, a Great Wheel and the Belvedere Tower. The appendix lists it, and the bibliography cites its official programme. Geppert's argument here is that all the expositions he studies had vertical structures, and the 1896 show is an example of this.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
ch. 4 'Human showcases', p. 91 (passage on Imre Kiralfy's career); index p. 91

Greenhalgh's chapter on displays of people at international exhibitions mentions the 1896 show only in a biographical passage on Imre Kiralfy. He places Kiralfy's 1895 Empire of India show at Earl's Court within a shift from naval spectacles to imperial subjects, and lists a 1896 show under the title 'India, Ceylon, Borneo and Burma Show', with no venue. For Greenhalgh, Kiralfy's rise meant that empire became a constant subject for British events. The passage read does not describe the exhibits or peoples of the 1896 show.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Qureshi, Sadiah. Peoples on Parade: Exhibitions, Empire, and Anthropology in Nineteenth-Century Britain. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2011.
ch. 7 'The End of an Affair' (PDF p. 259), one paragraph on Kiralfy's shows before Savage South Africa; printed page not seen

Qureshi discusses the 1895 Empire of India Exhibition, and its 1896 run as the Empire of India and Ceylon Exhibition, in one paragraph of chapter 7. He calls it one of Kiralfy's most pertinent shows: the first devoted solely to India, set up by a showman in collaboration with the government, with a custom-built site and imported South Asian performers and craftsmen. The paragraph describes the 1895 show in detail and gives the 1896 run only as a re-staging. For Qureshi, under Kiralfy's guidance fair exhibitions became more lavish with commercial success through visual impact as their primary goal, and this shifting context is where he places Frank Fillis's Savage South Africa.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Qureshi's book studies the display of living foreign peoples in nineteenth-century London, from the San exhibition at Exeter Hall in 1847 to Savage South Africa at Earl's Court in 1899. Its argument is that these shows were a large commercial entertainment, shaped by London's streets, printed promotion, managers and performers who consented on their own terms, and that they fed into ethnology and the scientific study of race rather than being only racist spectacle. Part One covers the streets, Part Two the promotion, the managers and the recruitment of performers, and Part Three the audiences' interpretations, the natural history court at the Crystal Palace and the learned societies, ending with the shift toward entertainment and imperial spectacle. The scope is London, with comparisons to Paris in 1889, Chicago in 1893 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are present in this book as one group among many displayed peoples and as a frequent comparison, but not as a central subject. The text names them in about 74 of its 17,790 lines, mostly in Chapters Six and Seven and the notes. Chinese people appear in Chapter One as physiognomic types in Eliza Lynn's 'Passing Faces' and as settlers stranded in the London docks, and Japanese and Chinese shows are named in the introduction. Chapter Six gives the most sustained attention: Latham's natural history court at the Crystal Palace displayed Chinese, Tibetans, Malaysians and Javanese, and the guidebook's classification of the Chinese as the most civilized of the Mongolians is discussed. Chapter Seven adds Asian displays at other fairs, used as comparisons with London: the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition's 'Malay' and Straits Settlements performers and Chinese junks from Hong Kong, the Cochin-China settlement and Kampong-Javanese camp of Paris 1889, and the Chicago 1893 Midway with its Chinese and Javanese villages and Japanese bazaar. The book says little about the Asian performers themselves or their experiences in London, and South Asia, through the Empire of India exhibition of 1895, receives more attention as a contrast.
