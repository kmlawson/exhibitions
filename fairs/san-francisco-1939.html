Golden Gate International Exposition

The Golden Gate International Exposition opened on Treasure Island, an artificial island in San Francisco Bay, on 18 February 1939 and closed on 29 September 1940, after a six-month break (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). It was a Depression-era project meant to celebrate the new bridges and to create jobs (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A reference entry calls it "the last of the old-style world’s fairs" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). Its Pacific Basin theme and its colonial and Asian pavilions make it a useful case of an exposition presenting the United States as a power across the Pacific (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the new bridges. Civic leaders wanted to celebrate the Golden Gate and San Francisco-Oakland Bay bridges, and a fair promised jobs during the Depression as a quasi-public works project (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). San Francisco had already held fairs in 1894 and 1915, and many of the civic leaders behind the 1939 fair had also organised the Panama-Pacific International Exposition of 1915 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 85). Leland Cutler, a Republican and head of the San Francisco Chamber of Commerce, and George Creel, a Democrat who had run wartime propaganda, brought in exposition specialists to plan it (Rydell 1993p. 85). Rydell argues that the planners framed the fair around an empire of the West (Rydell 1993p. 85).

The fair also competed with New York. Supporters of New York in the federal government tried to force San Francisco to postpone. Roosevelt kept federal support for the West Coast fair, and Mayor La Guardia came to dine there (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). Roosevelt opened the fair in person (Rydell 1993p. 147).

Site and architecture

The fair stood on an island of about 400 acres in the shoals off Yerba Buena Island, reached by the Bay Bridge and by ferries. The island took about a year and a half to build in the early 1930s, using 287,000 tons of rock and 20 million cubic feet of sand, at a cost of $3.72 million in federal Works Projects Administration (WPA) funds (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Two elephant towers, modelled on Angkor Wat, flanked the entrance, and the 400-foot Tower of the Sun, an Art Deco tower capped with a golden phoenix, rose in the Court of Honor (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). Strong Pacific winds shaped the layout, and the pavilions formed a walled perimeter like a medieval city (Allwood et al. 2001p. 120).

Gray Brechin, in a chapter of Benedict’s collection, argues that the architects looked back. Many had worked on the 1915 fair, and they built a stripped, Moderne version of it in the middle of the Bay (Benedict 1983p. 111). Brechin calls the fair a nostalgic nod to the past, out of step with the modernism that the Chicago fair of 1933 had pointed towards (Benedict 1983p. 111). Brechin’s verdict is blunt: "Treasure Island was a poor compromise, for the Panama Pacific International Exposition was in all respects the greater exposition" (Benedict 1983p. 112).

Exhibits and attractions

Many visitors got their first look at television. The fair also showed a translucent General Motors Pontiac and a Westinghouse robot called Willie Vocalite (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). The 1939 art programme was a $40 million exhibit of Old Masters in the Palace of Fine and Decorative Arts. The paintings went back to Europe, and in 1940 Art in Action took the space (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). Greenhalgh calls the Old Masters show the fullest gathering of its kind among fairs, and sets its gallery against the futuristic structures and entertainment of Treasure Island, the fair’s nickname (Greenhalgh 1988p. 217; Greenhalgh 1988p. 217).

The Gayway midway held Sally Rand’s Nude Ranch, a roller coaster and babies in incubators (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Billy Rose’s Aquacade, brought from New York in 1940 with Johnny Weissmuller, Esther Williams and Gertrude Ederle, made money for Rose (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). The Cavalcade of the Golden West ran with 200 actors and 200 animals in one account and with 300 actors in another (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 308). Some commercial exhibitors copied New York attractions, including the Nude Ranch and the Aquacade (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122).

Greenhalgh says the organisers sought the best works rather than the most, and Walter Heil, director of the European art division, boasted of masterworks the American public had not seen (Greenhalgh 1988p. 217). Loans came from Italy, Holland, France and Britain as well as from American collections (Greenhalgh 1988p. 217). The list ran from Fra Angelico and Botticelli to Rembrandt, Van Gogh and Velasquez (Greenhalgh 1988p. 217).

Ford had its own building in the Court of the Pacific area, while Chrysler and General Motors exhibited in Vacationland (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Schrenk notes a model service station and, in 1940, a quarter-size replica of the Ford wind tunnel (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Visitors could see a model of San Francisco as it was expected to look in 1999 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Pan-American China Clipper took off from Yerba Buena Cove beside the Hall of Air Transportation, bound for the Orient (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302).

Nations and peoples on show

The competition with New York kept many foreign nations away. Apart from France, Italy and Norway, the exhibitors were from Pacific-bordering nations or from Latin America (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122). Allwood, Allan and Reid add that gathering war clouds curtailed international support, so the exhibits of US industry provided most of the interest (Allwood et al. 2001p. 50). The planners grouped the regions under American military and economic control into a Latin American Court and a Pacific Basin area, and placed the rest in a zone of foreign pavilions (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78). Greenhalgh argues that the grouping implied the Latin American and Pacific areas were not foreign to the United States (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78). The Latin American Court presented its nations as producers of raw materials and consumers of American industrial surplus (Rydell 1993p. 90). The Pacific Basin area included the Hawaiian and Philippine pavilions from America’s Pacific empire (Rydell 1993p. 87).

The Pacific House held murals by Miguel Covarrubias and a colour-coded map of the Pacific’s peoples, which Rydell says lent anthropological cover to hierarchical ideas of race (Rydell 1993p. 89). At Creel’s urging, nearly forty Native Americans were shown in the government building, a display that Rydell likens to European colonial exhibits (Rydell 1993p. 89). The Women’s Board was headed by Hazel Pedlar Faulkner, and the texts record that women and African Americans had no prominent place at the fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh writes that the Board’s role was "a macrocosm of the housewife", taking on tasks that matched women’s work in the home (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). Rydell also describes the fair’s all-white employment policy as part of a wider pattern of exclusion at expositions (Rydell 1993p. 183).

Visitors, politics and aftermath

The visitor figures differ between texts. Findling and Pelle’s Historical Dictionary (1990) and Allwood, Allan and Reid’s survey both give a gross attendance of 17,041,999 for the two seasons and a debt of $559,423 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Findling and Pelle’s Encyclopedia (2008) gives 17,410,999 visitors and debts of $539,423 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). Opening day drew 138,000 visitors (Gelernter 1995p. 349). Maloney notes that attendance was under half that of New York (Maloney 2012p. 173).

Roche’s endnote mentions black trade-union pickets at the opening ceremony of the 1939 expo, but the same sentence names New York, so the texts do not settle which fair is meant (Roche 2000p. 240).

Plans to make the island an international airport were dropped when the U.S. Navy took over the island at the end of the second year, as war became imminent (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). The island became a naval aviation station (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The buildings were quickly torn down, and the statue Pacifica was pulled over and broken (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). Allwood, Allan and Reid note that many fairs, including this one, showed an operating profit that construction and demolition costs then wiped out (Allwood et al. 2001p. 132).

Work, money and labour on the island

The island was built as a jobs scheme. Engineers lowered the salt water table and leached the black sandy soil for four months, then spread 100,000 cubic yards of loam from the Sacramento Valley and installed a sprinkler system (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Twelve hundred workers were hired to plant millions of flowers grown in Golden Gate Park, and a further 435,000 bulbs went in to a fixed colour plan (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell adds that public works money was used to prepare the site (Rydell 1993p. 146). Larson sets one-fourth of the island aside for parking, which he says was unnecessary in 1915 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301).

Leland Cutler was exposition president in 1939 and Marshall Dill in 1940, and George Creel served as United States commissioner for both years (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). H. C. Bottoroff, the general manager, wrote the official history, which gives statistics from daily attendance figures to gardening costs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). Maloney puts the site at 400 acres, one-third the size of New York's (Maloney 2012p. 174). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the New York site as 1,216 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 117).

Colour and light

Jesse E. Stanton, the director of colour, built his scheme on nineteen colours drawn from the natural hues of the Pacific coast, and barred every other colour except those a corporate sponsor required (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The texts spell his name three ways: Jesse in Fair America, Jess in Allwood, and Jessie in Brechin (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Allwood et al. 2001p. 120; Benedict 1983p. 111). Allwood says his palette joined the soft tones of Guerin's 1915 scheme to the strident colours of Urban's Chicago fair of 1933 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 120). Brechin says Stanton took Guerin's role as master colourist (Benedict 1983p. 111).

Lighting ran on a larger scale. A team of illumination engineers used 10,000 coloured floodlights, 130 searchlights and 300 ultraviolet lamps, and murals painted with luminescent paint glowed in the dark (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Allwood places twenty-four searchlights on the north side of Yerba Buena Island as a scintillator (Allwood et al. 2001p. 121). Rydell calls the twin Elephant Towers "Mayan-cum-Cambodian" and says they seemed to derive from Oriental precedent and early skyscraper zoning studies (Rydell 1993p. 86). Fair America, by contrast, names Angkor Wat as the model (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), so the texts differ on the source of the towers.

The Pacific House and the Court of Pacifica

Larson describes a Court of the Seven Seas, about 1,000 feet long and lined with galley prows, which ended in the Court of Pacifica. There Ralph Stackpole's 80-foot statue of peace stood before an oriental prayer curtain lit in revolving colours at night (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). The Fountain of the Western Waters carried figures for the cultures of the Pacific Basin, and the names of the great discoverers lined the walls (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). Allwood describes two vistas across the site, one from the Esplanade to Pacifica and one from the Pacific entrance across the Lake of All Nations to the American Federal Building (Allwood et al. 2001p. 121). The Federal Building had forty-eight columns, one for each state (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). Pacific House, which Larson calls the theme building, held an oceanic relief map with spouting dolphins and six Covarrubias murals (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302).

Visitors and the tourist economy

Access was controlled. The organisers banned travel to the site on foot, by ordinary bus or by train. Visitors paid 50 cents to cross the bridge and 50 cents to enter, or left their cars downtown or in Oakland and took public ferries (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122). Fair America gives the same choice of ferry or car, with ample parking (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Rydell presents tourism as the fair's economic case. Creel told the Commonwealth Club in San Francisco that vacationing tourists left $250 million a year in the West, and that the sum could be doubled or tripled (Rydell 1993p. 130). The Vacationland Palace, with its motto about bringing all the outdoors under one roof, housed a Southern Pacific model railroad through western dioramas and a Union Pacific display for Sun Valley, Idaho (Rydell 1993p. 129). In the Hall of Western States, beside the Pacific Basin pavilions, eleven states tied recreation to extractive industries (Rydell 1993p. 129). Mayor Angelo Rossi hoped citizen-tourists would find "manana-land, spiced with American efficiency" (Rydell 1993p. 130). Rydell says the promoters aimed at seventeen million sightseers and directed them towards consumption, leisure and empire (Rydell 1993p. 130).

The Women's Board

Greenhalgh places the San Francisco board in a longer pattern. Women's sections at Atlanta in 1895, Buffalo in 1901, St Louis in 1904 and San Francisco in 1915 were taken over by anti-suffrage forces (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). The trend continued in the 1930s, and the Golden Gate board was made up mainly of officials from the Girl Scouts of America and the Women's Almanac (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). The Official Guide said the board advised the exposition's managers and the California Commission, and had directed the beautification of San Francisco, its suburbs and the state (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). The board's head had the task of interesting women in the fair and arranging recognition for distinguished women visitors (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell treats it as the West Coast culmination of what Rydell calls coloniale moderne, colonial display in a modern style (Rydell 1993p. 84). Rydell argues that the planners recast imperial display as a Pacific Rim empire of consumption, leisure and corporate modernism (Rydell 1993p. 87). Rydell ties the fair to New Deal public works and to the wish to restore Americans’ faith in their economic and political system (Rydell 1993p. 85). With Findling and Pelle in Fair America (2000), Rydell argues that the fair "breathed fresh life into dreams of an American empire in the Pacific Basin" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The same authors argue that the 1930s fairs diverted the public from the Depression (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Greenhalgh, in Ephemeral Vistas (1988), reads the fair’s imperial groupings and its Women’s Board as forms of control and domestic role (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78; Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). Schrenk, in Meet Me at the Fair (2014), reads the fair as a political exposition promoting Pacific unity, unlike the corporate fair in New York (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Samuel, in The End of the Innocence (2007), places it among the 1930s fairs that shored up faith in a consumer society, but holds that the New York fair celebrated consumerism most enthusiastically (Samuel 2007).

The readings agree that the Pacific theme sat at the centre of the fair. They differ on what it meant. Brechin reads the fair as backward-looking, while Rydell and Schrenk read it as a forward-looking imperial project (Benedict 1983p. 112; Rydell 1993p. 85; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Larson’s entry in the 2008 encyclopedia presents the fair as the culmination of American fair design, with a Pacific Basin theme that gave the site a cohesive plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 306). Roche notes that the organisers of inter-war expositions were less inhibited about colonialism, and that the San Francisco fairs of 1915 and 1939 were used to attract tourists and migrants (Roche 2000p. 59; Roche 2000p. 67). Roche’s table lists the fair as profit-making (Roche 2000p. 43), against the debts recorded by Findling and Pelle and by Allwood, Allan and Reid (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Brown asks whether the propaganda of the fair gardens delayed American responses to Japanese expansion. He suggests that Roosevelt's reluctance to embargo Japan until the summer of 1941 was affected by Japan's large-scale involvement in the New York and San Francisco fairs (Brown). He calls this "not far-fetched to imagine" but does not prove it (Brown).

Rydell says Roosevelt made long journeys to San Francisco to lend his personal endorsement (Rydell 1993p. 147). At the opening-day throng he said he was "quite open and unashamed in my liking for expositions" (Rydell 1993p. 148). Rydell argues that the fairs served as a primary vehicle for giving the New Deal programmes an appearance of coherence (Rydell 1993p. 147).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts show an Asian presence at this fair in three forms: a Japanese theatre company, the Japanese pavilion, and the colonial and national pavilions of the Pacific Basin. The texts say nothing about Chinese, Korean or Indian displays at this fair.

Japan

The Takarazuka Revue, an all-female Japanese theatre company, performed at the Golden Gate fair before its New York engagement (Peterson 2020p. 130). Peterson shows that the Revue’s tours were a partnership between the Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the Kokusai Bunka Shinkōkai (Society for International Cultural Relations, KBS) and the company itself (Peterson 2020p. 131). Peterson places the Revue within a government strategy to have the West accept Japan as a leader in Asia (Peterson 2020p. 131).

The Japanese design collective Nippon Kōbō was responsible for the interior of the Japanese pavilion at San Francisco (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96). Its displays used large photomurals made from photographs that had already appeared in Japanese promotional magazines (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96). Takashi Kōno, who had worked with the collective from about 1934 to 1939, helped design photographic murals for the Japanese pavilions at San Francisco (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Natori Yōnosuke, a co-founder of the collective, was associated with KBS, a semi-governmental body set up in 1934 to promote cultural understanding, and his activities were backed by the Japanese government (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96).

In November 1938 the Japan Times and Mail reported that similar folk-craft exhibits would be sent to the San Francisco fair in 1939 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 98). The texts do not say whether they were shown. Kargon and co-authors treat such craft displays as part of Japan’s effort to publicise its modernity, and note that Japanese craft traditions were becoming the focus of world attention (Kargon et al. 2015p. 98).

The Pacific Basin pavilions

The Pacific Basin was the fair’s name for the countries around the ocean. Keatinge, a British official who had run imperial pageantry at Wembley, and Cutler, who travelled in Asia to secure exhibits, persuaded Pacific Rim countries and European colonial powers that pavilions in the Basin would serve their own interests (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell 1993p. 87). On opening day the Pacific Basin displays included buildings from Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Johore, Java, the Netherlands East Indies and French Indochina (Rydell 1993p. 87). The texts do not say who paid for the national pavilions.

The French consul general in San Francisco reported to his foreign minister that the Indochina building, a copy of the structure at the 1931 Paris colonial fair, would help hold the Pacific against Japanese advances (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell 1993p. 88). Rydell reads this as imperial display, in which the fair’s colonial pavilions were to show the Pacific as an American-led zone (Rydell 1993p. 87). Covarrubias’s colour-coded map gave Asian populations yellow and green, and Rydell argues that it lent anthropological cover to hierarchical ideas of race (Rydell 1993p. 89; Rydell 1993p. 89).

Asian motifs also appeared as decoration. Rydell describes a mixture of Asian-inspired styles around the Tower of the Sun (Rydell 1993p. 86). Brechin notes Cambodian and Malaysian motifs in the architecture (Benedict 1983p. 111). Thai lanterns lit the walkways of the Lake of All Nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). These were settings, not exhibits, and the texts do not record Asian commissioners, visitors or critics who reacted to the displays at this fair.

Japanese gardens and the midway

Brown's chapter reproduces a photograph of the Japanese Garden at the Golden Gate Exposition, captioned with the 1939 date (Brown). Brown writes about gardens at the fairs in general. Photographs and postcards of them usually showed women, geisha in the terminology of the time, posed before an arched bridge, and Brown reads this feminisation as a non-threatening message for the whole country (Brown). He also describes the garden at the main Japanese site as a transitional space that visitors passed through before the national pavilion, where the government showed a contemporary Japan in photographs and dioramas (Brown).

The midway publicity promised the glamour and romance of the Pacific Ocean, and advertised Javanese and Oriental restaurants with idol dancers and native foods (Rydell 1993p. 90). Peterson gives the background of the Takarazuka Revue, founded in 1913 by Kobayashi Ichizō to fill his railway line to the hot springs resort near Osaka, and made up only of women performers (Peterson 2020p. 131).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Pacific empire — The texts read the Pacific Basin design as a display of American power across the ocean, with Hawaiian and Philippine pavilions at its centre (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Greenhalgh 1988p. 78).
  • Public works and jobs — The fair began as a Depression project that used federal money to build an island (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Rivalry with New York — San Francisco planned its fair against New York’s, and the rivalry kept many foreign nations away (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 122).
  • Architecture: nostalgia or future — Brechin reads the buildings as a backward-looking echo of 1915, while Rydell reads the Pacific Basin as a forward-looking imperial project (Benedict 1983p. 111; Rydell 1993p. 87).
  • Entertainment and spectacle — The Gayway, the Aquacade and the Cavalcade drew crowds and carried the fair’s message (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302).
  • Gender and exclusion — The Women’s Board was a domestic role, and women and African Americans had no prominent place (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
10World’s Fair Competitionp. 116

Allwood compares the New York World's Fair of 1939-40 with the Golden Gate International Exposition on Treasure Island in San Francisco, which opened in the same year. The New York fair, with its theme of 'The World of Tomorrow', the Trylon and Perisphere, its planned sectors and the reclaimed Corona Dumps site, is described first, along with the Aquacade, Railroads on Parade and the Swedish and Finnish pavilions. The chapter argues that the ambition for planned sectors and a unified architectural style was undone by exhibitors who ignored the plan, by loud flags and murals, and by an organizing body with little authority. The San Francisco fair is then set against it: an island built for the Bay Bridge and an airport, a site walled against the wind, a colour scheme that combined Guerin's 1915 palette with Urban's 1933 colours, and the Tower of the Sun. The chapter ends as war closes the fair and Treasure Island becomes a naval station.

Benedict, Burton. The Anthropology of World’s Fairs: San Francisco’s Panama Pacific International Exposition of 1915. London and Berkeley: Scolar Press, 1983.
Sailing to Byzantium: The Architecture of the FairGray Brechinp. 94

Brechin argues that the Panama Pacific International Exposition was an architectural fantasy of a walled Byzantine city, sustained for nine months, that stood in deliberate contrast to the disordered San Francisco it rose from after 1906. He reviews the abandoned citywide plans for permanent improvements and then the fair as built: Ernest Coxhead's compact court layout, in which eight palaces formed an inner core, the night lighting supervised by William D'Arcy Ryan, the Tower of Jewels with its Novagems, and Jules Guerin's colour scheme. A large part of the chapter is on the artificial travertine that gave the buildings an illusion of age, and on the Oriental and Mediterranean imagery visitors read into the fair. Brechin then follows the fair's influence on the later work of Kelham, Mullgardt and above all Maybeck, and its revival at the 1939 Treasure Island fair, which he presents as a stripped Moderne copy and a nostalgic nod to the past. He concludes that the fair was a brief realisation of a Byzantine myth of perfection, and that the city's future lay in urban redevelopment rather than in arcaded dream cities.

Brown, Kendall H. “Fair Japan: Japanese Gardens at American World’s Fairs, 1876–1940.” Journal article.
Performing on the World Stagep. 14Asia

Brown argues that Japanese gardens at world's fairs were staged performances, not neutral displays of beauty. Japan's official image split into a modern, industrial pavilion and a timeless, feminine garden. Brown ties this to fairs timed around Japan's wars, from Chicago in 1893 to San Francisco and New York in 1939 and 1940. The gardens, he concludes, naturalized a politically charged message, presenting Japan's imperial present as the natural expression of an unchanging culture.

In The Wake of Exposition Gardensp. 16Asia

Brown considers the gardens' legacy as relations between Japan and the United States soured from about 1913. American fascination with Japanese culture was separated from views of Japan as a political power, and the feminine, pastoral image became easy to dismiss once Japan was a threat. Rydell, Robert W. World of Fairs: The Century-of-Progress Expositions. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1993.
Introductionp. 1

Rydell opens with the depression-era world's fairs, which drew nearly one hundred million Americans to Chicago, San Diego, Dallas, Cleveland, San Francisco and New York, and asks why people paid to visit something so ephemeral while unemployment and political instability were so severe. Expositions, he argues, had long deflected criticism of the social order, and the interwar fairs, in modernistic dress, were built to restore faith in government, business, science and intellectual leaders. Their promise was of abundance and consumption within a racially exclusive social hierarchy, which he presents as continuous with the Victorian fairs rather than a break from them. The chapters take up colonial modernism, eugenics, the scientists' campaign to popularise science, a consumption-oriented welfare state, African American resistance and the American pavilion at Brussels in 1958. Rydell concludes that the century-of-progress expositions were theatres of power that gave a depression a sense of meaning.

ThreeColoniale Modernep. 61Asia

Rydell argues that colonial expositions between the wars made empire look like part of modernity, a fusion he calls coloniale moderne, and that American fairs adopted it. He begins with the Marseilles colonial fair of 1922 and the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where living colonial subjects and a pageant of empire were shown, and then the 1925 Paris decorative arts fair, where French colonial pavilions were given modernistic designs. The central case is the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, with its Angkor Wat replica and the U.S. government's prefabricated Mount Vernon, stocked with artifacts from the overseas territories. Rydell shows how protests in Puerto Rico and the Philippines were erased from the American display. The chapter then turns to Chicago's 1933 fair, which borrowed the colonial villages idea, and to San Francisco's 1939 Golden Gate exposition, where Pacific Basin pavilions and a Latin American court presented the West as a market for American goods. Rydell concludes that these fairs tied American expansion to the imperial policies of European powers.

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. Kargon and colleagues examine five international expositions planned in the years before the Second World War: Paris 1937, Düsseldorf 1937 and New York 1939, which were built, and Tokyo 1940 and Rome 1942, which were planned but never opened. The volume argues that these fairs were arenas of ideological struggle in which liberal, communist, fascist and militarist regimes competed to define modernity and to prepare their peoples for war. The introduction sets them against the history of fairs from the Crystal Palace of 1851 and the crises of the 1930s, and each chapter treats one fair through its planning documents and press coverage. The book closes with the first postwar universal exposition, Brussels 1958, which carried the Cold War rivalry into a fair devoted to peaceful atomic energy.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure as one case rather than as a theme. Japan is the only Asian nation with a substantial role, and its treatment is concentrated in chapter 5, "Modernity on Display: The 1940 Grand International Exposition of Japan", on the Tokyo and Yokohama exposition cancelled in 1938: the Commemoration Hall competition, the Nippon Kōbō studio, Bruno Taut's writings on Japanese architecture and the 1942 "Overcoming Modernity" symposium. Maloney's World's Fair Gardens (2012) traces the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and their lasting effect on public parks and gardens. Its main argument is that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, such as Fairmount Park, Jackson Park and Flushing Meadows, and that the pressure to create mature landscapes within months advanced transplanting, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The book has an introduction, one chapter on each of nine fairs (Philadelphia 1876, New Orleans 1884-85, Chicago 1893, Buffalo 1901, St. Louis 1904, Jamestown 1907, San Francisco 1915, Chicago 1933-34 and New York 1939-40), an epilogue on what the fairs left behind, and back matter with notes, reading list and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia, and Japan above all, is a substantial but secondary thread. Japanese gardens and exhibitors recur in six of the nine fair chapters, and the region is never the subject. Its heaviest concentration is in three chapters: Philadelphia 1876 (ch. 2, Centennial Exhibition), where Japan's bazaar, dwelling and garden helped set off a lasting American taste for things Japanesque; Chicago 1893 (ch. 4, World's Columbian Exposition), where the Japanese garden on Wooded Island, laid out by the gardener Izawa, won Olmsted's tacit approval although horticulturists found it inauthentic; and San Francisco 1915 (ch. 8, Panama-Pacific), where a three-acre Japanese garden was shipped in with more than 1,400 trees. Later chapters continue the pattern. St. Louis 1904 (ch. 6) has a Japanese display of nearly 150,000 square feet beside a Chinese pavilion garden; Chicago 1933 (ch. 9) has an 'Authentic Japanese Garden' tableau and Chinese dioramas; New York 1939 (ch. 10, The World of Tomorrow) has a Japanese pavilion and garden by Tamura and Sakurai that the city kept after the fair. Asian participation comes mostly through exhibits and gardens, not visitors. Southeast Asia is nearly absent: San Francisco's offered Siamese building was declined for lack of funds, and no Indonesian, Philippine or Indochinese display is discussed. The New Orleans chapter (ch. 3) has almost no Asian content.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Gelernter asks what the optimism of the 1939 New York World's Fair meant and why it has since vanished. He argues that the fair rested on a civic faith in America and a utopian future, that many of its promises came true in suburbs and highways, and that the faith itself has died. The book is built around the diary of Hortense Glassman, who walked the grounds with the engineer Mark Handler in 1939, and around later interviews with Mrs. Levine, who was Glassman. Early chapters follow the couple through a single day and set the pavilions beside essays on politics, technology, architecture and social rules. Later chapters follow Handler and Levine to his death in 1943, and the epilogue turns to the fair's history and to religion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive slight attention. Japan is the only regional power that recurs, and nearly always as a backdrop to war. Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. This edited volume, by Arthur P. Molella and Scott Gabriel Knowles, examines world's fairs as arenas of Cold War rivalry over science, technology and culture. Its sixteen chapters argue that the fairs both reflected and shaped Cold War ideas of progress, the space race, atomic energy, consumer technology, fashion, religion and urban renewal. Its main cases run from Brussels 1958 through New York 1964-1965, Montreal 1967, San Antonio 1968 and Osaka 1970 to Brisbane 1988, and the last chapters look at the fairs after 1989 up to Shanghai 2010. The Golden Gate International Exposition (GGIE) was a World's Fair held at Treasure Island in San Francisco, California, U.S. The exposition operated from February 18, 1939, through October 29, 1939, and from May 25, 1940, through September 29, 1940; it drew 17 million visitors to Treasure Island. Among other things, it celebrated the city's two newly built bridges: the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge and the Golden Gate Bridge.
