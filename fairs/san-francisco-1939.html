San Francisco · 1939–1940

Golden Gate International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Golden Gate International Exposition opened on Treasure Island, an artificial island in San Francisco Bay, on 18 February 1939 and closed on 29 September 1940, after a six-month break (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). It was a Depression-era project meant to celebrate the new bridges and to create jobs (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). A reference entry calls it "the last of the old-style world’s fairs" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). Its Pacific Basin theme and its colonial and Asian pavilions make it a useful case of an exposition presenting the United States as a power across the Pacific (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the new bridges. Civic leaders wanted to celebrate the Golden Gate and San Francisco-Oakland Bay bridges, and a fair promised jobs during the Depression as a quasi-public works project (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). San Francisco had already held fairs in 1894 and 1915, and many of the civic leaders behind the 1939 fair had also organised the Panama-Pacific International Exposition of 1915 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1993p. 85). Leland Cutler, a Republican and head of the San Francisco Chamber of Commerce, and George Creel, a Democrat who had run wartime propaganda, brought in exposition specialists to plan it (Rydell 1993p. 85). Rydell argues that the planners framed the fair around an empire of the West (Rydell 1993p. 85).

The fair also competed with New York. Supporters of New York in the federal government tried to force San Francisco to postpone. Roosevelt kept federal support for the West Coast fair, and Mayor La Guardia came to dine there (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). Roosevelt opened the fair in person (Rydell 1993p. 147).

Site and architecture

The fair stood on an island of about 400 acres in the shoals off Yerba Buena Island, reached by the Bay Bridge and by ferries. The island took about a year and a half to build in the early 1930s, using 287,000 tons of rock and 20 million cubic feet of sand, at a cost of $3.72 million in federal Works Projects Administration (WPA) funds (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Two elephant towers, modelled on Angkor Wat, flanked the entrance, and the 400-foot Tower of the Sun, an Art Deco tower capped with a golden phoenix, rose in the Court of Honor (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 301). Strong Pacific winds shaped the layout, and the pavilions formed a walled perimeter like a medieval city (Allwood et al. 2001p. 120).

Gray Brechin, in a chapter of Benedict’s collection, argues that the architects looked back. Many had worked on the 1915 fair, and they built a stripped, Moderne version of it in the middle of the Bay (Benedict 1983p. 111). Brechin calls the fair a nostalgic nod to the past, out of step with the modernism that the Chicago fair of 1933 had pointed towards (Benedict 1983p. 111). Brechin’s verdict is blunt: "Treasure Island was a poor compromise, for the Panama Pacific International Exposition was in all respects the greater exposition" (Benedict 1983p. 112).

Exhibits and attractions

Many visitors got their first look at television. The fair also showed a translucent General Motors Pontiac and a Westinghouse robot called Willie Vocalite (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). The 1939 art programme was a $40 million exhibit of Old Masters in the Palace of Fine and Decorative Arts. The paintings went back to Europe, and in 1940 Art in Action took the space (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303). Greenhalgh calls the Old Masters show the fullest gathering of its kind among fairs, and sets its gallery against the futuristic structures and entertainment of Treasure Island, the fair’s nickname (Greenhalgh 1988p. 217; Greenhalgh 1988p. 217).

The Gayway midway held Sally Rand’s Nude Ranch, a roller coaster and babies in incubators (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Billy Rose’s Aquacade, brought from New York in 1940 with Johnny Weissmuller, Esther Williams and Gertrude Ederle, made money for Rose (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). The Cavalcade of the Golden West ran with 200 actors and 200 animals in one account and with 300 actors in another (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 308). Some commercial exhibitors copied New York attractions, including the Nude Ranch and the Aquacade (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122).

Nations and peoples on show

The competition with New York kept many foreign nations away. Apart from France, Italy and Norway, the exhibitors were from Pacific-bordering nations or from Latin America (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122). Allwood, Allan and Reid add that gathering war clouds curtailed international support, so the exhibits of US industry provided most of the interest (Allwood et al. 2001p. 50). The planners grouped the regions under American military and economic control into a Latin American Court and a Pacific Basin area, and placed the rest in a zone of foreign pavilions (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78). Greenhalgh argues that the grouping implied the Latin American and Pacific areas were not foreign to the United States (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78). The Latin American Court presented its nations as producers of raw materials and consumers of American industrial surplus (Rydell 1993p. 90). The Pacific Basin area included the Hawaiian and Philippine pavilions from America’s Pacific empire (Rydell 1993p. 87).

The Pacific House held murals by Miguel Covarrubias and a colour-coded map of the Pacific’s peoples, which Rydell says lent anthropological cover to hierarchical ideas of race (Rydell 1993p. 89). At Creel’s urging, nearly forty Native Americans were shown in the government building, a display that Rydell likens to European colonial exhibits (Rydell 1993p. 89). The Women’s Board was headed by Hazel Pedlar Faulkner, and the texts record that women and African Americans had no prominent place at the fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh writes that the Board’s role was "a macrocosm of the housewife", taking on tasks that matched women’s work in the home (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). Rydell also describes the fair’s all-white employment policy as part of a wider pattern of exclusion at expositions (Rydell 1993p. 183).

Visitors, politics and aftermath

The visitor figures differ between texts. Findling and Pelle’s Historical Dictionary (1990) and Allwood, Allan and Reid’s survey both give a gross attendance of 17,041,999 for the two seasons and a debt of $559,423 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Findling and Pelle’s Encyclopedia (2008) gives 17,410,999 visitors and debts of $539,423 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). Opening day drew 138,000 visitors (Gelernter 1995p. 349). Maloney notes that attendance was under half that of New York (Maloney 2012p. 173).

Roche’s endnote mentions black trade-union pickets at the opening ceremony of the 1939 expo, but the same sentence names New York, so the texts do not settle which fair is meant (Roche 2000p. 240).

Plans to make the island an international airport were dropped when the U.S. Navy took over the island at the end of the second year, as war became imminent (Allwood et al. 2001p. 122; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). The island became a naval aviation station (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The buildings were quickly torn down, and the statue Pacifica was pulled over and broken (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309). Allwood, Allan and Reid note that many fairs, including this one, showed an operating profit that construction and demolition costs then wiped out (Allwood et al. 2001p. 132).

How historians have interpreted it

Rydell treats it as the West Coast culmination of what Rydell calls coloniale moderne, colonial display in a modern style (Rydell 1993p. 84). Rydell argues that the planners recast imperial display as a Pacific Rim empire of consumption, leisure and corporate modernism (Rydell 1993p. 87). Rydell ties the fair to New Deal public works and to the wish to restore Americans’ faith in their economic and political system (Rydell 1993p. 85). With Findling and Pelle in Fair America (2000), Rydell argues that the fair "breathed fresh life into dreams of an American empire in the Pacific Basin" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The same authors argue that the 1930s fairs diverted the public from the Depression (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Greenhalgh, in Ephemeral Vistas (1988), reads the fair’s imperial groupings and its Women’s Board as forms of control and domestic role (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78; Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). Schrenk, in Meet Me at the Fair (2014), reads the fair as a political exposition promoting Pacific unity, unlike the corporate fair in New York (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Samuel, in The End of the Innocence (2007), places it among the 1930s fairs that shored up faith in a consumer society, but holds that the New York fair celebrated consumerism most enthusiastically (Samuel 2007).

The readings agree that the Pacific theme sat at the centre of the fair. They differ on what it meant. Brechin reads the fair as backward-looking, while Rydell and Schrenk read it as a forward-looking imperial project (Benedict 1983p. 112; Rydell 1993p. 85; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Larson’s entry in the 2008 encyclopedia presents the fair as the culmination of American fair design, with a Pacific Basin theme that gave the site a cohesive plan (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 306). Roche notes that the organisers of inter-war expositions were less inhibited about colonialism, and that the San Francisco fairs of 1915 and 1939 were used to attract tourists and migrants (Roche 2000p. 59; Roche 2000p. 67). Roche’s table lists the fair as profit-making (Roche 2000p. 43), against the debts recorded by Findling and Pelle and by Allwood, Allan and Reid (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts show an Asian presence at this fair in three forms: a Japanese theatre company, the Japanese pavilion, and the colonial and national pavilions of the Pacific Basin. The texts say nothing about Chinese, Korean or Indian displays at this fair.

Japan

The Takarazuka Revue, an all-female Japanese theatre company, performed at the Golden Gate fair before its New York engagement (Peterson 2020p. 130). Peterson shows that the Revue’s tours were a partnership between the Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the Kokusai Bunka Shinkōkai (Society for International Cultural Relations, KBS) and the company itself (Peterson 2020p. 131). Peterson places the Revue within a government strategy to have the West accept Japan as a leader in Asia (Peterson 2020p. 131).

The Japanese design collective Nippon Kōbō was responsible for the interior of the Japanese pavilion at San Francisco (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96). Its displays used large photomurals made from photographs that had already appeared in Japanese promotional magazines (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96). Takashi Kōno, who had worked with the collective from about 1934 to 1939, helped design photographic murals for the Japanese pavilions at San Francisco (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 51). Natori Yōnosuke, a co-founder of the collective, was associated with KBS, a semi-governmental body set up in 1934 to promote cultural understanding, and his activities were backed by the Japanese government (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96).

In November 1938 the Japan Times and Mail reported that similar folk-craft exhibits would be sent to the San Francisco fair in 1939 (Kargon et al. 2015p. 98). The texts do not say whether they were shown. Kargon and co-authors treat such craft displays as part of Japan’s effort to publicise its modernity, and note that Japanese craft traditions were becoming the focus of world attention (Kargon et al. 2015p. 98).

The Pacific Basin pavilions

The Pacific Basin was the fair’s name for the countries around the ocean. Keatinge, a British official who had run imperial pageantry at Wembley, and Cutler, who travelled in Asia to secure exhibits, persuaded Pacific Rim countries and European colonial powers that pavilions in the Basin would serve their own interests (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell 1993p. 87). On opening day the Pacific Basin displays included buildings from Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Johore, Java, the Netherlands East Indies and French Indochina (Rydell 1993p. 87). The texts do not say who paid for the national pavilions.

The French consul general in San Francisco reported to his foreign minister that the Indochina building, a copy of the structure at the 1931 Paris colonial fair, would help hold the Pacific against Japanese advances (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell 1993p. 88). Rydell reads this as imperial display, in which the fair’s colonial pavilions were to show the Pacific as an American-led zone (Rydell 1993p. 87). Covarrubias’s colour-coded map gave Asian populations yellow and green, and Rydell argues that it lent anthropological cover to hierarchical ideas of race (Rydell 1993p. 89; Rydell 1993p. 89).

Asian motifs also appeared as decoration. Rydell describes a mixture of Asian-inspired styles around the Tower of the Sun (Rydell 1993p. 86). Brechin notes Cambodian and Malaysian motifs in the architecture (Benedict 1983p. 111). Thai lanterns lit the walkways of the Lake of All Nations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302). These were settings, not exhibits, and the texts do not record Asian commissioners, visitors or critics who reacted to the displays at this fair.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Pacific empire — The texts read the Pacific Basin design as a display of American power across the ocean, with Hawaiian and Philippine pavilions at its centre (Rydell 1993p. 87; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Greenhalgh 1988p. 78).
  • Public works and jobs — The fair began as a Depression project that used federal money to build an island (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Rivalry with New York — San Francisco planned its fair against New York’s, and the rivalry kept many foreign nations away (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303; Allwood et al. 2001p. 122).
  • Architecture: nostalgia or future — Brechin reads the buildings as a backward-looking echo of 1915, while Rydell reads the Pacific Basin as a forward-looking imperial project (Benedict 1983p. 111; Rydell 1993p. 87).
  • Entertainment and spectacle — The Gayway, the Aquacade and the Cavalcade drew crowds and carried the fair’s message (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 302).
  • Gender and exclusion — The Women’s Board was a domestic role, and women and African Americans had no prominent place (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Questions for study

  1. Did the Pacific Basin pavilions show Asian peoples and nations, or the choices of the colonial powers and organisers who paid for them? (Rydell 1993p. 87; Kargon et al. 2015p. 96)
  2. Brechin reads the fair as nostalgic, and Rydell reads it as a forward-looking imperial project. Which reading better explains the fair’s architecture? (Benedict 1983p. 111; Rydell 1993p. 85)
  3. Why do the reference works disagree on attendance and finances, and what evidence would settle the question? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 309; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 303)
  4. How did Japanese cultural tours and exhibitions, such as the Takarazuka Revue and the pavilion, shape foreign views of Japan? (Peterson 2020p. 131; Kargon et al. 2015p. 96)
  5. What did the fair’s exclusion of women and African Americans from leading roles reveal about the public it was built for? (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000)

Rydell's chapter 'Coloniale Moderne' treats the Golden Gate fair as the West Coast culmination of the Century-of-Progress model: he argues that its planners recast Victorian imperial display as a Pacific Rim empire of consumption, leisure and corporate modernism, with pavilions and Native American and Latin American exhibits presenting the United States as an imperial broker. He ties the fair to New Deal politics through public-works funds and Roosevelt's endorsement, and notes its 'lily white' employment policy as part of a pattern of exclusion at expositions. For Rydell the fair is one of the calculated responses of American elites to the crisis of capitalism, meant to restore faith in the nation's institutions and its imperial future.

Wikipedia

The Golden Gate International Exposition (GGIE) was a World's Fair held at Treasure Island in San Francisco, California, U.S. The exposition operated from February 18, 1939, through October 29, 1939, and from May 25, 1940, through September 29, 1940; it drew 17 million visitors to Treasure Island. Among other things, it celebrated the city's two newly built bridges: the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge and the Golden Gate Bridge.
