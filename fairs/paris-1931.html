Paris · 1931

International Colonial Exposition

Exposition Coloniale Internationale et des Pays d'Outre-Mer
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingOn the web

The Exposition Coloniale Internationale opened in the Bois de Vincennes, Paris, on 6 May 1931 and ran for six months (Geppert 2010p. 183). It was the largest French colonial exposition ever held (Geppert 2010p. 194) and the high point of greater France, the idea of empire as an extension of the nation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Historians use it to study how empire was staged for a mass public, and how visitors and opponents received that staging (Geppert 2010p. 179).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

Plans for a colonial exposition in Paris date from 1910, when it was first set for 1916; the war halted them (Geppert 2010p. 180). A law of 17 March 1920 named Paris as host for an interallied colonial exposition in 1925. The project was postponed six times and slipped by eleven years (Geppert 2010p. 180). It followed colonial fairs at Marseilles in 1906 and 1922 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Marshal Hubert Lyautey, a former governor of Morocco, became Commissaire général (the official in overall charge) in 1927, with Marcel Olivier as délégué général. The enterprise was publicly nicknamed Lyauteyville (Geppert 2010p. 181; Geppert 2010p. 181).

The colonial party, a network of lobbies that campaigned for empire, took a leading part in planning the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Its aim was to show what France had achieved in its colonies and to persuade a French public in economic crisis of the economic value of empire (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 261). Lyautey set out three lessons the fair should teach: history, economic activity and humanity (Geppert 2010p. 195).

Organisation and finance

Lagana's entry in the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions gives the conservative figure of at least 8 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265), and the Historical Dictionary repeats it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 264). Geppert gives about 33 million visitors, against roughly 27 million at Wembley over its two seasons (Geppert 2010p. 183). Geppert's note 15 gives the exact figure as 33,489,902 and calls the eight-million estimate unsupported (Geppert 2010p. 317). Benedict's table gives 33.5 million (Benedict 1983p. 31).

Money is also disputed. Geppert reports a profit of about 35 million francs (Geppert 2010p. 196). Hale gives receipts of over 318 million francs and a profit of 33 million francs (Hale 2008p. 167). The statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid records a profit of 29,000 francs, about £414 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Site and architecture

The grounds lay around Lac Daumesnil in the Bois de Vincennes. It was the first time a major Paris exposition had moved away from the Champ de Mars in the city centre (Geppert 2010p. 183). Three central pavilions faced the metropolis: the Cité des Informations, the Palais de la Section Métropolitaine and the Musée Permanent des Colonies (Geppert 2010p. 183). The layout was zoned. Civilised exhibits lay to the west, exotic ones to the east, and the international sections to the north (Geppert 2010p. 188).

The texts give different site sizes. Lagana's entry says the French empire took up most of 148 acres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Benedict's table gives 500 acres (Benedict 1983p. 31), and Rydell writes of over 200 acres (Rydell 1993p. 69). Hale gives 110 acres (Hale 2008p. 167).

The Musée Permanent des Colonies, designed by Albert Laprade, was the only permanent building. Lyautey had stipulated it, and the idea went back to a proposal by Charles Garnier in 1900 (Geppert 2010p. 189; Geppert 2010p. 190). Jaussely and Laprade are named as its architects (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 264). Çelik reports Laprade's argument that a colonial style would not flourish under the Parisian sky (Çelik 1992p. 183–184).

The Indochinese complex, designed by Charles and Gabriel Blanche under Joseph Albert Tournaire, took up a tenth of the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 264). Its centrepiece was a replica of the Cambodian temple Angkor Wat, built in the twelfth century and abandoned in 1431 (Geppert 2010p. 190). The texts disagree on its form. Geppert describes seven towers and stairways 55 metres high (Geppert 2010p. 191). Lagana describes five life-size towers, as high as 53 metres (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 264), and the Historical Dictionary also gives five towers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 264). The Tunisian quarter, designed by Victor Valensi, had a neo-Islamic main pavilion and a souk where about 300 Tunisians in national costume worked and sold goods (Çelik 1992p. 181–182).

Exhibits and attractions

The international information centre at the entrance held all the data on the exposition. Its 1,500-seat auditorium showed films, including 80 kilometres of film made on the colonies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 264). Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia showed about 400, 224 and 220 exhibits from local firms (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 263).

The fair staged ritual processions, concerts and regular African and Indochinese dances, and at night the site was lit, often with fireworks (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265). The zoo was built on the model of Hagenbeck's Tierpark and later became the permanent Zoo de Vincennes (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 47). Lagana reports over 5 million zoo visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265). Two hundred congresses and symposiums ran alongside the fair and produced some 3,000 reports (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 267). The fair was advertised as Le Tour du Monde en Un Jour (Hale 2008p. 88), and Allwood's account gives its slogan as See the World in a Day (Allwood et al. 2001p. 107). Geppert records about 10,000 schoolchildren brought from the provinces on organised trips, and more than 130 letters of thanks survive (Geppert 2010p. 196).

Nations and colonies on show

The French pavilions covered Madagascar, Indochina, Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon, French Guiana, Oceania, New Caledonia, Martinique, Réunion, Guadeloupe, French Equatorial Africa, North Africa and West Africa (Greenhalgh 1988p. 69). Greenhalgh calls this the most spectacular French imperial display since 1900 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 69). Belgium, Denmark, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal and the United States each had a national pavilion (Geppert 2010p. 193; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Britain was the main absentee. Lagana says the British government pleaded financial constraints to justify a very limited participation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Geppert says Britain first accepted, then largely withdrew, and offered a small display in the Cité des Informations (Geppert 2010p. 194). Lagana places Spain, Germany and Japan among those that refused to take part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). Geppert, by contrast, says Germany was not among the invitees (Geppert 2010p. 194). The texts do not reconcile these accounts.

The United States was the most unusual foreign participant. Its government agreed to an official representation, the first at a European colonial fair, and the pavilion was a replica of George Washington's house at Mount Vernon, supervised by Campbell Bascom Slemp (Geppert 2010p. 193; Rydell 1993p. 72). Italy built a rendering of the basilica at Leptis Magna, which Geppert reads as an effort to reclaim an ancient Roman legacy for Fascist propaganda (Geppert 2010p. 193).

Peoples on display

More than 3,000 colonial people were on the site, most prominently in the West African, Madagascan, Somali and New Caledonian sections (Geppert 2010p. 193). Greenhalgh calls Vincennes the last occasion anywhere on which a large area of villages was set up in an imperial context (Greenhalgh 1988p. 90). A Times correspondent reported that the French did not treat the colonial people at Vincennes as a freak show, and that Vietnamese, Cambodians and others went about their daily business without drawing curious attention (Geppert 2010p. 193).

Hale's account of the African displays is more critical. The army chose and trained 419 African and Indochinese soldiers at Fréjus for the parades (Hale 2008p. 108). The Ballet blanc et noir paired 100 African athletes with twenty dancers from the Paris Opéra. Hale reads it as suggesting that France was inadequate without its colonies (Hale 2008p. 109). Hale argues that the speeches on brotherhood meant little, because the exhibits presented Africans as exotic and less evolved than Europeans (Hale 2008p. 111).

Visitors and reception

Contemporaries widely judged the fair a success (Geppert 2010p. 196). Lagana writes that the ordinary citizen could not help being impressed by the exposition and informed about the empire (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265). Geppert argues that reception differed sharply between organisers, visitors, school groups, critics and the political left, and that the gap between intention and reception is central to the fair (Geppert 2010p. 179).

Politics and controversy

The Communist daily L'Humanité attacked colonial abuses and urged readers to boycott the fair (Geppert 2010p. 198). Soon after the opening, a group of Surrealists, an avant-garde circle of writers and artists, distributed 5,000 copies of a manifesto, Ne visitez pas l'Exposition Coloniale (Geppert 2010p. 198). A fire destroyed the Dutch pavilion on the night of 27 June 1931 (Geppert 2010p. 198). The Surrealists and Communists opened a counter-exposition, La Verite sur les colonies, on 20 September 1931 in a former exhibition pavilion outside the Bois (Geppert 2010p. 199). Rydell gives a different timing: a small Exposition anti-impérialiste, organised on the eve of the fair, which drew about 5,000 visitors according to Ageron (Rydell 1993p. 70; Rydell 1993p. 72). Hale says the opponents tried to disrupt the exposition, but their efforts failed under pressure from the authorities (Hale 2008p. 111). In July 1931 the Ministry of Colonies banned the recruitment of people for public display (Geppert 2010p. 199).

Aftermath and legacy

By the spring of 1932 all traces of the fair had been removed. The colonial museum survived. It was renamed the Musée de la France d'Outre-Mer in 1935 and the Musée des Arts africains et océaniens in 1960, and it reopened in 2007 as the Cité nationale de l'histoire de l'immigration (Geppert 2010p. 200). Chicago's planners for 1933 had visited the fair. Burnham judged it the greatest exposition of all times, and the planners urged similar native villages for their own fair (Rydell 1993p. 82). Roche argues that American organisers were less inhibited about colonialism as a theme at Chicago in 1933 and San Francisco in 1939 and 1940, after the United States accepted the invitation to Paris (Roche 2000p. 59). Ganz records that the Chicago diorama workshop made dioramas for the American exhibit in Paris (Ganz 2008).

How historians have interpreted it

The readings of the fair fall into three groups. Lagana, writing in both reference works, reads the fair as an instrument of imperial nationalism. Lagana argues that it fed the national myth of France's destiny (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 267; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 264). Çelik reads the fair's architecture as a conscious display of "the benefits of French colonialism" (Çelik 1992p. 135–136). Greenhalgh places Vincennes at the climax of French colonial display, arguing that it "focused Western imperialism more dramatically than any exhibition before or after it" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 70).

A second group stresses the audience. Geppert treats the fair as the apotheosis of imperial modernity and sees its reception as the key to the story (Geppert 2010p. 179). Geppert concludes that the exposition "signaled the beginning of the end of a particular type of imperial modernity" (Geppert 2010p. 195). Hale argues that the rhetoric of Franco-African brotherhood masked a hierarchy in which Africans were treated as less evolved (Hale 2008p. 111).

Rydell places the fair in the history of American fairs. Rydell calls it "a stunning imperial fantasyland" and argues that such fairs helped shape a coloniale moderne sensibility in the interwar years (Rydell 1993p. 69; Rydell 1993p. 61). Roche classes the fair as an imperial exposition that claimed to be international, and treats the American acceptance as a sign that imperial governments sought legitimacy from rivals (Roche 2000p. 59; Roche 2000p. 60). Benedict treats it briefly as one of the last displays of a declining empire, alongside Wembley (Benedict 1983p. 23).

The scholars agree that the fair was the high point of French imperial display. They differ on how far it succeeded. Lagana, Çelik and Greenhalgh stress the organisers' message. Geppert and Hale stress how visitors and opponents answered it, and Hale holds that the authorities overrode the protest. The texts say little about how well the message lasted with the public.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Indochina

Indochina was the fair's largest attraction, according to Rydell. Its centrepiece was a replica of Angkor Wat set among twenty-five pavilions with exhibits and indigenous people from Southeast Asia (Rydell 1993p. 70). Geppert calls the replica one of the fair's two highlights, with the colonial museum (Geppert 2010p. 190). The complex took up a tenth of the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 264).

The French organisers housed the Vietnamese, Cambodian and Laotian workers in separate dormitories. Officials presented 150 workers and 250 militiamen and tirailleurs (colonial infantry) from Indochina (Hale 2008p. 151). In March 1931 the commissioner general's technical committee agreed with a private concessionaire to run 100 single-seat and 50 double-seat rickshaws pulled by Chinese workers, and the committee bought the rickshaws. The Chinese consul in France protested strongly, and the plan was dropped (Hale 2008p. 151).

Hale describes how the Fête du Tourisme Colonial on 27 June ranked peoples by transport. Vietnamese rode bicycles, and Cambodian dancers rode in cars, the only colonial subjects to do so at the fair (Hale 2008p. 155). Hale reads this as the French claiming that the Indochinese were closest to modern civilisation (Hale 2008p. 155). Indochinese soldiers marched in the parades, and a Journée Indochinoise included a procession for the vice king of Tonkin (Hale 2008p. 155). In the closing weeks, performers from each colonial region descended a staircase modelled on Angkor's steps in a finale called L'Adieu des Colonies à la Métropole (Hale 2008p. 156).

The Dutch East Indies

Fifty Balinese performers took Paris by storm at the Dutch pavilion, drawing large audiences and press attention. Artaud was affected by the 90-minute show (Peterson 2020p. 212). Peterson places the fair at an early stage in the touring history of Indonesian dance, and notes that the dancer I Ketut Mario appeared there (Peterson 2020p. 219). The Dutch pavilion burned in June 1931, destroying its collections (Geppert 2010p. 198). Coté notes that the Netherlands used native exhibitions, including working villages, at its international exhibitions, among them Paris in 1931 (Cotép. 2).

The Philippines and the United States

The American pavilion was a prefabricated Mount Vernon built by Sears, Roebuck, and its annexes presented the Philippines, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, Hawaii and Alaska (Rydell 1993p. 74; Rydell 1993p. 75). In the Philippines annex, wax figures depicted Filipino types (Rydell 1993p. 76). Rydell says groups in Puerto Rico and the Philippines opposed the exhibit, but the displays did not acknowledge the protests (Rydell 1993p. 79; Rydell 1993p. 80). Rydell argues that the government's participation helped pave the way for American support of French efforts to keep Southeast Asian colonies in the 1940s and 1950s (Rydell 1993p. 82). A monument to Washington was dedicated in Saigon in gratitude for American participation in the fair (Rydell 1993p. 81).

Japan and other visitors

Japan refused to take part in the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263). None of the texts describes a Japanese display. Nguyen Van Tao, an Indochinese student, was arrested in April 1931 for protesting France's plans to execute Vietnamese prisoners, and forty Surrealists answered with a tract against the exposition (Hale 2008p. 178).

The texts say little more about Asian visitors and observers, and offer little direct interpretation of the Asian displays.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Imperial display and national prestige — The fair is treated as the high point of French imperial display and of greater France (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 261).
  • Economic propaganda — The colonial party aimed to persuade a public in crisis that empire paid, and the economic exhibits were central (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 261).
  • Native villages and human display — More than 3,000 colonial people were on site, and Greenhalgh calls Vincennes the last large imperial village display (Geppert 2010p. 193; Greenhalgh 1988p. 90).
  • Architecture and the exotic — The Tunisian quarter set exotic indigenous styles against the civilising mission of the Western-style Museum of the Colonies (Çelik 1992p. 181–182; Çelik 1992p. 183–184).
  • Visitors, schools and reception — The fair was widely judged a success, and school trips brought children from the provinces (Geppert 2010p. 196; Geppert 2010p. 196).
  • Opposition and counter-exhibition — L'Humanité, the Surrealists and the Communists opposed the fair, and the authorities' pressure is said to have overridden them (Geppert 2010p. 198; Hale 2008p. 111).

Questions for study

  1. How far could organisers control what visitors took away from a display that included school parties, protesters and a Surrealist critique? (Geppert 2010p. 196; Geppert 2010p. 198)
  2. Why do the texts disagree so sharply on visitor numbers, profit and site size, and what evidence would settle these figures? (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 265; Geppert 2010p. 196; Hale 2008p. 167)
  3. How did the displays of Indochinese people, shown by bicycle and car, differ from the displays of African soldiers and athletes? (Hale 2008p. 155; Hale 2008p. 109)
  4. Should the American pavilion, which Puerto Rican and Filipino groups opposed, be read as an imperial showcase or as a contested display? (Rydell 1993p. 79; Rydell 1993p. 80)
  5. How far did the fair's imperial message carry into later American and Southeast Asian politics? (Rydell 1993p. 82; Roche 2000p. 59)
  6. Why did Britain, Germany and Japan stay away, and what did their absence mean for the fair's claim to be international? (Geppert 2010p. 194; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 263)

Geppert's monograph on imperial expositions gives its sixth chapter to the 1931 Exposition Coloniale Internationale at Vincennes, which he treats as the apogee of French imperial modernity in the exposition medium. He argues against reading the fair as a simple, one-directional French propaganda machine: its displays were received very differently by visitors, school groups, critics and the Communist and Surrealist counter-exhibition, and the gap between organisers' intentions and reception is central to his reading. He shows how the fair's spatial and architectural choices (the Avenue des Colonies, the Angkor Wat replica, the colonial museum) built a 'dream city' of empire, and he places the fair within international exhibition networks rather than as an isolated French event. In the conclusion he reads the fair as the beginning of the end of this type of imperial spectacle, and the surviving colonial museum as a reminder of the cracks in its representational system.

In this encyclopedia entry, Marc Lagana presents the 1931 Exposition Coloniale Internationale at Vincennes as the peak of the 'greater France' idea, a fair the colonial party helped to plan in order to show what France had done in its colonies and to persuade the French public of the economic value of empire during an economic crisis. He describes the site, the central information centre, the colonial and foreign pavilions, the Angkor Wat replica and the zoo, and judges the fair a success that educated public opinion and produced a body of reports amounting to a colonial charter. Lagana's concluding argument is that the fair was a dynamic symbol of the Republic that fed the national myth of France's destiny, so the entry reads the fair as an instrument of imperial nationalism rather than a neutral showcase.

Rydell's chapter on 'coloniale moderne' presents the 1931 Paris Colonial and International Exposition as the high point of a European colonial fair tradition that fused modernist display with imperial propaganda. He stresses Lyautey as the driving figure and the Angkor Wat replica and native villages as the main attractions, and notes a small anti-imperialist counter-exposition. Rydell's central argument is that the fair's imperial displays shaped American fairs: the first official US exhibit at a European colonial fair, a Mount Vernon replica that framed US overseas possessions as benevolent, and Chicago's 1933 emulation of Paris's exotic villages. The chapter thus uses Paris 1931 to show how colonial design helped carry imperial ideas into American consumer culture.

Moderate discussion 3

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
Chapter 'Imperial Display' (printed pp. 69-73, the Vincennes passages and the Dutch and Belgian displays) and chapter 'Human Showcases' (printed pp. 89-94, the villages and the Senegalese); notes 42-43 nearby.

Greenhalgh's history of the exposition medium treats the 1931 Vincennes exposition as the most spectacular French imperial display since 1900 and as the most dramatic Western imperial exhibition of its kind, with Britain's absence as its only gap. He argues that its colonial pavilions followed a long-standing pattern in which Belgium, Holland and Portugal used fairs to display empire as a sign of their imperial standing. He presents the Vincennes village format as the last major example of the genre, by which time it was attracting criticism of imperialism and racism, and he notes that Senegalese performers were in Paris in 1931 as they had been at earlier Paris fairs.
