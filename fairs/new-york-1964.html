New York World's Fair (1964-65)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The New York World's Fair of 1964-65 ran at Flushing Meadows-Corona Park in Queens, on the grounds of the 1939-40 fair, in two six-month seasons from April 1964 to October 1965 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 136; Samuel 2007). It drew about 51.6 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 327). The Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), the body that registers official world's fairs, did not sanction it (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 80). Historians study it as the last large American world's fair, and as a case of corporate sponsorship, civic planning under Robert Moses and the limits of the fair as a cultural form (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair began as a private proposal. Robert Kopple, a New York lawyer and former concessionaire at the 1939-40 fair, conceived it around 1958 (Samuel 2007; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322). It was to mark the 300th anniversary of the English takeover of New York from the Dutch in 1664, under the theme Peace Through Understanding (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 330). Rival bids came from Seattle, Los Angeles, Chicago and Washington, D.C. (Cotter & Young 2008p. 9).

Robert Moses, who ran the Triborough Bridge and Transit Authority, became the fair's president. The planners knew of his interest in a second fair at Flushing Meadows Park after the 1939-40 fair went bankrupt (Cotter & Young 2008p. 11). He wanted the new fair to finish the park the earlier fair had left unfinished (Cotter & Young 2008p. 117; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322). The BIE refused to sanction the project. Its November 1960 ruling barred member nations from exhibiting at American fairs other than Seattle's, and Moses dismissed the BIE with contempt (Samuel 2007; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Organisation and finance

The World's Fair Corporation ran the fair. Moses kept construction costs low so that the fair would earn enough to pay for a zoo, a botanical garden and roads (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 330). Cotter and Young put the work at more than five years and $1 billion (Cotter & Young 2008p. 99). The projected $50 million surplus never appeared. Moses put the remaining $1.5 million in cash into the park instead of repaying the fair's debts (Cotter & Young 2008p. 117).

Attendance fell short of forecasts. Moses projected seventy million visitors (Kargon et al. 2015p. 81), and Lawrence gives 73 million, with 28 million advance tickets sold (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322). The 1964 season lost $20 million and the 1965 season added $1 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 327; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 331). Allwood, Allan and Reid give the loss as $17.5 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Note holders received 62.4 cents for each dollar invested (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 327). Lawrence adds that revenues ran 40 percent below predictions and that unused advance tickets had been counted as 1964 income (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 328).

Corporations paid for much of the fair. The editors of the reader Meet Me at the Fair describe it as the most commercialised American fair, with less government sponsorship than any before, and with more American corporations than foreign nations (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 5). U.S. Steel gave the Unisphere and insisted on a credit line (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 5). Samuel reports that Moses said the fair would not succeed without the U.S. government, the Vatican and General Motors (Samuel 2007). The city comptroller's interim audit of September 1965 charged the corporation with serious mismanagement, and the corporation had paid Disney Enterprises $250,000 for the Lincoln exhibit at the Illinois Pavilion (Samuel 2007).

Site and architecture

The planners reused the 1939-40 plan in a more formal manner. The avenues were simplified, and the Unisphere, a steel globe 120 feet across, replaced the Trylon and Perisphere at the centre (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322; Allwood et al. 2001p. 134). Its design changed during planning: the rotating globe was dropped, and water jets obscured its base (Cotter & Young 2008p. 25). The 1939 power system was built so well that much of it served the later fair (Cotter 2009).

Many designs were never built, among them a Goodyear proposal, a Texas-NASA pavilion and a Century Showcase (Cotter & Young 2008p. 53). The World of Food pavilion was abandoned (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110). Architects, including Bunshaft, resigned in protest at Moses's populist planning, which favoured no single aesthetic period (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 332). The planning team left early over the standard of building (Allwood et al. 2001p. 136).

Exhibits and nations

General Motors' Futurama II showed a future of conquest of deep space, jungles, oceans and deserts, all tied to a world market (Kargon et al. 2015p. 81). Disney's four attractions became prototypes for Disney parks, and Thomas calls EPCOT a permanent world's fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 432; Roche 2000p. 129). Michelangelo's Pietà, lent by Pope John XXIII, drew 27 million viewers, second only to Futurama II (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 333; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 100). Moses excluded most of the titillating shows of earlier fairs, and a Bourbon Street strip show was closed in 1965 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 334; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Sabat reads Moses's Panorama of New York City, a model made for the fair, as a presentation of the city as a utopia achieved (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 282).

Most major European nations stayed away. About eighty nations appeared in thirty-seven pavilions, about fourteen of them privately sponsored (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322). The Asian states are discussed in the ASIA section below.

Johnson Wax's film To Be Alive! was the surprise hit of the first season. It ran for eighteen minutes on three screens, each eighteen feet wide, was free, and was shown thirty times a day (Samuel 2007). Johnson Wax first asked Francis Thompson for a film on the new leisure in America, in which its time-saving products would star. Thompson talked the company out of that idea and made a non-commercial film about the joy of living, with no Johnson Wax products in it (Samuel 2007). The film won the 1965 Oscar for best short documentary (Samuel 2007).

GE's Carousel of Progress revolved six audiences past tableaux from the 1890s, the 1920s, the 1940s and the present day (Samuel 2007). A reporter for Time, less impressed with the technology than most visitors, summed up the ride as "the general effect of a family under the influence of drugs" (Samuel 2007). GE then put a further twenty million dollars into advertising to tie in with the ride (Samuel 2007).

Visitors, protest and reception

Opening day brought the most visible protest. James Farmer of CORE and Bayard Rustin disrupted President Johnson's speech at the Federal Pavilion, and Farmer and Rustin were arrested at the New York State Pavilion (Samuel 2007). More than 300 people were arrested during the protests (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 331; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Uva finds the planned stall-in against the Unisphere ill-attended (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 82), and Allwood, Allan and Reid note that it failed to materialise in great numbers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 137). Women Strike for Peace marched in silence around the Unisphere pool and near the Japanese Pavilion in August 1965 (Samuel 2007). Gaskins argues that the fair had no African American presence (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 159).

Visitors numbered 27,148,280 in 1964 and 24,458,757 in 1965 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 327). Samuel calls it the most attended American world's fair (Samuel 2007). Peterson gives 54 million (Peterson 2020p. 168), and Thomas gives 50 to 52 million (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 423). Weimann records a Woman's Hospitality Center on the roof of the Better Living Center, sponsored by the Purex Corporation (Weimann 1981p. 598).

Aftermath and legacy

Demolition began on the morning of 18 October 1965 (Cotter & Young 2008p. 83). Exhibitors had 90 days to clear their displays. The corporation spent more than half a million dollars removing about 35 abandoned pavilions, and the site went to the city on 3 June 1967 (Cotter & Young 2008p. 99). Cotter and Young write that most of the fair was demolished within six to twelve months of closing (Cotter & Young 2008p. 117). Only a handful of structures survived, including the United States and New York State pavilions (Cotter & Young 2008p. 117). The Unisphere remains, and the 1939-40 New York City Building houses the Queens Museum of Art (Cotter & Young 2008p. 121). The Queens Botanical Gardens had to move to the Kissena Corridor (Maloney 2012p. 196). Cotter and Young end on the fair's transience: "Here today and gone tomorrow; that is the nature of a world’s fair." (Cotter & Young 2008p. 99)

The Billy Graham Pavilion

The Billy Graham Evangelistic Association (BGEA) was offered space in the Protestant Center on 30 March 1962 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 120). It asked for a separate site instead. At a meeting with Robert Moses on 10 August 1962 it was given twice the usual free space and allowed to sell Bibles, books and records (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 121). A signed lease and an architectural plan were due by 1 October, and the BGEA met the deadline (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 121). The Protestant Council objected that a separate building would tie Graham's work to one Protestant faction (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 121).

Ground-breaking took place on 17 April 1963 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 121). The octagonal building, designed by Edward Durell Stone, had a detached 100-foot tower capped by a golden sunburst (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 122). Its lobby held a revolving globe with 1,075 jewelled cities, 175 where Graham had held crusades and 900 where his radio programme was broadcast (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 123). The highlight was Man in the 5th Dimension, a twenty-eight-minute film in 70mm Todd-AO, shown twelve times a day. Borden calls it "a visual crusade for the BGEA" (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 123). Over five million visitors from 135 nations passed through (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 126), and public decisions for Christ numbered 590,000 in 1964 and 517,000 in 1965 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 126). Of four religious pavilions not backed by a single church, only Graham's broke even, through donations (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 127). Borden gives the Vatican Pavilion 26 million visitors (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 127), against the 27 million figure given above.

The World of Food pavilion

Lionel K. Levy designed the World of Food pavilion (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110). Its rent was estimated at $14.5 million and its construction at $4,482,000 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110). The design was cut from five floors to three and a mezzanine (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110p. 111). The first floor held RCA/Whirlpool's Miracle Kitchen, beside Adolph's International Steak House and a replica of Hershey Chocolate World (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 111). The second floor held a 5,000-square-foot supermarket run by H. C. Bohack Co. (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 114). Its machines drew up customised menus, and shoppers paid only by credit card (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 114p. 115). Early renters included Whirlpool, Hershey, Pepsi-Cola and Seabrook Farms (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 112). Robert Moses and two colleagues turned the first earth on 23 January 1963 before about a thousand people (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 111). Work slowed, and the steel skeleton was razed in April 1964 (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 112).

Fair officials feared that the building would not be finished by opening day and ordered it demolished. Court battles followed, and the remains ended as a pile of steel beams stored in Brooklyn (Cotter & Young 2008p. 58). The order had been announced in advance: "Mr. Moses has decided that the structure should be demolished" (Cotter & Young 2008p. 51). Scarpellini argues that the kitchen and supermarket shifted the focus from producers to female consumers (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 114).

Money and the cost of exhibiting

Businesses spent a total of $300 million on their exhibits (Samuel 2007). GM's exhibit cost more than $50 million, Ford's $30 million, and GE's and AT&T's $15 million each (Samuel 2007). Steven Van Voorbis, manager of GE's Progressland, asked "Where else could we get the undivided attention of a captive audience of 74 million people?" (Samuel 2007). Sponsors also paid for props: Chrysler paid $50,000 to park two cars in the House of Good Taste, and Ford paid $20,000 for a Mustang and a Thunderbird in a Dick Button show (Samuel 2007). Each company in the "Wonder World" street scene paid $15,000 for its sign (Samuel 2007).

Exhibitors' actual costs came to $1 to $3 per person for each pavilion visit, higher than forecast because attendance fell short (Samuel 2007). The Pavilion of American Interiors went bankrupt in the second season (Samuel 2007). Asked to exhibit at Expo 67 in Montreal, the Big Three automakers passed on separate pavilions (Samuel 2007).

Phantom pavilions

Exhibiting was too expensive for all but a few sponsors, and many cut back or dropped out (Cotter & Young 2008p. 51). Rheingold's first design for Little Old New York had large elevators lifting guests to platforms over the fair (Cotter & Young 2008p. 52). The Marine Center, a 130,000-square-foot complex around a man-made lake, was to have stood where the Hall of Science was later built (Cotter & Young 2008p. 58). Chile nearly joined, but the dispute with the BIE made many governments reluctant to take part (Cotter & Young 2008p. 59). Georgia reserved a 69,584-square-foot plot near the Unisphere, and its project got little beyond early designs (Cotter & Young 2008p. 60). The United Nations had a pavilion only because it took over the failed Sierra Leone pavilion for the 1965 season (Cotter & Young 2008p. 60).

Women at the fair

Samuel lists female guides at the IBM and Hertz exhibits, at the Japanese and Indian pavilions and at Texas's state pavilion (Samuel 2007). Clairol's pavilion was for women only, and its Color Carousel took women through forty private booths (Samuel 2007). The Simmons Beautyrest Center used married women as "Beautyrest Ladies" to wake napping visitors (Samuel 2007). Samuel sets these hostesses against The Feminine Mystique, published a year earlier (Samuel 2007). Weimann records a Woman's Hospitality Center on the roof of the Better Living Center, sponsored by Purex, where women's groups held teas (Weimann 1981p. 598). For 1939, he notes, women's planning had centred on essay contests and goodwill tours (Weimann 1981p. 598).

How historians have interpreted it

The main dispute is whether the fair failed. Samuel argues that it has been wrongly dismissed, and that visitors' enjoyment, which Samuel calls popular memory, should be weighed against the official memory of debts, feuds and the BIE's refusal (Samuel 2007). Samuel reads the fair's conservatism as an asset: Moses's guarded grounds offered visitors a bridge over the troubled times (Samuel 2007). The real failure, in Samuel's view, was narrower: the fair left out youth culture and African American expression (Samuel 2007).

Allwood, Allan and Reid take a harsher view. They conclude that the fair "probably did more to discredit international exhibitions than any event before or since" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 133). Rydell, Findling and Pelle blame Moses's autocratic manner and his neglect of expert advice, and they read the failure as a sign of the limits of the fair as a cultural form (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Lawrence accepts the financial failure but locates the fair's real achievements in its architecture and exhibits, which Moses's management and lax accounting undermined (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322).

Uroskie reads the fair as having "become a kind of living relic—a museum in reverse," whose technologies pointed backward (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 330). Uva, writing in the Molella and Knowles volume, argues that the fair refused to face the failures of midcentury urbanism and proposed that Americans stay the course (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 79). Scarpellini finds the food displays shifting from producers to consumers, in a market narrative addressed to women (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110). Kargon and colleagues note that the fair opened six months after Kennedy's assassination, and that a new-frontiers subtheme governed it (Kargon et al. 2015p. 81). Thomas reads the fair's distinctive quality as deliberate fun (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 428). Gaskins reads the Unisphere through the exclusion of Black Americans (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 155). Benedict calls the fair a commercial hodgepodge (Benedict 1983p. 24). Mandell contrasts the corporate public relations of later American fairs with the idealism of the last French expositions (Mandell 1967p. 119).

The texts agree on the fair's commerce, Moses's centrality and its financial failure. They differ on what failure means: money for Lawrence and Allwood, visitors' experience for Samuel, Cold War ideology for Uva and consumer culture for Thomas.

Samuel argues that critics' verdicts on the Better Living Center were shaped by their dislike of Robert Moses, and that the public enjoyed the building (Samuel 2007). Time called it a trap in which salesmen clawed for the jugular (Samuel 2007). Samuel counts some 175 exhibitors in the building (Samuel 2007).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Status and organisers

The BIE did not sanction the fair, and most major European nations took no official part (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Asian states were nonetheless present. Lawrence lists India, Pakistan, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Nationalist China, the Philippines and Thailand as sponsors of pavilions, with Hong Kong among the privately sponsored exhibits (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 323). Uroskie notes that many of the foreign nations that did take part were newly independent African and Asian states, some represented only through private commercial interests (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 330). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say that for the first time developing nations enjoyed a significant presence at an American fair, with exhibits from newly independent Asian and African nations (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The Philippines

Peterson describes the Philippine pavilion as standing near the Unisphere, with guides drawn from the country's best families who gave regular dance programmes (Peterson 2020p. 167). Government funds had fallen short for the 1962 Seattle fair, so private donors met the shortfall. Domingo Arcega, a department-store magnate, became executive director and linked the pavilion to the economic programme of President Macapagal (Peterson 2020p. 169). Philippine Week in June 1964 brought Filipino dance and fashion into midtown Manhattan (Peterson 2020p. 167).

Indonesia

Sukarno, the Indonesian president, shaped the pavilion himself and reputedly chose its women guides. Its centrepiece was a theatre restaurant with music and dance (Peterson 2020p. 195; Peterson 2020p. 211). The total cost was US$6.5 million, according to a later press account (Peterson 2020p. 208). Peterson counts that over half the New York newspaper articles on the pavilion focused on its women, and that 17 of 31 articles (55 percent) did so once reports of the shutdown and a theft were removed (Peterson 2020p. 210). The Herald Tribune reported in January 1965 that Sukarno had helped select 25 guides from about 55 girls (Peterson 2020p. 211).

The texts give three accounts of its closure. Samuel says the pavilion ran for one season and that Indonesia stayed away in 1965, reportedly in protest at American support for Malaysia (Samuel 2007). Peterson says Sukarno withdrew Indonesia from the United Nations and the pavilion was boarded up for the rest of the fair (Peterson 2020p. 35), and that in March 1965 he formally halted plans to exhibit (Peterson 2020p. 225). Cotter and Young say the corporation closed the pavilion for 1965 after months of unpaid rent (Cotter & Young 2008p. 80).

Japan

The Japan Pavilion showed Japan's industrial future, including transistors, Hitachi television and the Shinkansen (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 467). Japanese companies such as Hitachi and NEC bought the advertisements in the official guide, fourteen in the 1964 edition and twelve in 1965 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 471). Walters argues that the pavilion's literature used the fair to promote economic growth more than cultural exchange (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 471). Its restaurants employed more than 120 kimono-clad hostesses and waitresses, chosen with care (Samuel 2007).

Other pavilions and visitors

The Korean pavilion's restaurant was served by thirty young women in traditional costume (Samuel 2007). President Chung Hee Park of South Korea and Deputy Prime Minister Chaerul Saleh of Indonesia were among the visiting dignitaries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 327). Ancient treasures from China, India and Pakistan were shown among the art (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 325). Samuel records that President Kennedy barred Communist China from the fair because the United States did not recognise its government, while the Taipei government accepted an invitation (Samuel 2007).

Interpretation

Peterson argues that these pavilions gave newly independent Southeast Asian nations "a unique and valued opportunity to connect with millions of Americans" (Peterson 2020p. 19). Peterson also argues that the pavilions staged national self-images through live performance, and that the press framed the women through a male, exoticising gaze (Peterson 2020p. 210).

Japan's industrial case

Walters says Japanese exports to the United States were hovering just below imports, a balance reached within a decade of John Foster Dulles's advice that Japan would find no large US market (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 471). Japan ranked first in shipbuilding, motorcycles, cameras and microscopes, and fifth in automobiles, where output rose from 482,256 units in 1960 to 1,283,488 in 1963 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 468). Toray showed kimonos made from its own synthetic fibres (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 470). Sony showed a 145-pound portable Videocorder for which there was no American counterpart (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 469).

The Philippine pavilion and its women

Peterson describes a circular, largely open pavilion designed by Otilio Arellano, with a roof shaped like a sakalot, a traditional farmer's hat (Peterson 2020p. 179). Gloria Macapagal represented her father, the President, at the fair; she was sixteen at her first visit in 1963 (Peterson 2020p. 176). During Philippines Week, 8-15 June 1964, she headed the official party ahead of the Philippine Ambassador (Peterson 2020p. 178). Peterson argues that Filipino women largely secured participation, notably Medina Lacson de Leon, Under Secretary of Commerce (Peterson 2020p. 177p. 178). At the groundbreaking the official photographs show no women on the stage, although the reception photographs show young women in Filipiniana dress (Peterson 2020p. 174).

Press photographs

Of the 58 feature photographs that accompanied 52 newspaper articles on Asian participation, 30 showed Asian women, and every human subject except Indian Prime Minister Gandhi was a young Asian woman (Peterson 2020p. 170). Peterson shows that captions stressed beauty and availability (Peterson 2020p. 170).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Moses and the park — the park Moses wanted shaped the fair's planning and finances, and the texts judge the fair largely by the park that survived it (Cotter & Young 2008p. 117; Cotter & Young 2008p. 117; Maloney 2012p. 196).
  • Commerce and sponsors — the texts treat the fair as the most commercial American fair, with corporate pavilions competing for attention and a sponsor's name on the Unisphere (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 5; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • The Unisphere and the 1939 site — the fair reused the earlier plan and replaced its central symbol, and critics read the Unisphere as a sign of exclusion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 322; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 155).
  • Protest and exclusion — opening-day protests and the planned stall-ins test the fair's claim to peace, and Samuel's bubble reading meets Gaskins's claim that Black Americans were absent (Samuel 2007; Samuel 2007; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 159).
  • Official and popular memory — Samuel sets the fair's debts against the enjoyment of its visitors, and Thomas treats fun as the fair's deliberate structure (Samuel 2007; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 428).
  • Cold War and consumer culture — Uva reads the pavilions as an endorsement of suburbs and highways, and Scarpellini reads the food displays as a market narrative aimed at women (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 79; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 110).
  • Asian and African participation — the fair gave developing nations a first significant presence at an American fair, and the texts differ on how far the Asian pavilions were state projects (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 323).

Questions for study

  1. Samuel measures the fair by its visitors, while Lawrence and Allwood measure it by its accounts. Which measure should a historian use for a fair that lost money but drew record crowds?
  2. The BIE refused to sanction the fair. Did that matter to visitors and exhibitors, or only to the reputation of the organisers?
  3. Why did newly independent Asian and African states take part in a commercial fair that the BIE did not recognise, and what did they expect to gain?
  4. Should a park built with the fair's money count as part of the fair's legacy, or only as a side effect of Moses's plans?
  5. Samuel's bubble reading explains why the fair felt calm, but whose experience does it leave out, and does that exclusion weaken the claim that the fair was a success?
  6. Uva reads the pavilions as Cold War messages, while Walters reads the Japan Pavilion as economic promotion. How far did the period decide what a display meant?
  7. Samuel reads critics of the Better Living Center through their dislike of Moses. If that is so, how should a historian weigh a critic's verdict against the crowds the same building drew?
  8. The BGEA won free space and its own building after the Protestant Council was refused. What did a separate religious pavilion add to the fair's aim of Peace Through Understanding, and why did the Protestant Council object?
  9. Peterson says the press framed Filipino and Indonesian women through a male gaze, yet Filipino women helped shape their own image. Why did similar guides read so differently in the press?

A book about this fair, chapter by chapter 10

Cotter, Bill, and Bill Young. The 1964–1965 New York World’s Fair: Creation and Legacy. Charleston, SC: Arcadia, 2008.

Cotter and Young, The 1964-1965 New York World's Fair, is a picture book of photographs, concept art, models and captions that follows the fair from its creation to its afterlife. Its main argument, made most directly in the closing chapter, is that Robert Moses used the fair chiefly to finish the Flushing Meadows park he had planned for the 1939-1940 site, spending leftover money on grounds rather than repaying creditors. The scope is one fair in one city: the 1959 proposal and planning, construction, the 'phantom fair' of cancelled and altered pavilions, the two seasons, the sale and demolition of the buildings, and the restored park. The introduction is followed by nine chapters that run roughly in chronological order, with captions doing much of the narrative work.

Introductionp. 7

Cotter and Young introduce a sequel to their earlier book on the 1964-1965 New York World's Fair, covering how the fair was created, built and left as a legacy. Cotter explains that many pavilion-building organisations have gone and corporate records were lost, so the book relies on photographs and personal collections. Young sets out its scope, from five years of design and construction to two years of park restoration.

In the contents of this work

1Why a Fair?p. 9

Cotter and Young trace the fair to a 1959 proposal by New York businessmen led by Thomas J. Deegan to mark the city's 300th anniversary. They argue that New York won federal backing over Seattle, Los Angeles, Chicago and Washington largely through politics, and reproduce a 1959 New York Times report. The chapter ends with Robert Moses being chosen to head the corporation, which the authors present as his chance to finish the park left unfinished after the 1939-1940 fair.

In the contents of this work

2Designing the Fairp. 13

Cotter and Young explain that the fair reused the roads and utilities of the 1939 fair site at Flushing Meadows, and that its pavilions used space-frame structures, fiberglass and a relaxed building code to keep costs down. Kargon compares the two New York world's fairs, of 1939-1940 and 1964-1965, through their visions of the future, using General Motors' Futurama as the main case. Wikipedia

The 1964 New York World's Fair (also known as the 1964–1965 New York World's Fair) was an international exposition at Flushing Meadows–Corona Park in Queens, New York City, United States. The fair included exhibitions, activities, performances, films, art, and food presented by 80 nations, 24 U.S. states, and nearly 350 American companies. The five sections of the 646-acre (261 ha) fairground were the Federal and State, International, Transportation, Lake Amusement, and Industrial areas. The fair's theme was "Peace through Understanding", and its symbol was the Unisphere, a stainless-steel model of Earth. Initially, the fair had 139 pavilions, and 34 concessions and shows. The site had previously hosted the 1939 New York World's Fair. In the 1950s, several businessmen devised plans for a similar event in 1964, and the New York World's Fair 1964 Corporation (WFC) was formed in 1959.
