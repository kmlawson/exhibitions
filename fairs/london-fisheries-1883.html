London · 1883

International Fisheries Exhibition (London)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The International Fisheries Exhibition was held at South Kensington, London, in 1883. It was the first of four annual themed international exhibitions on the same grounds, followed by health (1884), inventions (1885) and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition (1886) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Philip Cunliffe-Owen turned a modest fisheries project into a fashionable success with entertainment and spectacle (Filipová 2015p. 206). Greenhalgh reads it as a commodity show at the start of a sequence that ends in imperial pageantry (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). The texts give the year and place but no opening or closing dates and no attendance figures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 93).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair followed an earlier South Kensington series, of 1871 to 1874, which had not succeeded. That series failed to excite public enthusiasm. Its projected run of ten annual exhibitions closed after four, with sparse audiences and a deficit of £150,000 (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Philip Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protégé and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, took over an International Fisheries Exhibition scheduled for 1883 and transformed it (Filipová 2015p. 206). His work as a commissioner at the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876 and the Paris exposition of 1878 shaped a more visitor-friendly approach (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Greenhalgh places the fair among four commodity shows held at South Kensington between 1883 and 1886 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). In Greenhalgh's account, all of these except the last had the feel of giant conferences or trade fairs, and their purpose was to examine commerce and industry, with entrance profits taking second place to national economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33).

Organisation and the site

Cunliffe-Owen directed the South Kensington exhibitions held in London from 1883 to 1886 (Geppert 2010p. 266). In those years, Cunliffe-Owen organised a different exhibition each year on various themes (Geppert 2010p. 102).

Queensland and New South Wales also took part through their agents. They asked their Agents-General in London to act as official representatives at the 1883 show, rather than incurring the "unnecessary expense" of appointing an additional official (Hoffenberg 2001p. 44).

The texts give no figures for the fair's cost or income (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). The site was the South Kensington grounds. The 1886 show used the same spaces as the earlier shows, temporary arcades built over 24 acres around what was left of the Royal Horticultural Society's gardens (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). The Colonial and Indian Exhibition leased the arcades from the 1883 fisheries exhibition and added a few galleries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95).

Exhibits and attractions

Cunliffe-Owen added outdoor attractions to the fisheries show: promenades under electric light, illuminated fountains, visual effects and musical entertainments (Filipová 2015p. 206). The fisheries theme was stretched to cover things in general (Filipová 2015p. 206).

In 1883 there was an exhibition of seascapes and of art generally related to fish, and painting and sculpture were fitted in where they could be found conceptually to fit (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). An aquarium left over from the fisheries year was re-used at the 1886 show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89).

The texts describe the South Kensington shows of the early and mid-1880s as places of amusement and education. They stayed open at night, had shops, lit gardens and concerts, and invited visitors to take part (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).

Nations and colonies on show

The texts name Queensland, New South Wales and South Australia among the colonial participants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 44; Hoffenberg 2001p. 85). South Australia's Agent-General sent a set of publications connected with the Fisheries Exhibition to Adelaide's Public Library, whose readers used them after the South Kensington shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 85).

The Smithsonian Institution sent exhibits to the London show (Rydell 1984). Badger notes that by the 1880s American participation in smaller specialised fisheries exhibits, such as those in Berlin in 1880 and London in 1883, had become predictable and even expected (Badger 1979p. 15).

Asian participation is set out in the section below.

Peoples on display and visitors

The texts record no displays of people at the 1883 show. The 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition had a special Native Compound for visiting indigenous craftsmen (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95).

The Queen did not open the 1883 show. Poor health prevented her from officially opening it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 259). Hoffenberg notes that the Queen centred the state ceremony at Albert Hall for the Colonial and Indian Exhibition three years later (Hoffenberg 2001p. 259).

The texts describe the show as a fashionable success (Filipová 2015p. 206), and say that the Prince of Wales took up its popularity (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Aftermath and legacy

The Prince of Wales's patronage led to a programme of three further annual shows: Health in 1884, Inventions in 1885 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in 1886, all directed by Cunliffe-Owen (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Hoffenberg reports that Jules Joubert advised the organisers of Melbourne's Centennial Exhibition to follow the precedent of the South Kensington shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250). Ramsay's fish specimens from India and the Indian Ocean went on permanent display in Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47).

Geppert notes that, so far, only the last of the four South Kensington shows has found some, if limited, historiographical interest (Geppert 2010p. 305).

How historians have interpreted it

The texts agree on placing the fisheries show in the sequence of South Kensington shows of 1883 to 1886 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33; Filipová 2015p. 206; Geppert 2010p. 305). They differ in what they make of it.

Greenhalgh argues that the fisheries, health and inventions shows were types of produce rather than cultures (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh reads the sequence as treating empire as a resource and commodity, and never as people with lives and traditions of their own (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh also reads the four shows as revealing a lack of enthusiasm for the fine arts (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

Wilson Smith, in a chapter of Filipová's collection, reads the success as the result of spectacle and broadly interpreted themes, a formula the Prince of Wales's patronage carried forward (Filipová 2015p. 206). Wilson Smith sets this against the dry earlier series of 1871 to 1874, which failed to excite public enthusiasm (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Hoffenberg argues that colonial officials and commissioners were not always in subordinate positions at the exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48). Hoffenberg describes the Ramsay-Day exchange as part of "a growing, direct, intercolonial network" of Indian and Australian museum representatives working without direct English oversight (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Hoffenberg's reading gives the colonies agency where Greenhalgh's sees colonies only as resources.

Hur argues that Chinese exhibits were run by the Imperial Maritime Customs, so that a Chinese display counted as proof of foreign competence and implicitly justified foreign service at the cost of China's tariff autonomy (Hurp. 16). Hur's reading gives foreign officials control where Hoffenberg gives colonial commissioners independence.

Rydell presents Goode's view that fairs and museums both had great educational value and could help create the good citizenship needed for advancing civilisation (Rydell 1984). Geppert places the South Kensington shows within a wider London pattern of specialised, often privately organised exhibitions from the 1880s (Geppert 2010p. 102).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

China

China was the first nation to appear at the London Fishery Exhibition of 1883, according to a Times article that Hur quotes: "To the surprise of everyone, China has been the first nation to put in an appearance." (Hurp. 16). Sir Robert Hart, Inspector-General of the Chinese Customs, prepared the collection on the instructions of the Chinese government. Hart undertook the preparation in China only in the September before, and James Duncan Campbell, Hart's representative in London, arranged its installation (Hurp. 16).

Hur argues that the Imperial Maritime Customs ran the work of world exhibitions, so a Chinese exhibit was read as proof of foreign competence. That implicitly justified the foreign service and encroached on China's tariff autonomy (Hurp. 16). Hur adds that the Qing government hardly led the initiative in organising China's participation (Hurp. 16). The Times also took the collection as further evidence of the efficiency of the European Customs Service as a department of the Chinese government (Hurp. 17).

Campbell, the Customs' non-resident secretary in London, claimed that the three London exhibitions of 1883 to 1885 helped remove English ignorance of China. In his words, they "did something to remove this ignorance" (Hurp. 17). Hur notes that more critical verdicts on China's displays at other exhibitions qualify these positive claims (Hurp. 17).

Japan

Japan appears in one text only. Hotta-Lister's appendix list of Japan's international exhibitions from 1873 to 1910 records participation in a London fishery exposition of 1883, with expenditure of 2,400 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The texts say nothing about what Japan showed, who organised the Japanese participation, or how it was received (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221).

India

Hoffenberg is the only text on India's presence, and it concerns exchanges rather than a display. E. P. Ramsay, the New South Wales museum administrator, used the 1883 show to build scientific and social links with Deputy Surgeon-General Francis Day, an Indian colleague (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Ramsay bought over 750 specimens of fish from India and the Indian Ocean and sent them to Sydney for permanent display (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). The texts name no Asian visitors to the 1883 show, and do not say who organised or paid for India's presence there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commodity shows — The texts treat the 1883 fisheries show as a commodity exhibition and the first of four South Kensington shows, closer to trade fairs than to cultural displays. (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008)
  • Spectacle and entertainment — The show's success came from outdoor attractions, night-time promenades and music, a formula carried into the shows that followed. (Filipová 2015; Hoffenberg 2001)
  • Empire as resource — Greenhalgh reads the sequence as treating colonies as commodities, with no regard for their peoples. (Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Colonial networks — Colonial commissioners and museum officials built exchanges outside direct English control, according to Hoffenberg. (Hoffenberg 2001; Hur)
  • Chinese presence and customs control — The Chinese collection is read as a display of foreign customs authority rather than of Chinese initiative. (Hur)
  • The Crown — The Queen did not open the 1883 show for reasons of health, and her public role returned with the 1886 ceremony. (Hoffenberg 2001; Filipová 2015)
  • Historiography — Geppert notes that the South Kensington shows have received little scholarly attention. (Geppert 2010)

Questions for study

  1. 1. Why did the 1883 show succeed where the 1871 to 1874 South Kensington series had failed, and how far did entertainment rather than trade explain that success? 2. Does the Indian and Chinese material support Greenhalgh's view that the sequence treated empire as a resource, or does it complicate it? 3. Hoffenberg finds colonial officials acting with some independence, while Hur finds Chinese displays controlled by foreign customs officers. What evidence would decide between these accounts of agency? 4. Greenhalgh finds little place for the fine arts in these shows. What does the 1883 seascape exhibition suggest about how organisers classified art? 5. If the Queen's absence was a matter of health, what does the Crown's role in the 1886 ceremony tell us about the political standing of the South Kensington shows? 6. Why have the South Kensington shows of 1883 to 1886 attracted so little scholarly study, and what would a fuller study of the fisheries show need to examine?

ch. 2, 'The Exhibition Wallahs' (pp. 44-48); ch. 3, 'Commissioners and the State' (p. 85); ch. 8, 'The Imperial Pilgrims' Progress' (pp. 250, 259)

Hoffenberg's study of colonial exhibitions treats the South Kensington shows of 1883-86 as places where Australian and Indian officials built their own networks, not only as imperial displays. For the 1883 Fisheries show, Hoffenberg describes E. P. Ramsay, New South Wales museum administrator, forming links with the Indian official Francis Day and buying over 750 fish specimens from India and the Indian Ocean for Sydney. Hoffenberg argues that such exchanges ran outside direct English control and show that colonial officials were not always subordinate at the exhibitions. The author also notes that Queen Victoria did not open the 1883 show because of poor health, and describes the early-1880s South Kensington shows as places of amusement and education that invited visitors to take part.

“a growing, direct, intercolonial network” p. 47
About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

Hur's dissertation on China's presence at world exhibitions shows that the Chinese collection at London's 1883 fisheries show was prepared under Robert Hart of the Imperial Maritime Customs, a service run by foreigners. Hur reports a Times article that called China the first nation to appear, and notes that Hart's London representative, James Duncan Campbell, arranged the installation. Hur sets the positive claims of Hart and Campbell against more critical verdicts on other displays, and notes that the Qing government hardly led its own participation.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.

Filipova's edited collection on exhibitions from 1840 to 1940 includes a chapter by Wilson Smith that explains how the 1883 International Fisheries Exhibition became a fashionable success. Wilson Smith credits Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protege and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, with taking a modest project and broadening it into an exhibition of things in general, with night-time promenades, fountains and musical entertainment. The chapter contrasts the dry earlier South Kensington series of 1871-74 with Cunliffe-Owen's more visitor-friendly approach. It presents the fair as the start of a programme of spectacle-led shows that the Prince of Wales took up under his patronage.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

Geppert's study of London's exhibition culture places the South Kensington shows of 1883-86 among the specialised, often privately organised exhibitions that London began to host from the 1880s. Its footnote says that the four South Kensington shows have attracted little historical study, and that only the Colonial and Indian show has found some, if limited, historiographical interest. Elsewhere the fisheries show appears in exhibition lists and in biographical notes on Cunliffe-Owen and Henry Wood.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
'Funding, Politics and Society' section (printed p. 33); 'The Fine Arts' section (printed p. 209 for l. 10876)

Greenhalgh's history of ephemeral exhibitions places the South Kensington shows of 1883-86 among commodity-based events that resembled giant conferences or trade fairs more than cultural spectacles. Greenhalgh argues that these shows examined commerce and industry, with entrance profits secondary, and reads the sequence as revealing British attitudes to empire, which treated colonies as resources and commodities and never as peoples. On the fine arts, Greenhalgh notes that the 1883 show included an exhibition of seascapes and art related to fish, and that the four shows as a whole reveal little enthusiasm for the fine arts.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Greenhalgh's book is a study of how international exhibitions from 1851 to 1940 gained legitimacy as a medium of national expression and kept it through a traumatic period. It concentrates on Britain, France and the United States, treating about thirty major events in detail, and argues that the exhibitions were saturated with liberal ideology and imperial display. After an introduction and a chapter on origins, chapters on funding, imperial display, human showcases (peoples exhibited in native villages), national profiles, design and architecture, women's exhibiting, and the fine arts are organised by theme. A short postscript on the surviving Crystal Palace at Sydenham closes the argument.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, concentrated in chapters 3 and 4, and mostly as objects of imperial display rather than as exhibitors. Chapter 3, Imperial display, centres on the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, where Japan's Colonial Palace showed Formosa, Korea and Manchuria and the Japanese army was shown, and on the Indochinese section at Paris 1889, the Dutch Indies at Paris 1900 and the Philippine displays at Buffalo 1901 and St Louis 1904. Chapter 4, Human showcases, covers the Tonkinese village and Cambodian elephant at the Paris expositions, the Kampong-Javanese village, a private undertaking subtitled 'Life in the Dutch Indies', the Chinese and Japanese teahouses and Javanese villagers at Chicago, and the St Louis Philippine villages, arranged by stage of civilisation from Negritos to Moros. Chapter 11, The prefabricated and the mass-produced, treats Japanese art as a source of Western design reform, shown from London 1862 onwards. Asia is a comparison within a wider empire story. India and Ceylon, outside this question, get far more space in the imperial and village chapters. Korea and Manchuria appear only in one quoted 1910 guide passage, and the Chinese figure mostly as crowds, villagers and tea-house staff. Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong and Sarawak appear mainly in lists of colonies at Wembley in 1924 and Glasgow in 1938.

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
printed pp. 43-44 (per the index); chapter not shown

Rydell's study of American expositions treats the Smithsonian Institution as a standing exhibition agency and names the London International Fisheries Exhibition of 1883 as one of the shows that received Smithsonian exhibits. Rydell notes that G. Brown Goode, the Smithsonian's assistant secretary, served as a federal commissioner at the London and Berlin fisheries shows. Rydell reports Goode's view that international fairs and museums both had great educational value and could help create the good citizenship needed for advancing civilization.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Chinese protests closed one concession at San Francisco, and Dosch reported that Asian exhibitors were discouraged from Portland by their treatment at Saint Louis. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

Badger, Reid. The Great American Fair: The World’s Columbian Exposition and American Culture. Chicago: Nelson-Hall, 1979.
printed p. 15 (running head)

Badger's history of American fairs names the 1883 London fisheries show only in passing, as one of the smaller specialised events to which American participation had become predictable by the 1880s, linked to growth in American fishing and agriculture.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Badger uses the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 as a test case for what the great world's fairs say about the societies that made them. He argues that the fairs expressed a Victorian faith in progress, order and culture, and that the White City's unity answered the fears of an unsettled age rather than describing American society. The book opens with a background of the London, Paris, Vienna and Philadelphia fairs and the American role in them. It then treats the Chicago fair in detail: its origins, organisation, site and design, finance and promotion, congresses, exhibits and Midway, and its legacy. Four parts and an epilogue carry the story to the fire and violence of 1894.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are slight in this book, and most of the material is in lists and quotations. Japan appears as an exhibitor and as a government. Chapter 3 (America and the Great World's Fairs) notes that Japanese displays at Philadelphia in 1876 showed how far America still had to go in the decorative arts. Chapter 9 (Finance and Promotion) says that of the fewer than twenty governments that had accepted by June 1891, only Germany, Turkey and Japan seemed enthusiastic. Chapter 14 (The Midway) lists Persian, Japanese and Indian bazaars, a Chinese village and a Dutch settlement with what the text calls South Sea island villages, without naming the islands. Wikipedia

The International Fisheries Exhibition was a Victorian era scientific, cultural, and animal exhibition open in South Kensington, London, United Kingdom, between May 12 and October 31, 1883. (The busiest day was May 15, when the official visitor count was over 25,000.) One of many world's fairs that took place in the second half of the nineteenth century, the exhibition was the largest special event held in the world to that point, attracting 2.6 million visitors, an average of 18,545 per day. The grounds of the exhibition encompassed 21 acres (0.085 km2) of the Royal Horticultural Society grounds in South Kensington, site of the 1862 International Exhibition. Many of the exhibitions on display were based upon the Buckland Museum of Economic Fish Culture, a private collection at South Kensington, and were expanded upon by exhibits from thirty-one countries and colonies.
