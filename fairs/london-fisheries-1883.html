International Fisheries Exhibition (London)

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyOn the web

The International Fisheries Exhibition was held at South Kensington, London, in 1883. It was the first of four annual themed international exhibitions on the same grounds, followed by health (1884), inventions (1885) and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition (1886) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). Philip Cunliffe-Owen turned a modest fisheries project into a fashionable success with entertainment and spectacle (Filipová 2015p. 206). Greenhalgh reads it as a commodity show at the start of a sequence that ends in imperial pageantry (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). The texts give the year and place but no opening or closing dates and no attendance figures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 93).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair followed an earlier South Kensington series, of 1871 to 1874, which had not succeeded. That series failed to excite public enthusiasm. Its projected run of ten annual exhibitions closed after four, with sparse audiences and a deficit of £150,000 (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Philip Cunliffe-Owen, Cole's protégé and successor as Director of the South Kensington Museum, took over an International Fisheries Exhibition scheduled for 1883 and transformed it (Filipová 2015p. 206). His work as a commissioner at the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876 and the Paris exposition of 1878 shaped a more visitor-friendly approach (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Greenhalgh places the fair among four commodity shows held at South Kensington between 1883 and 1886 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33). In Greenhalgh's account, all of these except the last had the feel of giant conferences or trade fairs, and their purpose was to examine commerce and industry, with entrance profits taking second place to national economy (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33).

Organisation and the site

Cunliffe-Owen directed the South Kensington exhibitions held in London from 1883 to 1886 (Geppert 2010p. 266). In those years, Cunliffe-Owen organised a different exhibition each year on various themes (Geppert 2010p. 102).

Queensland and New South Wales also took part through their agents. They asked their Agents-General in London to act as official representatives at the 1883 show, rather than incurring the "unnecessary expense" of appointing an additional official (Hoffenberg 2001p. 44).

The texts give no figures for the fair's cost or income (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). The site was the South Kensington grounds. The 1886 show used the same spaces as the earlier shows, temporary arcades built over 24 acres around what was left of the Royal Horticultural Society's gardens (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89). The Colonial and Indian Exhibition leased the arcades from the 1883 fisheries exhibition and added a few galleries (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95).

Exhibits and attractions

Cunliffe-Owen added outdoor attractions to the fisheries show: promenades under electric light, illuminated fountains, visual effects and musical entertainments (Filipová 2015p. 206). The fisheries theme was stretched to cover things in general (Filipová 2015p. 206).

In 1883 there was an exhibition of seascapes and of art generally related to fish, and painting and sculpture were fitted in where they could be found conceptually to fit (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209). An aquarium left over from the fisheries year was re-used at the 1886 show (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 89).

The texts describe the South Kensington shows of the early and mid-1880s as places of amusement and education. They stayed open at night, had shops, lit gardens and concerts, and invited visitors to take part (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250).

Nations and colonies on show

The texts name Queensland, New South Wales and South Australia among the colonial participants (Hoffenberg 2001p. 44; Hoffenberg 2001p. 85). South Australia's Agent-General sent a set of publications connected with the Fisheries Exhibition to Adelaide's Public Library, whose readers used them after the South Kensington shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 85).

The Smithsonian Institution sent exhibits to the London show (Rydell 1984). Badger notes that by the 1880s American participation in smaller specialised fisheries exhibits, such as those in Berlin in 1880 and London in 1883, had become predictable and even expected (Badger 1979p. 15).

Asian participation is set out in the section below.

Peoples on display and visitors

The texts record no displays of people at the 1883 show. The 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition had a special Native Compound for visiting indigenous craftsmen (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 95).

The Queen did not open the 1883 show. Poor health prevented her from officially opening it (Hoffenberg 2001p. 259). Hoffenberg notes that the Queen centred the state ceremony at Albert Hall for the Colonial and Indian Exhibition three years later (Hoffenberg 2001p. 259).

The texts describe the show as a fashionable success (Filipová 2015p. 206), and say that the Prince of Wales took up its popularity (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Aftermath and legacy

The Prince of Wales's patronage led to a programme of three further annual shows: Health in 1884, Inventions in 1885 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in 1886, all directed by Cunliffe-Owen (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Hoffenberg reports that Jules Joubert advised the organisers of Melbourne's Centennial Exhibition to follow the precedent of the South Kensington shows (Hoffenberg 2001p. 250). Ramsay's fish specimens from India and the Indian Ocean went on permanent display in Sydney (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47).

Geppert notes that, so far, only the last of the four South Kensington shows has found some, if limited, historiographical interest (Geppert 2010p. 305).

How historians have interpreted it

The texts agree on placing the fisheries show in the sequence of South Kensington shows of 1883 to 1886 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 33; Filipová 2015p. 206; Geppert 2010p. 305). They differ in what they make of it.

Greenhalgh argues that the fisheries, health and inventions shows were types of produce rather than cultures (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh reads the sequence as treating empire as a resource and commodity, and never as people with lives and traditions of their own (Greenhalgh 1988p. 62). Greenhalgh also reads the four shows as revealing a lack of enthusiasm for the fine arts (Greenhalgh 1988p. 209).

Wilson Smith, in a chapter of Filipová's collection, reads the success as the result of spectacle and broadly interpreted themes, a formula the Prince of Wales's patronage carried forward (Filipová 2015p. 206). Wilson Smith sets this against the dry earlier series of 1871 to 1874, which failed to excite public enthusiasm (Filipová 2015p. 206).

Hoffenberg argues that colonial officials and commissioners were not always in subordinate positions at the exhibitions (Hoffenberg 2001p. 48). Hoffenberg describes the Ramsay-Day exchange as part of "a growing, direct, intercolonial network" of Indian and Australian museum representatives working without direct English oversight (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Hoffenberg's reading gives the colonies agency where Greenhalgh's sees colonies only as resources.

Hur argues that Chinese exhibits were run by the Imperial Maritime Customs, so that a Chinese display counted as proof of foreign competence and implicitly justified foreign service at the cost of China's tariff autonomy (Hurp. 16). Hur's reading gives foreign officials control where Hoffenberg gives colonial commissioners independence.

Rydell presents Goode's view that fairs and museums both had great educational value and could help create the good citizenship needed for advancing civilisation (Rydell 1984). Geppert places the South Kensington shows within a wider London pattern of specialised, often privately organised exhibitions from the 1880s (Geppert 2010p. 102).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

China

China was the first nation to appear at the London Fishery Exhibition of 1883, according to a Times article that Hur quotes: "To the surprise of everyone, China has been the first nation to put in an appearance." (Hurp. 16). Sir Robert Hart, Inspector-General of the Chinese Customs, prepared the collection on the instructions of the Chinese government. Hart undertook the preparation in China only in the September before, and James Duncan Campbell, Hart's representative in London, arranged its installation (Hurp. 16).

Hur argues that the Imperial Maritime Customs ran the work of world exhibitions, so a Chinese exhibit was read as proof of foreign competence. That implicitly justified the foreign service and encroached on China's tariff autonomy (Hurp. 16). Hur adds that the Qing government hardly led the initiative in organising China's participation (Hurp. 16). The Times also took the collection as further evidence of the efficiency of the European Customs Service as a department of the Chinese government (Hurp. 17).

Campbell, the Customs' non-resident secretary in London, claimed that the three London exhibitions of 1883 to 1885 helped remove English ignorance of China. In his words, they "did something to remove this ignorance" (Hurp. 17). Hur notes that more critical verdicts on China's displays at other exhibitions qualify these positive claims (Hurp. 17).

Japan

Japan appears in one text only. Hotta-Lister's appendix list of Japan's international exhibitions from 1873 to 1910 records participation in a London fishery exposition of 1883, with expenditure of 2,400 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). The texts say nothing about what Japan showed, who organised the Japanese participation, or how it was received (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221).

India

Hoffenberg is the only text on India's presence, and it concerns exchanges rather than a display. E. P. Ramsay, the New South Wales museum administrator, used the 1883 show to build scientific and social links with Deputy Surgeon-General Francis Day, an Indian colleague (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). Ramsay bought over 750 specimens of fish from India and the Indian Ocean and sent them to Sydney for permanent display (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47). The texts name no Asian visitors to the 1883 show, and do not say who organised or paid for India's presence there (Hoffenberg 2001p. 47).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Commodity shows — The texts treat the 1883 fisheries show as a commodity exhibition and the first of four South Kensington shows, closer to trade fairs than to cultural displays. (Greenhalgh 1988; Findling & Pelle 2008)
  • Spectacle and entertainment — The show's success came from outdoor attractions, night-time promenades and music, a formula carried into the shows that followed. (Filipová 2015; Hoffenberg 2001)
  • Empire as resource — Greenhalgh reads the sequence as treating colonies as commodities, with no regard for their peoples. (Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Colonial networks — Colonial commissioners and museum officials built exchanges outside direct English control, according to Hoffenberg. (Hoffenberg 2001; Hur)
  • Chinese presence and customs control — The Chinese collection is read as a display of foreign customs authority rather than of Chinese initiative. (Hur)
  • The Crown — The Queen did not open the 1883 show for reasons of health, and her public role returned with the 1886 ceremony. (Hoffenberg 2001; Filipová 2015)
  • Historiography — Geppert notes that the South Kensington shows have received little scholarly attention. (Geppert 2010)

Questions for study

  1. 1. Why did the 1883 show succeed where the 1871 to 1874 South Kensington series had failed, and how far did entertainment rather than trade explain that success? 2. Does the Indian and Chinese material support Greenhalgh's view that the sequence treated empire as a resource, or does it complicate it? 3. Hoffenberg finds colonial officials acting with some independence, while Hur finds Chinese displays controlled by foreign customs officers. What evidence would decide between these accounts of agency? 4. Greenhalgh finds little place for the fine arts in these shows. What does the 1883 seascape exhibition suggest about how organisers classified art? 5. If the Queen's absence was a matter of health, what does the Crown's role in the 1886 ceremony tell us about the political standing of the South Kensington shows? 6. Why have the South Kensington shows of 1883 to 1886 attracted so little scholarly study, and what would a fuller study of the fisheries show need to examine?

On the web

Wikipedia

The International Fisheries Exhibition was a Victorian era scientific, cultural, and animal exhibition open in South Kensington, London, United Kingdom, between May 12 and October 31, 1883. (The busiest day was May 15, when the official visitor count was over 25,000.) One of many world's fairs that took place in the second half of the nineteenth century, the exhibition was the largest special event held in the world to that point, attracting 2.6 million visitors, an average of 18,545 per day. The grounds of the exhibition encompassed 21 acres (0.085 km2) of the Royal Horticultural Society grounds in South Kensington, site of the 1862 International Exhibition. Many of the exhibitions on display were based upon the Buckland Museum of Economic Fish Culture, a private collection at South Kensington, and were expanded upon by exhibits from thirty-one countries and colonies.
