Sixth Regular Domestic Exhibition (Japan)

OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

A sixth regular domestic exhibition was planned for 1907 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The authorities then preferred a grand international exhibition in Tokyo, the Grand Japanese Exhibition of 1912, which the government originated and Count Kaneko Kentaro chaired (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). That scheme was postponed to 1917 and later abandoned (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 131). Two other texts describe a Tokyo Industrial Exhibition of 1907 that took place and included a display of Koreans. The texts do not say whether it was the planned sixth domestic exhibition (Kwonp. 24; Henry 2014p. 96).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The sixth domestic exhibition belonged to a series of government national exhibitions. The government organised five large ones before 1910, administered by the Ministry of Home Affairs (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). The first three were held at Ueno Park in Tokyo in 1877, 1882 and 1890, the fourth at Kyoto in 1895 and the fifth at Osaka in 1903 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219). The sixth was due in 1907 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). The text says the authorities "considered it appropriate that it should be on a grand international scale instead" (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217), particularly in view of the recent victory over Russia (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).

Organisers and plans for 1912

The Grand Japanese Exhibition plan originated within the Japanese government and was to include foreign governments and peoples (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). In April 1907 it was decided that it would run from 1 April to 31 October 1912 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). The budget was 20 million yen and the site was 250 acres in Tokyo (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217). In June 1907 Komura, ambassador in London, invited Britain to send exhibits (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41). Kaneko Kentaro was elected chairman of the Grand Japanese Exhibition Committee in August 1907 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42).

The Tokyo Industrial Exhibition of 1907

Kwon describes the Crystal Pavilion of the 1907 Tokyo Industrial Exhibition (Kwonp. 24). Its three stages were a dark cave, a Crystal Palace of mirror illusions and a final room with two Korean people seated in traditional dress (Kwonp. 24). Japanese merchants built the pavilion beside a Korea hall that had few visitors (Kwonp. 24). Henry lists a Tokyo display of 1907 among the events that late Chosŏn leaders joined, but gives no further detail (Henry 2014p. 96). Neither text connects this exhibition with the 1912 plan.

Visitors and reception

Kwon writes that the Tokyo asahi and Yomiuri described the Koreans as coming to the exposition of their own free will (Kwonp. 25). Korean students in Tokyo told the pavilion manager of their painful feelings, because displaying a Korean woman went against Korean custom (Kwonp. 25). They protested several times, and Korean media printed critical articles (Kwonp. 27). The Yomiuri took a different view, reading the display as "an attempt at women’s liberation to enable women to transcend their lives of seclusion" (Kwonp. 31). Kwon says the display was removed after the protests (Kwonp. 27).

The colonial setting

Kwon links the display to the crisis of 1905 to 1907. The Korean emperor sent envoys to The Hague at the time the Koreans were shown (Kwonp. 37). The emperor was dethroned in July 1907 and the army was disbanded ten days later (Kwonp. 37). Korean reactions were characterised by concern over national disgrace (Kwonp. 36).

Politics and why the 1912 plan was postponed

An announcement early in 1908 postponed the Grand Exhibition of 1912 to 1917 (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). The text gives the reason as the country's financial situation (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42). In 1910 the 1917 plan was still current, and some of its committee members worked on the London exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188). Furuichi Koi had studied European facilities for the 1917 exhibition, which was later abandoned (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 131).

Its place in the series

Kwon describes the 1903 Osaka Anthropological Pavilion as the earlier display of Koreans at a national exposition, and both displays were withdrawn after protests (Kwonp. 19; Kwonp. 27). Hotta-Lister counts five government domestic exhibitions before 1910, the last at Osaka in 1903, which leaves no place for the planned sixth (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 219).

How historians have interpreted it

Kwon reads the 1907 display as a freak show that combined public education with entertainment and the spread of a culture of consumption (Kwonp. 27). Hotta-Lister treats the 1912 plan as tied to Japan's diplomacy with Britain, and says that Komura also hoped the London exhibition would advertise the 1917 scheme (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 43; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 188).

Asian participants and visitors

Participants, exhibits, people on display and visitors from other parts of Asia and from colonies, as our texts describe them.

Koreans on display

Kwon's sources say the Korean man was hired by a Japanese (Kwonp. 24). The woman was from Taegu and in her early twenties, but sources differ on her name (Kwonp. 25). The man was introduced as Interpreter Pak and also explained the woman to the crowds (Kwonp. 25; Kwonp. 25). Korean newspapers described the Koreans as exhibited like animals (Kwonp. 26).

Chinese students and Taiwanese women

Hotta-Lister records that Kato, writing to Komura in November 1909, cited a recent domestic exhibition in Tokyo where Chinese students in Japan had protested the exposure of Taiwanese women (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 180). The text does not date that exhibition in this passage. It may be the same event as the Korean display at the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition, but the texts do not say so.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Planned replacement — Hotta-Lister says the 1907 plan gave way to the 1912 scheme for an international exhibition (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217).
  • Entertainment and display — Kwon calls the 1907 Crystal Pavilion a freak show that mixed education with entertainment (Kwonp. 27).
  • Korean nationalism — Kwon links Korean reactions to the 1905 treaty and the 1907 dethronement (Kwonp. 36; Kwonp. 37).
  • Finance and diplomacy — the 1912 plan's budget, the Komura letters and the postponement are set against Japan's finances and relations with Britain (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 41; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42).
  • Separate Seoul exhibition — Henry's Keijo Exhibition of 1907 in Seoul is a different event, a small, Japanese-dominated market (Henry 2014p. 96).

Questions for study

  1. Was the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition of 1907 the sixth regular domestic exhibition that Hotta-Lister says was set aside, and what evidence would settle it (Kwonp. 24; Hotta-Lister 1999p. 217)?
  2. Why did Kwon find Korean reactions in 1907 angrier than in 1903 (Kwonp. 36)?
  3. Hotta-Lister gives financial difficulty as the reason for the postponement. What other factors might explain the decision to postpone (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 42)?
  4. The Keijo Exhibition opened in Seoul on 1 September 1907, months after the Tokyo event. What did the Japanese organisers hope to achieve in Seoul that they could not in Tokyo (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 93)?

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

Kwon, Hyeokhui. “An Analysis of Korean Intellectual Responses to the Exhibition of Koreans at Japanese Expositions: Nationalism and the Discourse on Northeast Asian Solidarity at the Turn of the Century.” Journal article.
Introductionp. 19Asia

Kwon introduces the human displays at Japanese expositions, which combined social Darwinist evolutionary schemes with freak-show entertainment and colonial ideology. Kwon argues that earlier work on the 1903 Anthropological Pavilion in Osaka has focused on the Japanese organisers and overlooked Korean responses, as well as the 1907 Crystal Pavilion in Tokyo. The article traces how Korean intellectuals moved from hope in Northeast Asian solidarity with Japan to nationalist resistance, using newspapers and magazines of the period.

In the contents of this work

The 1907 Crystal Pavilion and Its Representation of Koreansp. 24Asia

Kwon turns to the 1907 Crystal Pavilion at the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition, which Japanese merchants built beside a small Korea hall to draw visitors. It was a fantasy and terror attraction with a dark cave, mirror illusions and a final room where a Korean man and woman sat in traditional dress, and no anthropologists took part. Kwon reconstructs the pair's identities from competing Japanese and Korean newspapers, which disagree over names, origins and whether the woman had come willingly. A table sets out how the display was described, and Korean newspapers likened it to the exhibition of animals. Kwon concludes that the Crystal Pavilion was both educational and commercial, like the 1903 display, and that both were withdrawn after Korean protests stirred ethnic feeling against Japan.

The Anthropological Pavilion and Korean Responsesp. 27Asia

Kwon's longest section follows the protests that brought about the withdrawal of the 1903 Korean display. Japanese officials, worried about relations with Korea and China, ordered the display removed after a protest by three Koreans living in Japan, who appealed to shared race and culture. Kwon notes that these protesters were pro-Japanese bureaucrats, and sets their stance beside the Chinese students' fierce objections to a Chinese display cancelled before it opened. Turning to 1907, Kwon argues that Korean newspapers and the T'aegŭk hakpo responded with far more emotion, condemning the display as racist and as national shame. The Japanese Yomiuri, by contrast, framed the affair as women's liberation and downplayed discrimination. The section ends by linking these reactions to the nationalism of the Patriotic Enlightenment movement after the 1905 Protectorate Treaty.

In the contents of this work

The Transformation in Korean People’s Perceptions of the Display of Koreansp. 34Asia

Kwon's penultimate section traces how Korean intellectuals' views of the displays changed between 1903 and 1907. Kwon places both reactions within social Darwinism and the idea of a racial struggle that Japanese politicians such as Itō Hirobumi promoted, and argues that elites at first hoped Japan would lead a yellow-race solidarity against the West. The 1903 protest grew from that hope, Kwon suggests, but the Russo-Japanese War and the 1905 Protectorate Treaty weakened it. Quoting the diaries of Yun Ch'i-ho and the editorials of Chang Chi-yŏn, Kwon shows that the 1907 reaction was angrier and more nationalist as Japan took control of the Korean state. The section notes that the display coincided with the emperor's envoys to The Hague and the later dethronement of the emperor. It concludes that Korean intellectuals turned from solidarity to nationalism.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Kwon examines Korean intellectual responses to two Japanese exhibitions that displayed Koreans: the Anthropological Pavilion at the Fifth National Industrial Exposition in Osaka in 1903 and the Crystal Pavilion of the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition in 1907. The article argues that the human displays combined social Darwinist classification with commercial entertainment, and that Korean responses, traced in newspapers and magazines of the period, moved from hope in Northeast Asian solidarity under a common race and culture to nationalist resistance once Japan imposed the 1905 Protectorate Treaty. It is organised as an introduction, a section on each exhibition and its Korean reactions, a comparative section on changing perceptions, and a conclusion.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole setting, though the article treats only Korea, Japan and China within it. Korea is central: the 1903 Anthropological Pavilion (Osaka) and the 1907 Crystal Pavilion (Tokyo) are read as Japanese displays of Koreans, and chapters 2 to 5 concentrate on the Korean press (Taehan maeil sinbo, T'aegŭk hakpo, Mansebo) and on intellectuals such as Yun Ch'i-ho and Chang Chi-yŏn. Japan is the organiser and the imperial power at issue; the Ainu, Ryukyuans and Taiwanese seiban shown in 1903 appear as its internal and colonial subjects. China appears in chapter 4 through the Chinese display cancelled before opening after protests by students in Japan, which the Korean protests are set beside. Southeast Asia is marginal. Javanese are named in the 1903 prospectus and in the Chinese protest, where they are called among 'the world's lowliest races', but nothing further is said of them. Manchuria appears only as a site of Japanese overseas research, and the Indians in the 1903 display serve as a South Asian contrast. The article does not discuss exhibitions held elsewhere in Asia or Asian visitors beyond the Korean and Chinese students in Japan.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hotta-Lister studies the Japan-British Exhibition, held at the White City in London from May to October 1910. Its argument is that the Japanese government treated the exhibition as a national undertaking, meant to correct British views of Japan and to serve political and economic aims. The book covers the background from 1905 to 1910, the preparations in Japan and London, the sponsors' objectives, the opening months, British and Japanese reactions, and the aftermath in trade, loans and diplomacy. Its final chapter judges the exhibition on balance a success and compares it with the 1981-82 Great Japan Exhibition and the 1991 Japan Festival. Appendices list Japan's exhibition participation from 1873 to 1910 and the main exhibits.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole book, so its coverage is central. Japan is sponsor, exhibitor and object of display, and state participation runs through every chapter: the commissioners, the Exhibitors Association, the 1.8 million yen budget, the Palace of the Orient and the Japanese Village. Korea and Manchuria appear mainly as politics: in chapter 1, Background of the Time, on the open door dispute and the annexation of Korea, and in chapter 3, Exhibition Objectives, which sets the Palace of the Orient, with its Korean and Formosan displays, beside the colonial sections of the 1908 Franco-British Exhibition. Chapter 5, Reactions, and chapter 6, Aftermath, treat the Ainu and Formosan displays and the Chinese displays as embarrassments to Japanese residents. China also enters through the Osaka domestic exhibition of 1903 and Chinese student protests against it. Southeast Asia is slight. Indochina appears as a French possession in a list of annexations cited for Korea (chapter 3) and as a pavilion at the 1908 exhibition (chapter 5). The Philippines are mentioned in the 1905 Taft agreement (chapter 1) and as Filipino natives at St Louis in 1904 (chapter 5). Singapore is one of the new trade posts of 1910 (chapter 1), and chapter 7, Some Concluding Reflections, cites Japanese wartime atrocities in Thailand and Burma. The Dutch East Indies appear only as a participant at Osaka in 1903 (Appendix A). Siam, Malaya and Borneo are not discussed.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.
