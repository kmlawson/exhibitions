Proposed Chinese agricultural exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliography

A Chinese agricultural exhibition was proposed in 1903. The texts record only the proposal: a Hong Kong Chinese newspaper argued in May 1903 that China should hold an agriculture exhibition, citing Japan's domestic produce exhibitions (Hurp. 83). The texts name no organiser, date or venue, and they do not say whether the exhibition was held or abandoned (Hurp. 83). It matters chiefly as evidence of the Chinese press's rivalry with Japan and of its wish for China to hold exhibitions of its own (Hurp. 83).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

In 1903 the Hong Kong huazi ribao claimed that China should hold an agriculture exhibition (Hurp. 83). Hur dates the article to 11 May 1903 in a note (Hurp. 83). The argument rested on the view that nations could not survive in a world of evolution without competition, which Hur reads as the influence of Social Darwinism among Chinese elites (Hurp. 83). The article also set China against Japan: because Japan had held domestic produce exhibitions, it would be regrettable if China never had one (Hurp. 83).

Earlier and parallel calls for Chinese exhibitions

Hur places the proposal within a longer argument for exhibitions in China. In the 1890s Zheng Guanying expected a national exhibition after ten years of smaller local ones (Hurp. 31). Chen Chi attributed Japan's growth to its exhibitions and asked why China was laughed at by Japan (Hurp. 32). In 1898 Pan Shengnian submitted a memorial proposing an exhibition in Beijing, but it was sent a week before the coup that ended the Hundred Days' Reforms and received no consideration (Hurp. 33). Hur's note calls Pan's proposal a national exhibition (Hurp. 83). The texts do not connect Pan's memorial with the agricultural article.

Press debate after Osaka

In April 1903 Chen Jingru's ode in Zhongwai ribao said that China must have its own exhibitions, and that the court was aware of this need but no one had proposed it (Hurp. 82p. 83). Hur says that the Chinese press drew its case for a national exhibition from the unpleasant incidents at the Osaka exhibition of 1903 (Hurp. 84). Zhongwai ribao also made practical suggestions, among them systematic reporting of exhibit items and the costs of shipping, insurance and maritime customs (Hurp. 83). These concerned exhibitions in general, not agriculture.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Social Darwinism and competition — the article justified an agriculture exhibition by the need for competition between nations (Hurp. 83)
  • Rivalry with Japan — Japan's domestic produce exhibitions were the model and the spur for China (Hurp. 83)
  • Calls for a Chinese national exhibition — the agriculture proposal sits among other 1903 calls for China to hold its own exhibitions (Hurp. 83p. 84)
  • Earlier reformers — Zheng Guanying and Chen Chi argued for Chinese exhibitions in the 1890s (Hurp. 31p. 32)

Questions for study

  1. The texts record a proposal but not its outcome. What other evidence would show whether an agricultural exhibition was held in China in 1903 or later?
  2. Why did a Hong Kong newspaper choose Japan's domestic produce exhibitions as its model, and how does that comparison fit the rivalry Hur describes? (Hurp. 83)
  3. How does the Social Darwinian language of competition change the case for exhibitions, compared with Zheng Guanying's economic argument? (Hurp. 83p. 31)
  4. Hur links the 1903 calls to the Osaka incidents. Does the agriculture article support that link, given that the passage does not mention Osaka? (Hurp. 83p. 84)

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
15International Ambitions of an Exhibition at the Margin: Japan’s 1903 Osaka ExpositionJeffer Daykinp. 333Asia

Daykin asks what it meant for Japan to host an international exposition in 1903, using Osaka's Fifth National Industrial Exhibition in Tennoji Park as the case. The event was planned as a domestic show and became international only when foreign applications far exceeded the space reserved for them, so a Foreign Samples Building and separate national pavilions had to be provided. Daykin uses the Japan Weekly Chronicle, foreign-language guides and the commissioners' introductions to show how the awards policy and language arrangements lagged behind the event. His central example is the Jinrui Kan, a live 'native peoples' concession outside the grounds, which was altered after protests from Chinese and Korean students and officials and from the Okinawan press. The Formosan section, by contrast, presented Taiwan as a colonial foil that made Japan the civilised spectator. Daykin concludes that Osaka was a marginal international exposition, one that asserted Japan's equality with the West just two years before the Russo-Japanese War.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
AbstractAsia

Hur's abstract summarises a dissertation on the Qing government's attempt to stage modern statehood at world exhibitions and how that attempt was undermined. The Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan and Chinese reformers each shaped China's image, and criticism of the Qing grew after Osaka in 1903 and St Louis in 1904. It ends with the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, which Chinese intellectuals came to support once the government promised constitutional reform.

Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

TwoChina, the “Barbarian” Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903p. 47Asia

Hur examines the Fifth Domestic Encouragement of Industry Exhibition, held in Osaka in 1903, which Japan staged as a semi-international show and a rehearsal for a future world exposition. The chapter first shows how the Meiji government courted Chinese officials and gentry with invitations and complimentary tickets, and how Japanese newspapers cast Japan as a model for Chinese reform. Its main case is the Jinruikan, an entertainment building beside a zoo that planned to display 'native' peoples, including Chinese. Hur follows the protests of Chinese students and merchants in Japan, the involvement of the Chinese minister Cai Jun, and the coverage in Chinese newspapers, then turns to the Taiwan Pavilion and a Taiwanese woman mistaken for a Chinese exhibit. The chapter concludes that Zaizhen's imperial commission made Osaka China's first meaningful step into official overseas exhibiting, but that press distrust of the court was already visible in these disputes.

Kwon, Hyeokhui. “An Analysis of Korean Intellectual Responses to the Exhibition of Koreans at Japanese Expositions: Nationalism and the Discourse on Northeast Asian Solidarity at the Turn of the Century.” Journal article.
The Anthropological Pavilion and Korean Responsesp. 27Asia

Kwon's longest section follows the protests that brought about the withdrawal of the 1903 Korean display. Japanese officials, worried about relations with Korea and China, ordered the display removed after a protest by three Koreans living in Japan, who appealed to shared race and culture. Kwon notes that these protesters were pro-Japanese bureaucrats, and sets their stance beside the Chinese students' fierce objections to a Chinese display cancelled before it opened. Turning to 1907, Kwon argues that Korean newspapers and the T'aegŭk hakpo responded with far more emotion, condemning the display as racist and as national shame. The Japanese Yomiuri, by contrast, framed the affair as women's liberation and downplayed discrimination. The section ends by linking these reactions to the nationalism of the Patriotic Enlightenment movement after the 1905 Protectorate Treaty.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. The work has an introduction, four chapters running from China's participation before 1903 to the 1910 fair, and a conclusion arguing that the Chinese case does not fit Western theories of exhibitions as hegemonic propaganda or as a stage for the colonized to resist.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the subject of the whole work, which asks how China presented itself at world exhibitions. Japan and Taiwan are active rivals rather than background: the Introduction casts Japan as using the fairs to present itself as Asia’s only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan, and Chapter One compares China with Japan from Philadelphia in 1876 onwards. The densest material is in Chapter Two, “China, the ‘Barbarian’ Guest of Honor at the Osaka Exhibition of 1903”, on the Jinruikan, which planned to display Chinese and other ‘native’ peoples, and on the Taiwan Pavilion; and in Chapter Three, “Recurrence of Trauma at the St. Louis Exposition of 1904”, on a Chinese Village where a bound-foot woman was displayed and on the Japanese section’s wooden map that included Chinese and Korean coasts. Southeast Asia is marginal. Chapter Four, on the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing in 1910, mentions overseas Chinese merchants from Southeast Asia, such as Zhang Zhenxun, and large Japanese visiting groups, and a footnote states that “Nanyang” does not mean Southeast Asia. The Philippine Reservation at St. Louis appears only in a footnote, and Siam, Indochina and the Dutch East Indies are not discussed.
