Paris · 1878

Paris Universal Exposition

Exposition universelle de 1878
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Exposition universelle de 1878 opened in Paris on 1 May 1878 and ran for about six and a half months on the Champ de Mars and the Trocadéro hill across the Seine (Findling & Pelle 2008; Benedict 1983; Allwood et al. 2001). The Third Republic staged it to show that France had recovered from defeat by Prussia and from the Paris Commune of 1871 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Mandell 1967). It ran at a heavy loss. Historians value it for its Rue des Nations of national pavilions, its first colonial sections, and the exhibition fatigue that followed, the complaint that fairs had grown too large and repetitive (Findling & Pelle 2008; Geppert 2010; Roche 2000; Hale 2008).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The government decreed the fair in March 1876, leaving little more than two years to prepare, against a background of defeat by Prussia, about five billion francs of indemnities and the Commune (Findling & Pelle 2008). Mandell says the deputies had to produce something grander than the Second Empire's exhibition of 1867, and that the fair was meant to calm the political crisis of 1877 (Mandell 1967). Chandler reads it as a republican answer to 1867 and a proclamation that France was ready again to play its civilising role (Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood, Allan and Reid class it among exhibitions held to restore a nation's pride (Allwood et al. 2001). ## Organisation and finance

Jean-Baptiste Krantz was commissioner general, Leopold Amedee Hardy designed the main Palais d'Industrie, and Georges Berger ran the foreign sections (Findling & Pelle 2008). The fair had 52,835 exhibitors and 29,810 awards (Findling & Pelle 2008; Badger 1979).

The texts disagree about the loss. Roeber's entry gives at least 28 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008). The reference work's statistics table gives about 31 million (Findling & Pelle 2008). Allwood, Allan and Reid give nearly 32 million (Allwood et al. 2001), and Mandell's footnote gives 38 million (Mandell 1967). Mandell says less than half the cost was recovered, and the deficit was paid from the national budget. The fair was "too serious to entrust to private hands", unlike the 1867 fair, which was partly financed by a private guarantee corporation (Mandell 1967). Roeber still judges the fair a success from the French point of view (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The loss had lasting effects. The deficit led the deputies to insist that private banks bear part of the risk for 1889 (Mandell 1967). In 1889 the State, the city and banks funded the fair, and the banks recouped through admission-ticket books sold as lottery tickets (Greenhalgh 1988). Congresses were a new feature: thirty-two were held, and Victor Hugo presided over the one on literary property, which Roeber links to later international copyright law (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Site and architecture

The site grew from 170 to 185 acres and reached the Trocadéro hill across the Seine (Findling & Pelle 2008). Other texts give 192 acres (Allwood et al. 2001; Benedict 1983), and Badger's appendix gives 66 acres (Badger 1979). The texts do not settle the figure.

The Palais d'Industrie had twenty giant ventilation pipes and four fans that kept its interior about 8 degrees Celsius cooler than the outside air (Allwood et al. 2001). Behind it, a wooden wall about ten feet high cut the site off from the city, and annexes held steam engines and eight chimneys (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Four hydraulic pumps raised water from the Seine through 23 miles of pipe to the fountains, the aquarium and the Trocadéro towers, which Chandler calls the triumph of French engineering (Findling & Pelle 1990).

The Palais du Trocadéro, by Gabriel Davioud and Jules Bourdais, came to stand for the fair itself (Benedict 1983; Findling & Pelle 2008). The texts disagree about its merit. Chandler calls its mixed style an unqualified failure, though its colonnades impressed (Findling & Pelle 1990). Çelik says contemporaries called its style Byzantine, Romano-Spanish-Moorish or modern (Çelik 1992). It was unfinished on opening day, and its music festival had to wait until June (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Georges Berger's Rue des Nations gave each nation an entrance to its section in the central courtyard (Findling & Pelle 2008). Chandler calls it the most innovative and most widely admired feature of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990). Della Coletta treats it as the prototype of the national street, in which each nation expressed its individuality within a shared civilising progress (Della Coletta 2006).

Exhibits and attractions

Machines drew crowds. Chandler lists the typewriter, rubber tyres and American personal appliances, and Edison's megaphone and phonograph (Findling & Pelle 1990). Roche's table of innovations adds the internal combustion engine and refrigeration (Roche 2000). Bartholdi's statue Liberty Enlightening the World stood in the grounds as a head, and visitors climbed inside it by the hundreds (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

Art caused friction. The fine-arts selection left out Millet, admitted one Courbet landscape, and excluded the Impressionists as a group (Greenhalgh 1988). Greenhalgh reads this as a rift between the official Beaux-Arts and the avant-garde (Greenhalgh 1988). The committee refused battle scenes from the Franco-Prussian War, so young military painters showed at the Goupil Gallery, which German visitors went to see (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Education was a second strand. Fuchs argues that the fair helped found the Musee Pedagogique in 1879, whose first collection came from the fair (Lawn 2009). The Spanish pavilion, in neo-Mudejar style, won a gold medal (Lawn 2009). Counts of its objects differ: 4,131 in the British catalogue, 4,537 in the Austrian and 6,672 by the Spanish commissioner Santos (Lawn 2009).

Nations, colonies and peoples on show

The colonial displays are the fair's most important innovation for the texts. Hale dates to 1878 the start of a period in which the colonial section became a distinct and growing part of French fairs (Hale 2008). Hale's verdict is that "In summary, the 1878 Paris exposition inaugurated a period of innovation for colonial expositions." (Hale 2008) The first colonial section was a walled-off part of an exhibit building, with its own greenhouse (Hale 2008).

Algeria built a palace with Moorish and Oriental decoration, and Cochinchina had its own Annamite pavilion (Hale 2008). The texts give different dimensions for the Algerian palace: 35 by 55 metres in Hale (Hale 2008), and 40 by 50 metres with four towers in Çelik, whose interior caravanserai (an inn for travellers) showed the products of about two thousand exhibitors (Çelik 1992). Eleven Algerian tirailleurs (infantry) and eleven spahis (cavalry) guarded the Algerian exhibits, although the Paris Algerian commission had objected (Hale 2008).

Egypt had one pavilion, a Pharaonic house by Mariette-Bey (Çelik 1992). Greenhalgh describes a modest Cairo street of shops and a bazaar, which grew much larger in 1889 (Greenhalgh 1988). Qureshi notes a Rue de Caire staffed by many North Africans (Qureshi 2011). Greenhalgh treats the Cairo street and the Algerian bazaar as the first step towards the peopled colonial displays of 1889 (Greenhalgh 1988). Japan's delegation was led by Matsukata Masayoshi, who had a large map of Asia made to correct European ignorance of Japan (Hotta-Lister 1999). The Chinese exhibit was entrusted to the Imperial Maritime Customs, a service dominated by foreigners, and Hur argues that this, with foreign expansion and the Boxer Uprising, left the Qing government little attention for the fairs (Hur). Zeng Jize, who visited the site, judged the Chinese exhibit: "The Chinese exhibit lacks its core." (Hur)

India and Australia were represented through colonial commissioners. Hoffenberg says a representative group of commissioners, including Lord Lytton and Indian princes, produced the Indian displays (Hoffenberg 2001). Australian wines won a gold medal (Hoffenberg 2001).

Peoples were displayed as well as objects. Geppert says the first distinct native villages were added to the Paris exhibition of 1878 (Geppert 2010). Greenhalgh argues that the peopled displays at the Jardin d'Acclimatation were partly inspired by plans to bring foreign peoples to the fair (Greenhalgh 1988). Roche says that in 1878 and 1889 the form changed to self-contained exotic cities (Roche 2000). Çelik counts Morocco, Tunisia and Persia as the only Muslim countries on the Rue des Nations (Çelik 1992). Çelik quotes Hautecoeur: "Many nations trusted French architects to make their countries known." (Çelik 1992)

Visitors, reception and politics

The texts agree on roughly 16 million visitors but not on detail. Roeber gives more than 16 million (Findling & Pelle 2008), Mandell gives 16 million, more than twice the 1867 figure (Mandell 1967), and Badger gives 16,032,725 (Badger 1979). Roche's table gives 6.0 million without explanation (Roche 2000). Geppert cites an estimate of 571,792 people who came to Paris during the exposition, a city figure and not fair admissions (Geppert 2010). Chandler records 258,342 paid admissions in the first nine days of 1878, against 38,363 in the first nine days of 1867 (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Visitors found the fair exhausting. Sala, whose account Allwood, Allan and Reid use, complained of crowds, commercialised restaurants and the lack of cheap food (Allwood et al. 2001). Max Eyth, who ran a stand, asked why each generation needed its own exhibition, and called the fair a dazzling bubble of soap (Geppert 2010). Hoyer calculated that visiting 53,000 exhibitors for a minute each would take 53,000 minutes (Geppert 2010). Politics marked the fair. Most European monarchies boycotted it, though Kaiser argues that the German boycott did not decide their policy (Kaiser). Geppert gives Germany's abstention a political cause, the Franco-Prussian War and the clash of monarchism and republicanism (Geppert 2010). Kaiser notes a small German exhibition of paintings, and Allwood, Allan and Reid say some German painters sent work to the fine-arts section (Kaiser; Allwood et al. 2001). Kaiser reads the fair as France presenting itself, against a militarily stronger Germany, as the more educated and politically advanced nation (Kaiser). The Prince of Wales attended, and his visit was read in France as support for the new Republic (Kaiser). Many parish priests told their congregations to stay away from the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Aftermath and legacy

The deficit shaped later funding, as shown above (Greenhalgh 1988; Mandell 1967). Greenhalgh counts the Trocadéro among the permanent buildings that offset the fair's losses (Greenhalgh 1988). Hale says that after 1878 France acquired protectorates over Tunisia (1881), Tonkin (1884) and Madagascar (1885), and that overseas-expansion advocates won the republican debate after the Franco-Prussian War (Hale 2008).

Roche records Pierre de Coubertin attending the fair as a teenager (Roche 2000). MacAloon argues that the 1878 and 1889 fairs shaped Coubertin's thinking before the first modern Olympic Games of 1896, and Roche says Coubertin's trust in the fair tradition led the Olympic movement to tie its next games to the Paris exposition of 1900 (Roche 2000).

How historians have interpreted it

Most of the texts agree that the fair was a state project of recovery and legitimacy. Roeber presents it as the Third Republic's attempt to restore continuity and legitimacy, and as a shift from industry towards culture (Findling & Pelle 2008). Roche quotes Hobsbawm to argue that expos gave the Republic the legitimacy of prosperity, progress and colonial conquest (Roche 2000). Della Coletta says fairs spread a veil over political divisions, and that the fair reaffirmed France's centrality after Sedan and the Commune (Della Coletta 2006). Kaiser sees world exhibitions as a continuation of war by other means (Kaiser).

They differ on success. Roeber judges the fair a success from the French side despite the loss (Findling & Pelle 2008). Chandler says most people accepted the loss as the price of confidence in the Republic (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh argues that too few believed in the recovery the government wanted to show, and that attendance did not cover costs (Greenhalgh 1988).

They differ on the colonial displays. Hale treats 1878 as the start of a colonial genre that grew with the empire (Hale 2008). Greenhalgh sees it as a step towards the peopled displays of 1889 (Greenhalgh 1988). Roche describes the imperial sections as exotic cities of pavilions and palaces (Roche 2000). Çelik argues that the pavilions presented Islamic architecture as a fixed historical style, which denied Muslim architects creative freedom (Çelik 1992).

Other readings differ again. Bird reads the Palais du Champ de Mars as a dream building, whose real power lay in the engines and transmission hidden in its annexes (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Geppert treats the fair as the start of the German exhibition question, the debate over whether Germany should hold its own great exhibition (Geppert 2010). Benedict argues that the symbolic architecture and classifications of fairs expressed each nation's view of an ideally ordered world (Benedict 1983).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Matsukata Masayoshi headed the Japanese delegation. Hotta-Lister says Matsukata, astonished at European ignorance of Japan, had a large map of Asia made and displayed it with charts at the entrance of the Japanese pavilion (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 210). Unsold Japanese goods were withheld from dealers who wanted them at bargain prices (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 210). Japan's expenditure is given as 213,242 yen (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 221). Japanese design sketches issued by the Exhibition Bureau were used for the 1878 exhibition (Lawn 2009p. 74). It is said that Japan's educational exhibits earned admiration there, and Komatsu credits the Educational Museum with contributing to this (Lawn 2009p. 80). Mandell calls a tidy Japanese farm a popular success that increased the rage for oriental art (Mandell 1967p. 14).

China

The Chinese exhibit was entrusted to the Imperial Maritime Customs, a service dominated by foreigners (Hurp. 45). Hart hesitated to ask for Chinese people on the commission, not wanting the foreign section "swamped by the native element" (Hurp. 14). Chinese high officials came to the site without being commissioners (Hurp. 26). Guo Songtao wrote little about the fair beyond the opening ceremony (Hurp. 27). Li Shuchang listed the Chinese exhibits without criticism (Hurp. 28). Zeng Jize was harsher: the Chinese exhibit "lacks its core", and China erred in entrusting it to Westerners while the Japanese ran their own (Hurp. 28). Hur blames the customs control and the lack of engaged officials for the weak showing (Hurp. 45), and adds that the Boxer Uprising left the Qing government little attention for the fairs (Hurp. 46). Hur argues that Zeng, back in China in 1886, could have improved the exhibit had death not intervened four years later [16, l. 2467-2476; 2486].

India

Hoffenberg says Rudyard Kipling's father was organising the Indian Section of Arts and Manufactures (Hoffenberg 2001). Lord Lytton and Indian princes were among the commissioners who produced the Indian displays (Hoffenberg 2001p. 45). Sir Richard Temple was commissioner for India (Hoffenberg 2001p. 284). Greenhalgh gives the 1878 Indian Court as 20,000 square feet at £3,278, against 10,000 square feet at £12,342 at Vienna in 1873 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 59). Findling and Pelle's 1990 dictionary says that India appears among the statues of the powers only as "British India", an admission that it counted only as a British colony (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 67).

Southeast Asia

Findling and Pelle's 2008 encyclopedia says most of the rest of the world sent no exhibits, so the space was filled with ethnographic displays and Asian art, and the facades of Siam and China were small (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 61). Drevet was assigned the foreign pavilions, including Siam and Annam, in the Trocadéro Park (Çelik 1992p. 135–136). Cochinchina built a separate Annamite pavilion (Hale 2008p. 15).

Other peoples

Coté says the Dutch made abundant use of Native exhibitions, including working villages, and lists Paris 1878 among the fairs concerned (Cotép. 2).

Reading

Hur reads the Chinese record as evidence that the Qing government gave the fairs too little attention (Hurp. 45). Li's account is neutral, but Zeng's is sharply critical [16, l. 1566-1580; 1591-1596]. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions left lasting legacies, not only momentary spectacle (Hoffenberg 2001p. 12).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Republican recovery — the texts read the fair as the Third Republic's proof that France had recovered from 1870-71 and the Commune (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001)
  • Finance and loss — every text that gives a figure reports a loss, but the amounts differ, from 28 million to 38 million francs, and the loss changed how later fairs were funded (Findling & Pelle 2008; Mandell 1967; Allwood et al. 2001; Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Colonial display — the first colonial sections, the Algerian palace and the Cairo street made colonies a regular part of French fairs (Hale 2008; Çelik 1992; Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Peoples on display — native villages, exotic cities and peopled colonial shows appear as a new form that grew from 1878 (Geppert 2010; Greenhalgh 1988; Roche 2000)
  • Art and the war — the refused battle scenes and the narrow selection of modern painting show how the fair handled the defeat (Findling & Pelle 1990; Greenhalgh 1988)
  • Congresses and learning — the congresses, copyright and school museums show the fair as a place where knowledge was exchanged (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Lawn 2009)
  • Machines and industry — the machine halls and new inventions are read as the face of modern industry, and as a hidden power behind it (Hollengreen et al. 2014; Findling & Pelle 1990; Roche 2000)

Questions for study

  1. The texts disagree about attendance and about the loss. How far can the figures for a fair like this be trusted, and what would a historian need to check them?
  2. Did the Rue des Nations and the colonial sections show nations as they were, or as the organisers wanted them seen? How do Çelik and Hale answer this differently?
  3. Is 1878 the start of a new kind of colonial display, or part of a longer pattern that the texts only partly describe?
  4. Why did Germany stay away, and did its absence change the meaning of the fair for other nations?
  5. Who should carry the risk of a state-run fair? What did the 1878 deficit teach the deputies, and what did the 1889 funding show?
  6. Bird reads the machinery as a dream and as a hidden power. What does this reading show about the fair, and what does it leave out?

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
chapter 'Aucune Usine au Monde: Dreaming Work in the Exposition Universelle, Paris 1878' by Lawrence Bird, pp. 299-308

Bird reads the Palais du Champ de Mars of 1878 as a dream building, drawing on Marx's Capital and on Freud's writings on dreams. He argues that the building's face (the Rue des Nations and the Pavilion de Paris) hid its power, the steam engines, chimneys and transmission network of the annexes, which he treats as the displaced heart of the building. He reads the Galeries des Machines as a world factory that Marx's image of the 'mechanical monster' anticipates, and concludes that the fair's commodities and machines embodied a modern fear of replacement by the automaton. His case is a critical reading of industrial modernity through Marxist and Freudian concepts, rather than a narrative of the fair.

Allwood, Allan and Reid's survey places the 1878 fair among exhibitions held to restore a nation's pride: they say France, after the 1870-71 war and the Commune, used it to show that it remained an important cultural and political centre, and that its loss of nearly 32 million francs was used by its detractors. The Paris section describes the fair's peace theme, its building and its ventilation in detail, and argues that the fair was dominated by large manufacturers, so that its 'high principled, educational displays' had begun to give way to commerce. Sala's visitor's-eye account is used to show the exhaustion of crowds and the commercialisation of food and restaurants.

The 1878 Universal Exposition (French: Exposition Universelle, [ɛkspozisjɔ̃ ynivɛʁsɛl]), also known as the 1878 Paris Exposition, 1878 World Fair, or 1878 World Expo, was a world's fair held in Paris, France, from 1 May to 10 November 1878, to celebrate the recovery of France after the 1870–71 Franco-Prussian War. It was the third of ten major expositions held in the city between 1855 and 1937.
