In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The listing

Appendix D, headed Fairs Not Included, lists fairs and exhibitions by year and city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). Its introduction says the appendix covers fairs that fell short of the qualifications for the main body of the book (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). It adds that many of them were very specialised in nature and that others lacked a suitable international scope (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).

The entry for the Malaya-Borneo Exhibition gives only three facts: the year 1922, the city Singapore and the name (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427). It sits between the 1922 Exposition nationale coloniale at Marseille (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427) and the 1923 Gothenburg Tercentennial Jubilee Exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427). The list does not connect these entries.

Status and naming

The appendix describes its entries as exhibitions held after 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). This is consistent with the exhibition having taken place, but the entry itself gives no further evidence of that. The appendix also says that names are given in the language of the host country unless that language is not a common Western tongue (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). This entry gives the name in English (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427). The text does not explain the choice.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Excluded fairs — the appendix covers exhibitions that did not qualify for the main body of the book, and this entry is one of them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 427).
  • Pervasiveness of fairs — the introduction says the list as a whole shows how widespread such fairs were, especially in the nineteenth century; the entry adds nothing to that claim (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422).
  • Naming — the appendix's rule on names, and the English name given here (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 427).

Questions for study

  1. Why does a list of fairs excluded from the main body give this Singapore exhibition an English name, when its own rule points to the host-country language (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 427)?
  2. What sources, such as organisers' records or press reports, would show the exhibition's dates, scale and purpose, which this entry does not give (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427)?
  3. What does it mean for a reference work to exclude a fair from its main body, and how far does that limit what a historian can say about it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422)?
  4. What did the 1922 Marseille colonial exhibition and the 1922 Singapore exhibition have in common, if anything, given that the list places them side by side (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 427)?

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

On the web

Wikipedia

The Malaya–Borneo Exhibition was an exhibition of the trade and economic possibilities of British Malaya and British Borneo which was arranged to synchronise with the Prince of Wales's (later Edward VIII) visit to Singapore in March 1922. Laurence Guillemard, Governor of the Straits Settlements, conceived the idea of bringing people together from Malaya and Borneo for the purpose of enhancing trade and other mutual benefits. "It was also felt that the opportunity was a fitting one for the exhibition, by the commercial and foreign communities, of imported goods in illustration of the varied trade activities of this country in general, and of the great exchange port of Singapore in particular."
