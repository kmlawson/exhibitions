The Tokyo Taishō Exhibition (Tōkyō Taishō hakurankai) was held in Tokyo in 1914 (Parkp. 9; Henry 2014p. 101). Two texts mention it briefly, both as a precedent for the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition in Korea. Park says the 1915 architecture followed a Tokyo precedent, and Henry says a Tokyo poster supplied imagery for the 1915 poster (Parkp. 9; Henry 2014p. 101). The texts give no opening dates, organisers, site or visitor figures.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Architecture and display

Park says that the architecture of the 1915 exhibition on the Kyŏngbok Palace site followed a precedent set by the Tokyo exhibition (Parkp. 9). At Tokyo, Western-style pavilions denoted "modernizing Japan", while the Korean pavilion was built in a traditional native Korean style (Parkp. 9). Park's note cites Tanaka's guide to the Tokyo exhibition, p. 40, for the Korean pavilion (Parkp. 9). The Tokyo buildings adopted Renaissance and Secession styles, with high towers and arches, as the 1915 pavilions did (Parkp. 10).

Hygiene displays

Park also says that the Tokyo exhibition of 1914 presented modern hygiene and sanitation through displays of bacteria samples, anatomical specimens, and modern medical appliances. Its aim was to present Japan as a civilised modern country (Parkp. 12). Park places the Tokyo exhibition beside the Sixth National Exposition for the Promotion of Industry in 1907, which showed hygiene in the same way (Parkp. 12).

Posters and names

Henry says that the kisaeng on the promotional poster for the 1915 exhibition on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds was borrowed from "a poster issued for the Tokyo Taisho- Exhibition in 1914" (Henry 2014p. 101). The text gives no further description of that Tokyo poster. Henry reproduces it as figure 12b, from the official report of the Tokyo exhibition, published in Tokyo in 1917 (Henry 2014p. 103p. 279). The two texts romanise the name differently: Park writes Tōkyō Taishō hakurankai and Henry writes To-kyo- taisho- hakurankai (Parkp. 9; Henry 2014p. 103).

The Korean pavilion

At Tokyo the Korean pavilion stood apart from the Western-style pavilions that Park reads as signs of a modernising Japan (Parkp. 9). The texts say nothing more about Korean exhibits or visitors there.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Modern and native styles — At Tokyo, Western-style pavilions were set against a native Korean pavilion, and Park treats this as a precedent for the 1915 site (Parkp. 9).
  • Architectural precedent — Renaissance and Secession styles, with high towers and arches, appear at Tokyo as in the 1915 pavilions (Parkp. 10).
  • Hygiene as display — Hygiene displays of bacteria samples and medical appliances presented Japan as civilised (Parkp. 12).
  • Posters and imagery — A Tokyo poster lent its imagery to the 1915 poster (Henry 2014p. 101).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the Tokyo organisers set Western-style pavilions against a native Korean pavilion, and what did that contrast tell visitors about Korea (Parkp. 9)?
  2. Park's account of the Korean pavilion rests on a guide (note 26, l. 510). What other evidence would show how the Tokyo pavilions looked and who designed them?
  3. Henry shows a Tokyo poster only as the source of a kisaeng image (Henry 2014p. 101). What does borrowing a poster across exhibitions suggest about how colonial organisers learned from Japanese exhibitions?
  4. Park presents hygiene displays as proof of Japan's civilisation (Parkp. 12). How might a Korean visitor have read the same displays?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Representations of “Modern Korea” in the 1915 Exhibitionp. 7Asia

Park examines the architecture, layout and display of the 1915 exhibition as a way of representing 'modern Korea'. The chapter draws on the official report, the exhibition guide and visitor routes printed in Maeil shinbo and Keijō nippō, and on posters, photographs and postcards. It shows that the organisers built Western-style pavilions on the east of the Kyŏngbok Palace site, destroying a large part of its structures, and kept older palace halls on the west as small exhibition rooms. Visitors followed a numbered route from the 'modern' pavilions to the 'premodern' or 'old' space. Displays of penal and hygiene systems, provincial development and products were arranged to show Korean backwardness against Japanese progress. Park situates the architecture in the Tokyo Taisho exhibition of 1914 and in Renaissance and Secession styles, and concludes that the buildings served the colonial claim to have made a 'new modern' Korea.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 2

Coté, Joost. “Staging Modernity: The Semarang International Colonial Exhibition, 1914.” Journal article.
Untitled opening section (no heading in text)p. 1Asia

Coté opens by placing the Semarang International Colonial Exhibition of 1914 within the history of world's fairs, which he presents as stages for a discourse of progress and a pedagogy of mass education. Colonial exhibitions, he argues, carried the same didactic aims as European ones but faced a particular problem: could native audiences read them correctly? Drawing on Prakash's account of British India and on Dutch participation in world exhibitions from London in 1851 to Paris in 1931, he shows how the Netherlands used such displays to present itself abroad. The Semarang exhibition, planned in 1912, postponed from 1913 and staged by the city's chamber of commerce rather than the colonial government, is described through its scale, its architecture, its electric lighting and the foreign pavilions of Japan, China and two Australian states. Coté concludes that the exhibition, opening as war began in Europe, was a settler-led showcase whose meanings could not be fixed, and that it marked the beginning of the end of the imperial future it celebrated.

Findling, John E., and Kimberly D. Pelle, eds. Encyclopedia of World’s Fairs and Expositions. Jefferson, NC: McFarland, 2008.
EAppendix E: Fairs That Never Werep. 428

This appendix to the Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions lists expositions that were planned but never held, from New York 1913 and Baltimore 1914 to Calgary 2005, each as a short history. It does not argue a thesis; it records how each proposal was made, which sites and themes were chosen, and where it stopped. The causes recur: world wars and the Great Depression, economic slumps, doubts that a city could afford the cost, governments and mayors withdrawing support, and rival bids that won the BIE vote. Several entries are detailed, notably Philadelphia 1976, Los Angeles 1981, Paris 1989, Chicago 1992, Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, which trace the bidding, financing and political fights. Some entries show a fair's afterlife, such as Tokyo 1940 tickets honored at Osaka 1970. The appendix ends with Calgary's defeat by Aichi for 2005.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
section on the 1915 exhibition's route, architecture and displays (printed pp. 9-10); hygiene passage (PDF p. 13); notes 26 and 35; bibliography

Park's article is about the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition at Kyŏngbok Palace, and it mentions the Tokyo Taishō exhibition of 1914 at three points. Park says the 1915 architecture followed a precedent set at Tokyo, where Western-style pavilions denoted 'modernizing Japan' and the Korean pavilion was built in a native style. Park also says the Tokyo buildings used Renaissance and Secession styles, and that its hygiene displays presented Japan as a civilised modern country. These points support Park's wider argument that the Western styles of 1915 signified Western authority and that its displays set Korea's uncivilised past against Japanese modernisation.

“the Western-style pavilions denoted “modernizing Japan” p. 9
About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
