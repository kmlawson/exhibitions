Calcutta Exhibition

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Calcutta Exhibition was an Indian exhibition held in 1923 in Calcutta's Eden Gardens (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). Geppert describes it as a rehearsal for the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley, although it was largely independent of that show (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). The texts do not name its organisers (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). It matters because India's official pavilion at Wembley was made conditional on a preliminary exhibition in Calcutta (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background, organisers and site

Geppert places the Calcutta exhibition within the preparations for Wembley. It was held in part to select the exhibits Bengal would send to London (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). Local opposition, mostly on political grounds, met the exhibition, and some of the invited companies refused to take part (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). Its exhibits came from all parts of India, but only a small portion were later transferred to London (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). The passages give no organiser, sponsor or visitor figures for the Calcutta show (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145). Hoffenberg cites its handbook once, in a note, and does not discuss the show.

Empire and colony

The Wembley organisers called their show the "first Empire Exhibition ever" (Geppert 2010p. 145). Geppert describes India, within the Dominions' Ring at Wembley, as "technically still a colony" (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163). On 25 March 1922 the Indian Legislative Assembly decided that India should have an official pavilion at Wembley, "on condition that a preliminary exhibition be held in Calcutta" (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163). The passage gives no date for that preliminary exhibition; the 1923 date appears at line 6547 (Geppert 2010p. 145). The Indian section was then organised by the government of India, not the India Office in London, for the first time (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163).

Place in the series

Geppert's appendix of exhibitions for 1920 to 1929 lists the "Calcutta Exhibition" for 1923, between the Gothenburg Jubilee Exhibition and the Exposition Coloniale, Agricole et Industrielle at Strasbourg (Geppert 2010p. 253; Geppert 2010p. 253; Geppert 2010p. 253). His notes also record an earlier international exhibition in Calcutta, held in 1883 to 1884, which is a different show (Geppert 2010p. 311; Geppert 2010p. 311).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Preparation for Wembley — Geppert treats the Calcutta show as a rehearsal for Wembley that was largely independent of it (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145).
  • Selection of exhibits — the show was held in part to choose the Bengal exhibits sent to London, and only a small portion of its exhibits went there (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145).
  • Political opposition — local opposition, mostly on political grounds, led some invited companies to refuse to take part (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145).
  • Conditional participation — India's official Wembley pavilion depended on a preliminary exhibition in Calcutta (Geppert 2010p. 163; Geppert 2010p. 163).
  • Thin evidence — the texts give no visitor figures, organisers or finance for the Calcutta show (Geppert 2010p. 145; Geppert 2010p. 145).

Questions for study

Brief discussion 1

Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.
Ch. 5, 'Wembley 1924' (the chapter is not about Calcutta): pp. 145-146 (ll. 6538-6563); India pavilion pp. 163-164 (ll. 7180-7222); notes pp. 311 and 313 (ll. 13808, 13928); appendix p. 253 (l. 11030); bibliography p. 337 (l. 15254); index (l. 18087)

Geppert mentions the 1923 Calcutta Exhibition in one paragraph of his chapter on the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley, plus notes, an appendix entry and the index; it is not his subject. He calls it a 'rehearsal' for Wembley that was largely independent of it, held in part to choose the exhibits Bengal would send to London, and he records local opposition, mostly on political grounds, that led some invited companies to refuse. He also shows that India's official pavilion at Wembley was made conditional on a preliminary exhibition in Calcutta, and that the Indian section was organised by the government of India rather than the India Office. The Calcutta material serves his wider account of Wembley as a suburban display of the Empire, in which India's place had changed from the India of 1851 to a self-governing partner.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a minor, scattered thread in this book rather than a theme. Japan and China appear among the foreign colonial displays at the Trocadéro in chapter 3, Paris 1900: The Exposition Universelle as a Century's Protean Synthesis, and China is one of the countries 'visited' by the Tour du Monde panorama. Chapter 4, London 1908: Imre Kiralfy and the Franco-British Exhibition, mentions the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910 within the bi-national pattern, and describes Burmese artisans and a Buddhist priest among the performers Kiralfy recruited for his 1896 India and Ceylon Exhibition, with the terms of their contract. Chapter 5, Wembley 1924: The British Empire Exhibition as a Suburban Metropolis, has a Burma Pavilion and a Burmese Pagoda, lists Malaya and Hong Kong among the colonies, and quotes observers on the 'atmosphere of Orientalism'. The fullest Southeast Asian material is in chapter 6, Vincennes 1931: The Exposition Coloniale as the Apotheosis of Imperial Modernity. Indochina is the centrepiece of its colonial section, where a reconstruction of Angkor Wat on the Avenue des Colonies is described as Indochina's contribution and one of two chefs-d'oeuvre. A Times correspondent notes Annamites and Cambodians going about their business among visitors, and the chapter reports the burning of the Dutch pavilion, which the Surrealists read as contempt for the peoples whose objects it held. Geppert does not discuss exhibitions held in Asia, Asian commissioners or merchants, or Asian visitors, and Japan's Expo '70 at Osaka appears only in the introduction. Ceylon and India receive far more attention than the Southeast Asian territories.
