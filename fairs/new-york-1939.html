New York World's Fair

The New York World's Fair of 1939–40 ran at Flushing Meadows, Queens, in two six-month seasons, opening on 30 April 1939 and closing on 27 October 1940 (Cotter 2009; Maloney 2012p. 174). Its official theme, Building the World of Tomorrow with the Tools of Today, was given form by the Trylon and Perisphere and by General Motors' Futurama and Democracity (Kargon et al. 2015p. 64; Allwood et al. 2001p. 83). Historians use the fair to examine corporate sponsorship, planning and a democratic optimism that the war overtook (Kargon et al. 2015p. 66; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 300). It ended with a deficit of $18.7 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and finance

The fair began as a business plan. Cotter opens by saying it "began, as so many grand projects do, as a way to make money" (Cotter 2009). Its model was Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933 (Cotter 2009; Kargon et al. 2015p. 60). Gelernter dates the first meetings of the promoters to 1934 (Gelernter 1995p. 339), and places the first presentation of the plan at a Manhattan dinner on 17 September 1935 (Gelernter 1995p. 340). The public announcement followed on 23 September (Gelernter 1995p. 340; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 293).

The texts disagree about who first linked the fair to the 150th anniversary of Washington's inauguration. Gelernter judges it likely that the civil engineer Joseph Shadgen proposed the link, and says Shadgen later sued over the claim and the corporation settled out of court (Gelernter 1995p. 340; Gelernter 1995p. 341; Cotter 2009). Mullen attributes the idea to a remark by Shadgen's daughter (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 293).

The fair was incorporated in September 1935 as a non-profit body backed by 23 banks and trust companies and about 30 corporations (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 300). Sixteen Manhattan banks lent $1.6 million (Gelernter 1995p. 343). Robert Moses, the parks commissioner, won an arrangement that set aside the first $2 million of profit for a park, but Gelernter says the profits came to nothing (Gelernter 1995p. 344). Spending reached $156,905,000 by opening day, against an expectation of 40 million visitors in one season (Cotter 2009). Sayers puts the cost at about $155 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 300).

Site, architecture and the zones

The site was the Corona Dump, a city refuse ground since 1908 (Maloney 2012p. 176). Engineers drained the marsh, rerouted the Flushing River and moved about 10,000 large trees (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 299; Maloney 2012p. 177). The fair covered 1,216.5 acres (Cotter 2009).

The texts disagree about the layout. Benedict counts seven zones, each with a focal exhibit (Benedict 1983p. 35), while Mullen counts nine colour-coded zones, two of which had no focal exhibit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 296). Mullen calls the architecture modern but not daringly so, and Frederic Gutheim labelled it corporation style (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 296). Allwood and Reid call the architecture dull (Allwood et al. 2001p. 83). Greenhalgh sees Art Deco and Streamform, a style of smooth, tear-drop forms, almost everywhere (Greenhalgh 1988p. 168; Greenhalgh 1988p. 169). At least 34 large corporate buildings stood on the grounds (Benedict 1983p. 25). The organisers had no government backing to enforce their plan for coordinated sectors (Allwood et al. 2001p. 117).

Schrenk argues that Walter Dorwin Teague's influence, especially his preference for theatrical presentations of simplified messages over detailed explanation, reached across the fairgrounds (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). In his account the New York fair was a closer descendant of the corporate Chicago fair than of the political Golden Gate exposition (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Even at the Ford Building, designed jointly by Albert Kahn and Teague, public education was no longer a central aim (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 190). Its 30-foot Cyclorama of Production was a rotating display of eighty-seven groups of animated figures, showing how twenty-seven raw materials went into a Ford car (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 191; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 191).

Exhibits and attractions

The Trylon, a tall tower, and the Perisphere, a great sphere, were the symbols of the fair (Allwood et al. 2001p. 83). Sayers gives the Trylon as 610 feet tall, and the New York Times called the pair "the egg and the folk" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 302; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 302). Democracity lay inside the Perisphere, reached by an electric staircase and a moving balcony above a model of a garden city for a million people (Greenhalgh 1988p. 136). Sayers gives its population as 1.5 million across 10,000 square miles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 302). Kargon and others name Henry Dreyfuss as its designer, and record that Mumford called its conception stale (Kargon et al. 2015p. 73). Gelernter argues that "Democracity's utopian World of Tomorrow amounts, in essence, to the modern suburbs" (Gelernter 1995p. 67).

General Motors hired Norman Bel Geddes in 1938 under a contract worth $200,000 plus expenses (Rydell 1993p. 133). Futurama let visitors glide in moving chairs over a model of a 1960 America (Benedict 1983p. 17). Over 27 million people queued for it, some for up to two hours (Kargon et al. 2015p. 76; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 69). The amusement zone was led by Billy Rose's Aquacade, which took in more than $2.7 million in 1939 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 298). Rydell says Bel Geddes told the dancers in the erotic shows to discard the bras and nets that management had ordered (Rydell 1993p. 142). Gelernter records arrests at the nude shows (Gelernter 1995p. 387).

Morshed gives Futurama's ride as eighteen minutes, with an average attendance of more than 28,000 a day (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 511; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 511). Schrenk gives a fifteen-minute ride over a model including over two million buildings (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 192; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 192). Morshed quotes a General Motors leaflet giving 500,000 individually designed houses (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 510; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 511). The two accounts of the model do not reconcile. The one-acre model was built from aerial photographs supplied by Fairchild Aerial Surveys, and visitors left with a pin reading "I have seen the future" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512).

Morshed gives the Trylon as 700 feet tall, against the 610 feet given above (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 508). Democracity's population also differs. The official guidebook, as Morshed quotes it, describes a city of a million people, against the 1.5 million given above (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 509; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 302).

Gelernter's diary calls the Con Edison City of Light the largest diorama in the world, a shallow model of Manhattan with a thunderstorm and nightfall. He notes that Walter Dorwin Teague designed it (Gelernter 1995p. 264; Gelernter 1995p. 265; Gelernter 1995p. 266). Gelernter reads the fair's dioramas as an art of overview and control, a thirties wish to hold the city whole from above (Gelernter 1995p. 266; Gelernter 1995p. 266). Alexander Calder choreographed the fountains that played in front of it (Gelernter 1995p. 266).

Gelernter describes the Westinghouse time capsule, installed in September 1938 for opening in 6939. It held a child's Mickey Mouse cup and a copy of Gone with the Wind (Gelernter 1995p. 269; Gelernter 1995p. 269). Its Book of Record, sent to libraries, carried messages from Einstein, Thomas Mann and Robert Millikan (Gelernter 1995p. 269; Gelernter 1995p. 270). Gelernter's diarist found Elektro, a seven-foot robot with its dog Sparko, heavy and clunky (Gelernter 1995p. 270; Gelernter 1995p. 270). Rydell names a Westinghouse robot Moto (Rydell 1993p. 111; Rydell 1993p. 111). The texts do not say whether they describe the same machine.

Nations and colonies on show

The counts differ. Sayers gives 58 nations and two international bodies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303); Peterson gives 33 nations (Peterson 2020p. 113); Samuel gives 59 countries in 1939 and 27 in 1940 (Samuel 2007). Germany's absence is the best-recorded case. Sayers says Germany signed a pavilion contract in 1937 and in April 1938 said it could not raise the foreign exchange (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 301). Gelernter says Germany and China were the only large countries not represented (Gelernter 1995p. 291). Greenhalgh argues that by 1939 the German government no longer wished to live in the Democracity vision (Greenhalgh 1988p. 135).

Gelernter says Whalen secured a Soviet pavilion through the Soviet ambassador in Washington, and that the announced outlay of four million dollars drew other nations into the contest (Gelernter 1995p. 346; Gelernter 1995p. 346). Rydell and others single out its 79-foot worker statue as the most impressive feature (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The pavilion was dismantled and shipped home in 1940 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303). A Pan American Union pavilion showed exhibits from nine Latin American countries (Benedict 1983p. 26). Colonial displays were modest. Greenhalgh argues that the theme left no room for overt American imperialism (Greenhalgh 1988p. 78), and Cotter notes a small Hall of Nations display of France's colonies (Cotter 2009). The Asian displays are discussed below.

Visitors and the people on display

The first season drew 25,817,265 paid visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 298). Market Analysts Inc., hired by the fair, found its surveyed visitors mostly middle class, with one in six a professional and two per cent unemployed (Rydell 1993p. 154). Mullen's survey of 449 people at Coney Island and Rockaway Beach found complaints about prices and walking, since the fair charged 75 cents when other fairs charged 50 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299).

African Americans were largely excluded from the fair's work. The NAACP found about twenty African American workers among several hundred in 1937 (Rydell 1993p. 183). By April 1939 the figure was 392 of 6,335 staff (Rydell 1993p. 184). Rydell records nearly 500 pickets outside the gates on opening day (Rydell 1993p. 185); Rydell, Findling and Pelle give more than 400 (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). In 1940 the American Common hosted a Negro Week led by T. Arnold Hill (Rydell 1993p. 185). Roche describes this as a late and partial step (Roche 2000p. 86).

Women were a planning category. Weimann reports that the fair's director of women's participation said women had been a first thought in planning (Weimann 1981p. 598). Forty-eight committees, balanced by geography, religion, race and politics, centred on essay contests and goodwill tours (Weimann 1981p. 598). Ganz, reporting Freedman, contrasts the 1939 fair, which had no separate women's building, with the Woman's Building of 1893 (Ganz 2008).

Politics and controversy

In 1937 La Guardia urged a chamber of horrors devoted to Nazi Germany (Gelernter 1995p. 291). Expatriate Germans and Americans proposed a Freedom Pavilion. Whalen offered a free site and then withdrew it, and the effort ended after a meeting with Swope and caution from Monsignor Lavelle (Gelernter 1995p. 291). Sayers records that the Nazi press attacked La Guardia's idea (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 301). The Soviet pavilion was dismantled after the Soviet invasion of Finland and replaced by the American Common (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303).

The Bureau of International Expositions (BIE), the Paris body that regulated official fairs, was concerned with the frequency of fairs and the rights of exhibitors (Samuel 2007). The texts disagree on whether it approved this fair. Allwood and Reid say the BIE registered the fair and that it succeeded admirably (Allwood et al. 2001p. 148). Samuel says the fair launched without official BIE sanction, and that member nations took part through Whalen's persuasion (Samuel 2007). Maloney calls it the last grand BIE-sanctioned fair in twentieth-century America (Maloney 2012p. 174).

Second season, war and afterlife

Harvey Dow Gibson replaced Whalen for the 1940 season. Gibson dropped the zones and leased buildings to any tenant (Cotter 2009), and the theme became For Peace and Freedom (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 151). Admission fell from 75 to 50 cents (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The second-season figures differ: about 17 million in one picture book (Cotter 2009) and seventeen million in Hollengreen et al. (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 152); just over 19 million in Rydell, Findling and Pelle (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Both seasons together drew 44,932,978, according to Badger's appendix (Badger 1979p. 131).

The 1940 season earned nearly $13 million against almost $8 million of expenses (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299). The overall deficit was $18,723,222 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299; Allwood et al. 2001p. 120). Other texts give about $18 million (Allwood et al. 2001p. 83), almost $19 million (Kargon et al. 2015p. 80) and $22 million (Samuel 2007).

The Trylon and Perisphere were demolished and their steel sold for scrap for the war effort (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299). Moses's park never materialised, because the money was not there (Samuel 2007). The Japanese pavilion and garden were kept as the only foreign structure in the postfair park (Maloney 2012p. 194). The 1964–65 fair reused the roads and utility systems left by the 1939 fair (Cotter & Young 2008p. 14).

Flight, wings and the aerial view

Adnan Morshed argues that the fair's ascension theme, with its wings, flying figures and aerial viewpoints, gave the fair its picture of the World of Tomorrow, and that 1939 was the last burst of this utopian technological culture before the war (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 505; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 505). Joseph Binder's winning poster showed nine aircraft climbing beside the 700-foot Trylon, and the official guidebook used it for its cover (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 503; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 503; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 503). In July 1938 Howard Hughes flew round the world in a Lockheed 14-N named New York World's Fair 1939. On his return Grover Whalen met him at Floyd Bennett Field, where he counted 20,000 other New Yorkers among the crowd (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 506; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 506; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 506). Morshed says Whalen blended the popular myth of the aviator with corporate promotion (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 506).

Robert Foster's steel figure with a cape crowned the Ford pavilion. Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney's sculpture, three wings on an inclined base for two figures, had a 24-foot span (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 507; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 507). Whitney called it an homage to youth, but La Guardia called it a tribute to aviation at its dedication (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 507; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 507).

Spectators on the revolving balconies inside the Perisphere, which made one turn every six minutes, were themselves part of the exhibit. Morshed argues that they evoked the aviator looking down on a chaotic world below (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 509; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 509; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 510). Donald Deskey's focal exhibit in the Chrysler Building ended with a rocket launched towards London (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512). On 30 April 1939 Roosevelt proclaimed that "the eyes of the United States are fixed on the future" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512). Morshed argues that the sponsors and the New York elite used this future-gazing to dispel the economic woes of the Depression (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 512).

Science and the fair's ideology

Rydell argues that scientists were the intellectual underwriters of the Chicago and New York fairs, and helped to design their themes (Rydell 1993p. 93). Planners of the New York fair at first saw no need to involve the wider scientific community (Rydell 1993p. 106). The chemist Harold Urey, of Columbia, objected that the fair gave room to a "meaningless symbol of a sphere" where a science building should have stood (Rydell 1993p. 107; Rydell 1993p. 107). Managers then appointed Gerald Wendt as consultant and created an Advisory Committee on Science, which first met in March 1938 (Rydell 1993p. 107; Rydell 1993p. 107; Rydell 1993p. 107). Waldemar Kaempffert, science editor of the New York Times, urged that the area recently vacated by Nazi Germany should house a science hall (Rydell 1993p. 108). Managers offered a converted building, but no funds for exhibits (Rydell 1993p. 108; Rydell 1993p. 108).

Albert Einstein was named nominal chairman of the committee, though he never attended one of its meetings (Rydell 1993p. 109; Rydell 1993p. 109). On opening night cosmic rays replaced the star Arcturus as the signal, and Einstein threw the switch (Rydell 1993p. 111). Wendt argued that housing needed the method that had made the automobile industry an example of scientific progress (Rydell 1993p. 110; Rydell 1993p. 111). Rydell says the New York fair turned this scientific formula into a recipe for utopia (Rydell 1993p. 106). The Science and Education Building opened with a rotunda dominated by an aluminum airplane, and Wendt's book Science for the World of Tomorrow appeared in time for the opening (Rydell 1993p. 112; Rydell 1993p. 110).

The Consumers building and the income exhibit

Egmond Arens designed the focal exhibit in the Consumers building, which set four official income levels: $800 for subsistence, $2,000 for maintenance, $2,500 for the good life and $5,000 for luxury (Gelernter 1995p. 113; Gelernter 1995p. 113; Gelernter 1995p. 113). Gelernter converts these into early-1990s dollars (Gelernter 1995p. 113). Visitors were wealthier and better educated than the average American, yet about a quarter of each month's visitors earned less than the $2,000 maintenance level (Gelernter 1995p. 113; Gelernter 1995p. 113). The exhibit claimed that a third of the country lived below subsistence (Gelernter 1995p. 113). Arens's wording echoed Roosevelt's second inaugural, in which a third of the nation was ill-housed, ill-clad and ill-nourished (Gelernter 1995p. 114; Gelernter 1995p. 114).

Gelernter sets these figures against 1935, when the typical middle-class family rented a four-and-a-half-room apartment and about three per cent of families owned their home (Gelernter 1995p. 114; Gelernter 1995p. 114). He gives the average 1939 income as $1,266, roughly $11,400 in modern money, and concludes that the nation is far richer than it was in 1939 (Gelernter 1995p. 114; Gelernter 1995p. 114). The critic Gardner Harding called the building a "nearly empty shell" apart from its focal exhibit, and named the Consumers Union as the only real consumer presence there (Gelernter 1995p. 116; Gelernter 1995p. 117). Paul Manship's four white sculptural groups stood in the courtyard pool, on clouds above misty fountains (Gelernter 1995p. 117; Gelernter 1995p. 117).

How historians have interpreted it

The main readings agree that the fair was optimistic and lost money. They disagree about what that optimism meant. Gelernter reads it as an expression of 1930s faith in planning, technology and community, and argues against Kessner, who called Futurama rank commercialism. Gelernter holds that the intellectual focal exhibits were noncommercial (Gelernter 1995p. 360).

Kargon argues that the fair was all about the future, and that its meaning was contested between New Deal planners and business interests (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 67; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 67). Kargon, Fiss, Low and Molella argue that the planning message lost out after the 1937 recession, when corporate sponsors overtook the New Deal reformers, and that Democracity was much compromised (Kargon et al. 2015p. 66). Rydell argues that the fair imagined a future perfect, designed by corporate sponsors and social engineers (Rydell 1993p. 130). Roche, citing Rydell, says the fair embodied New Deal social policy (Roche 2000p. 77).

Rydell, Findling and Pelle present the optimism as more durable in memory than the finances (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Mullen says the fair is popular because it represents an "optimistic world that was destroyed by World War II" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 300).

Greenhalgh reads Democracity as a political statement by an America confident of its democratic leadership, and sees New York's modernism as market-driven, unlike the Paris exhibition of 1937 (Greenhalgh 1988p. 136; Greenhalgh 1988p. 168). Benedict calls the fair a watershed after which fairs became less important, and argues that its everyman's fair slogan promoted middle-class values (Benedict 1983p. 59; Benedict 1983p. 35). Maloney argues that the landscape reclaimed the dump as a park and held the corporate pavilions together (Maloney 2012p. 174). Samuel uses the 1939 fair as the baseline against which the 1964 fair is judged (Samuel 2007). Peterson reads Japan's displays as staged for American taste (Peterson 2020p. 117). Cotter writes affectionately and visually, without a sustained argument (Cotter 2009).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan was the only Asian nation with a dedicated country pavilion in 1939, because China was in turmoil and could not offer one (Peterson 2020p. 119). The temple-like pavilion, modelled on the Ise shrine, was designed by Nippon Kōbō (Peterson 2020p. 113; Maloney 2012p. 187). Peterson calls that firm, by 1939, essentially a propaganda arm of the Japanese military government, and says its publishing project was funded by the foreign ministry and the army (Peterson 2020p. 124; Peterson 2020p. 124). Kargon and others credit the architect Kishida Hideto, and say Matsui Yasuo largely designed the building. Yamawaki Iwao designed a display backed by Japanese businesses in the Hall of Nations (Kargon et al. 2015p. 94; Kargon et al. 2015p. 94). Nippon Kōbō made the photomurals (Kargon et al. 2015p. 96). Tsuyoshi Tamura designed the garden, which included a replica of the Isuzu river (Maloney 2012p. 186; Maloney 2012p. 187). After the fair the pavilion and garden went to New York City, and Commissioner General Kaname Wakasugi called them a monument to friendship (Maloney 2012p. 194; Maloney 2012p. 194).

On 2 June 1939 Akiko Tsukimoto, presented as Miss Japan, handed a flame of friendship to Grover Whalen, though she was not formally Miss Japan (Peterson 2020p. 125; Peterson 2020p. 125). The Takarazuka Revue played an eight-day season from 21 May 1939 at $2.80 a ticket (Peterson 2020p. 133). The New York Times critic John Martin dismissed it as a girl show (Peterson 2020p. 135).

At the pavilion's opening on 6 May 1939, Commissioner General Edward J. Flynn said Japan had adapted to the machine age without greatly disrupting its social habits (Peterson 2020p. 116). Peterson says diplomats' speeches presented Japan as the rightful leader of Asia (Peterson 2020p. 117). The pavilion's Liberty Bell in pearls was valued at one million dollars; Benedict gives 400 diamonds in it, and Peterson 366 (Benedict 1983p. 18; Peterson 2020p. 121). On closing day, Church of God representatives addressed Japanese officials across the way and hoped there would be no war with the United States (Gelernter 1995p. 354).

China

China had no official exhibit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303). Sayers notes that Japan had begun a war in China in 1937, and that it continued as the fair opened (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303). A replica of the Golden Temple of Jehol, first shown at Chicago, stood in the amusement zone (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303). Cotter calls it the Chinese Lama Temple (Cotter 2009). Chinese-American investors sought to fund a Chinese village on the site, but the plan failed (Peterson 2020p. 110). The fair's Official Chinatown Guide Book, introduced by Whalen, sent visitors to New York's Chinatown (Peterson 2020p. 110).

Southeast Asia

Siam had a pavilion in the 1939 season (Cotter 2009). In 1940 Siam left and the Society of Friends moved in (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 299). Cotter says the building later housed the American Bureau for Medical Aid to China (Cotter 2009). The Netherlands pavilion showed the home country, the Dutch East Indies, and Suriname and Curaçao (Cotter 2009). Frank Buck's Jungleland featured Malayan workers from his expeditions, shown in a re-created native village (Cotter 2009). The texts record no official display from India, Ceylon, Korea or the Philippines.

Peterson argues that the Japanese displays offered a delicate, traditional image that the press and publicity wanted, and that this hid the modern and militarised side of Japan (Peterson 2020p. 117). Kargon and others note that Japan's planned 1940 exposition, for the 2,600th anniversary celebrations, was abandoned (Kargon et al. 2015p. 84).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Corporate sponsorship — Corporate sponsors shaped the fair's displays and its idea of the future, and the 1937 recession strengthened their hand (Kargon et al. 2015p. 66; Rydell 1993p. 130).
  • Planning and Democracity — Democracity was the fair's planning statement, which Gelernter reads as a utopia and Greenhalgh as political propaganda (Gelernter 1995p. 67; Greenhalgh 1988p. 136).
  • Futurama and commerce — Futurama drew the largest crowds, and the texts dispute whether it was commercial advertising or a serious statement of the future (Kargon et al. 2015p. 76; Gelernter 1995p. 360).
  • Politics and war — The Freedom Pavilion, Germany's withdrawal and the Soviet pavilion's dismantling show the fair pressed by the approach of war (Gelernter 1995p. 291; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 301; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303).
  • Race and exclusion — Few African Americans found work at the fair, and the concessions made in reply were judged insufficient (Rydell 1993p. 183; Roche 2000p. 86).
  • Women and display — Women were a planning category, and the texts contrast the fair's approach with earlier women's buildings (Weimann 1981p. 598; Ganz 2008).
  • Nations and colonial display — Participation figures differ, and colonial displays were modest (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 303; Greenhalgh 1988p. 78).

Questions for study

  1. Did corporate sponsors shape the fair's vision of the future, or did planners such as the New Deal reformers keep some control over it?
  2. Should the popularity of Futurama be read as evidence of commercial success, or as a sincere hope for the future?
  3. Is the fair's nostalgia for optimism a fair account of 1939, or does it hide the failures and exclusions of the fair?
  4. Why did African American protest make so little difference to the fair's staffing, and what did the American Common achieve?
  5. How should a historian weigh the staged tradition of the Japanese pavilion against the modern and militarised Japan that the same displays concealed?
  6. How much did the approach of war change the meaning of the fair between its two seasons?
  7. How far did the aerial view of Democracity and Futurama persuade fairgoers that corporate planning was the route to the future, and how far did the sponsors' own publicity shape what visitors reported seeing?
  8. Did the Advisory Committee on Science change the fair's science displays, or did it mainly lend the authority of the scientific community to corporate exhibits?
  9. How should a student weigh the Consumers building's income exhibit, which put a third of the nation below subsistence, against the optimism of the commercial exhibits around it?

A book about this fair, chapter by chapter 28

Gelernter, David. 1939: The Lost World of the Fair. New York: Free Press, 1995.

Gelernter asks what the optimism of the 1939 New York World's Fair meant and why it has since vanished. He argues that the fair rested on a civic faith in America and a utopian future, that many of its promises came true in suburbs and highways, and that the faith itself has died. The book is built around the diary of Hortense Glassman, who walked the grounds with the engineer Mark Handler in 1939, and around later interviews with Mrs. Levine, who was Glassman. Early chapters follow the couple through a single day and set the pavilions beside essays on politics, technology, architecture and social rules. Later chapters follow Handler and Levine to his death in 1943, and the epilogue turns to the fair's history and to religion.

1Prologue: The Center of Timep. 1

The Prologue sets New York in 1939 against the present, a city of new bridges, skyscrapers and La Guardia's reform government. Long conversations with Hattie Levine, a fairgoer who recalls the Futurama and the Trylon and Perisphere, give the fair its texture. Gelernter argues that its optimism rested on a civic religion in which the American creed and a utopian future were believed, and that the fair's promises were largely fulfilled, notably in suburbs and highways. Evidence includes the exhibits, a 1940 Roper poll in which a plurality stayed optimistic after France fell, and the Polish and Palestine pavilions, which were shadowed by war. The chapter concludes that the faith has since died, and ends by inviting readers on a tour of the fair.

10Technology: Gorgeousp. 162

"Technology: Gorgeous" argues that the 1939 fair treated machines as objects of beauty and almost spiritual feeling, and that art and engineering were so closely joined that visitors saw no conflict between them. Miss Glassman's diary places the couple in the Ford courtyard, where a Novachord concert was advertised, and Gelernter uses that ensemble, led by Ferde Grofe, to introduce Gershwin as the decade's representative composer. The main evidence is the fair's murals, about half of which had technological themes, and the work of Stuart Davis, Leger and de Kooning. A quoted diary of a WPA muralist shows the Federal Art Project's commitment. Industrial designers such as Loewy and Dreyfuss, who redesigned refrigerators and telephones, show how the fair's machines were made presentable, while bridges, skyscrapers and streamlined trains show the thirties faith in progress. Gelernter compares the calm 1939 reaction to television with present-day alarm and concludes that the split between art and technology has alienated us from the future. The chapter ends with the Futurama's Polyrhetor, a twenty-ton sound system that the New York Times called the outstanding technological contribution of the fair.

14Dynamite, Manhattan, 1939p. 209

"Dynamite, Manhattan, 1939" opens with Life's 1939 portrait of America and a photograph of an unguarded dynamite store on a New York construction site, and asks why people then could be trusted with such risks. Gelernter's answer is that 1939 lived in an ought culture, in which daily conduct followed rules that people kept because they were expected to. The chapter catalogues these rules: how to greet a stranger, when to remove a hat, who stands for whom, which clothes suit which occasions, and how porters, filling-station attendants and housecall doctors served the public. Emily Post's 1937 etiquette book, film scenes and a La Guardia anecdote show that such rules were enforced and not merely decorative. Gelernter then turns to the larger oughts governing sex, divorce and contraception, and argues that good conduct is built by practice, as Theodore Roosevelt and traditional Judaism both suggest. The chapter closes by setting these habits against present-day ones, arguing that the loss of small oughts helps explain why the dynamite could no longer be left unguarded.

20The Landscape and Its Sanctityp. 273

Gelernter argues that Americans in the 1930s believed they could control both the landscape and the future, and that this confidence has since drained away. The evidence is the decade's building programme: New York's parkways, bridges and tunnels, Boulder Dam, the Golden Gate Bridge, the planned town of Radburn and Margaret Bourke-White's photograph of Fort Peck Dam under construction. Architects' drawings, Lewis Hine's photograph of an engineer on a skyscraper and the fair's slogan 'Building the World of Tomorrow' show an almost spiritual faith in building. Set against this is a present-day view of nature that leaves people passive, illustrated by the decades that passed before the West Side highway, which collapsed in 1974, was replaced and by the stalled debate over public toilets in New York. The result is a busy society that goes nowhere, a 'postutopian' condition in which activity adds up to nothing. The chapter closes by doubting that a nation which has given up its claims on space can keep its claims on time.

28Epilogue: Herein Utopiap. 339

The epilogue has three parts. The first traces the New York fair's history from the 1934 businessmen's meetings and the rival origin stories of Grover Whalen and Joseph Shadgen, through its financing, its deal with Robert Moses to turn the Flushing Meadow ash dump into a park, and the attendance and debts of the 1939 and 1940 seasons. 10World’s Fair Competitionp. 116

Allwood compares the New York World's Fair of 1939-40 with the Golden Gate International Exposition on Treasure Island in San Francisco, which opened in the same year. The New York fair, with its theme of 'The World of Tomorrow', the Trylon and Perisphere, its planned sectors and the reclaimed Corona Dumps site, is described first, along with the Aquacade, Railroads on Parade and the Swedish and Finnish pavilions. The chapter argues that the ambition for planned sectors and a unified architectural style was undone by exhibitors who ignored the plan, by loud flags and murals, and by an organizing body with little authority. The San Francisco fair is then set against it: an island built for the Bay Bridge and an airport, a site walled against the wind, a colour scheme that combined Guerin's 1915 palette with Urban's 1933 colours, and the Tower of the Sun. The chapter ends as war closes the fair and Treasure Island becomes a naval station.

Wikipedia

The 1939 New York World's Fair (also known as the 1939–1940 New York World's Fair) was an international exposition held at Flushing Meadows–Corona Park in Queens, New York City, United States. The fair featured exhibitions, activities, performances, films, artworks, and food presented by 62 nations, 35 U.S. states and territories, and more than 1,400 organizations and companies. Slightly over 45 million people attended across two seasons. Themed to "the world of tomorrow" and promoted with the slogan "Dawn of a New Day", the 1,202-acre (486 ha) fairground was divided into seven color-coded zones and two standalone focal exhibits, with approximately 375 buildings. Plans for the fair were first announced in September 1935, and the New York World's Fair Corporation (WFC) began construction in June 1936.
