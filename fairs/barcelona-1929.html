Barcelona International Exposition

Exposición Internacional de Barcelona

The Exposición Internacional de Barcelona opened on 19 May 1929 on the Montjuïc hill and ran until January 1930 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253). It stood beside the Seville Ibero-American Exposition, and the two were together called the General Exposition of Spain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254). Historians study it for its illuminated spectacle, Mies van der Rohe's German pavilion and the afterlife of the site, which hosted the 1992 Olympic Games (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Roche 2000p. 143).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair began as an electrical project. Civic leaders in Barcelona planned a second fair, the Electrical Industries Exhibition, for 1913 to celebrate the city's electrification, but the First World War halted it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251). Allwood and colleagues instead date the idea to a group of electrical manufacturers who proposed an international electrical exhibition in 1913 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 103). Palmer gives the initiative to the city, which began planning the fair in 1913 to mark its recent electrification and industrialisation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252).

After the war the project was revived for political reasons. General Miguel Primo de Rivera's military government wanted an event that would glorify Barcelona and win favour with European leaders (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Findling adds that Spanish industry, which had not recovered from the wars in Cuba and the Philippines, needed a showcase (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251). Allwood and colleagues describe a trial International Exhibition of Furniture planned for 13 September 1923, which was overtaken when the monarchy fell to a military dictatorship. The new rulers widened the scope to industry, sport and Spanish art, to be held with Seville as one general Spanish exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105).

Organisation and finance

The texts disagree about who paid. Findling says the Barcelona city government and the Spanish national government both financed the fair, and that the national government expected it to play well in European diplomatic circles (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251). Palmer says the city financed it while the national government backed it for diplomatic reasons (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Allwood and colleagues say King Alphonso XIII accepted the presidency, which placed the enterprise under central government control (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105).

The figures differ as well. Palmer says the fair electrified Spain's eastern coast for almost nine months (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Findling's dates give about eight months (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253), while Allwood and colleagues' table records six months open and attendance of 5,800,000, with a loss but no amount (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). The texts also differ on Seville's dates: Allwood and colleagues date it to 1930 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105), while Findling and Palmer give 1929-30 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255).

Site and architecture

The fair covered about 300 acres on Montjuïc, a hill formerly the site of a grim prison where executions were common (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251). Pedro Domenech headed a team of regional architects who laid out avenues, museums, water gardens and a 60,000-seat stadium, and the area became a permanent city park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Allwood and colleagues give the site as 291½ acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). Palmer describes the Plaza de España as the main entrance, with the Avenida de la Reina María Cristina leading to the Palacio Nacional, the primary pavilion (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252).

The German pavilion, designed by the architect Mies van der Rohe, who is described as a Bauhaus architect (the German design school of the 1920s), was the best-known foreign building (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254). Findling says it was built of travertine, marble, onyx and glass and became the prototype for a generation of architects; it was dismantled after the fair and rebuilt in the 1980s (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253). Palmer adds that a replica was built in the 1980s (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254).

Exhibits and attractions

Officially the fair was about industry, Spanish art and sport, and "the spotlight shone brightest on industry" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Eleven pavilions showed Spanish and foreign industrial achievements in textiles, chemicals, electricity, agriculture and city planning (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 252). The Palacio Nacional held the Arte de España, a display of Spanish art from the cave paintings of Altamira to the early seventeenth century, with 15,000 objets d'art (decorative art objects) (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252).

The Pueblo Español, or Spanish village, was a set of replicas of 320 buildings, the product of ten years of research, where swordmakers, silversmiths and leatherworkers worked during the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254). Greenhalgh reads it as a tourist image: the village was built to sell a particular vision of the Spanish nation to a Europe that seemed reluctant to visit (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105).

Lighting was the feature the texts stressed most. Coloured lights lit the central fountain and cascades, and searchlight beams rose behind the Palacio Nacional each night (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253). Allwood and colleagues describe the Art Deco (streamlined decorative style) glass lights leading up to the illuminated fountain, and say that they "were never to be surpassed" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). Rydell and colleagues say the Spanish fairs of 1929 used coloured light effectively on buildings and fountains, and that Chicago's designers later went further with gas tube lighting (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Palmer describes the Spanish Village as "precise replicas" of 320 homes, churches and plazas (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254). Greenhalgh describes a village of examples of Spanish vernacular building, constructed wholly from authentic materials, which was kept after the exposition and survived as the only village from the whole exhibition tradition (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105). The two accounts differ on what the village was made of, and neither says how far the reconstructions followed particular originals.

The National Palace, which held the Arte de España, was originally called the Central Palace. Findling says that it also staged musical performances featuring world-famous composers, among them the Brazilian Heitor Villa-Lobos (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251).

Nations and private exhibitors

Official participation was European: fourteen nations took part officially (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Japan and the United States were represented by private exhibitors rather than by their governments (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). The texts say little more about foreign displays.

Aftermath and legacy

The fair changed the city. The metro was moved underground, a new railway station was built, and the city hosted parades, bullfights, theatre, Zeppelin flights and conferences (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253). Findling says the city was left with an improved international reputation, but that the fair "did not bring about any lasting economic benefits of significance" because the Great Depression of the early 1930s followed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253).

The site outlived the fair. Greenhalgh says the Spanish Village was kept after the exposition and remains on the Montjuïc hillside (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105). Roche records that the 1929 exposition helped create a second park in the city (Roche 2000p. 146). The new stadium was to host the Workers' Olympics of 1936, an anti-fascist event, but the Spanish Civil War began on the morning of the opening ceremony and the event moved to Antwerp (Roche 2000p. 107). The stadium was later used for the 1992 Olympics, and the media centres were located in the 1929 exposition area (Roche 2000p. 143; Roche 2000p. 149).

The Montjuïc stadium was refurbished rather than replaced for the 1992 Games. The local organising group resisted IOC pressure to build a new large stadium and merely refurbished the old expo and Workers' Olympics stadium on Montjuïc hill (Roche 2000p. 144). Roche also places the Olympic media centres in the 1929 fair area. The Main Press Center and the International Broadcasting Center were both located centrally there, and the Games had nearly 5,000 press personnel and nearly 8,000 television and radio personnel (Roche 2000p. 149).

The National Palace had a later career as well. The entry on the palace says it was used as the press office for the 1992 Summer Olympic Games and remains an important tourist site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253). Geppert's appendix table lists the Palacio Nacional as surviving and refurbished for the 1992 Olympics (Geppert 2010p. 259). The same table lists the park, the smaller pavilions, the Via Reina María Cristina and the Plaça d'Espanya as the fair's residual features (Geppert 2010p. 259). Rydell and colleagues count the "monumental structures that survive in Barcelona" among the lasting evidence of the interwar fairs, beside those at Wembley, Brussels, Paris and Dallas (Rydell 1993p. 221).

The fair in the sequence of exhibitions

Rydell and colleagues list Barcelona among the fairs that the British Empire Exhibition set off in quick succession, with Seville in 1930, Antwerp, Liège, Paris in 1931 and Brussels in 1935 (Rydell 1993p. 3). Geppert shows the cost of that sequence. When Great Britain and other invited nations asked for more time, they pointed to the rapid succession of comparable events, Barcelona (1929-30) among them, and the Paris colonial exposition was postponed six times, by eleven years in all (Geppert 2010p. 181).

Findling separates the two Spanish fairs by purpose. Barcelona concentrated on world-wide commerce, while Seville emphasised Spain's cultural ties with its former colonies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). Allwood and colleagues call Seville a relatively modest affair beside Barcelona, whose lighting they call one of the most spectacular events of the inter-war period (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). Maloney notes that Barcelona and Seville were among the 1929 fairs that led to the creation in 1928 of the Bureau of International Expositions, which was charged with reducing the number and raising the quality of world's fairs (Maloney 2012p. 156).

How historians have interpreted it

Findling and Palmer both place the fair within Spanish diplomacy under Primo de Rivera (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). They part on its economic meaning. Palmer calls the fair "a celebration of light" that symbolised the economic transformation of Catalonia and says it is remembered more for culture than for the industry it stressed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Findling concludes that it brought no lasting economic benefit (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253).

Allwood and colleagues stress the state's rewriting of the project under the dictatorship (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). Greenhalgh reads the Spanish Village as national image-making for tourists (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105). Epps, whom Della Coletta cites, argues that Barcelona turned sites of repression into spaces of diversion, but Della Coletta contends that the change is only a matter of surface, since the rules of diversion were as strict as those of state discipline (Della Coletta 2006p. 275).

Caramellino and colleagues read fairs as long-term drivers of city change, and they cite the Barcelona expansion that ended with the 1992 works as an example (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 311; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 311). On architecture, Rydell and colleagues treat Barcelona and Seville as precedents for Chicago's modernist design and lighting (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Roche disputes Rydell's claim that Wembley needed modernist architecture to win a mass public, arguing that avant-garde forms previewed at Paris 1925 and Barcelona 1929 would not have made Wembley more popular (Roche 2000p. 64). Ganz shows how Barcelona's old Spanish town served as a model for the Old European Village planned for Chicago (Ganz 2008).

The texts agree that the lighting and the German pavilion mattered most, and they disagree on whether the fair's meaning lay in its economic promise or in its diplomatic and cultural display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 254; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 313).

Marina Muñoz Torreblanca, writing on exhibitions in Spain, argues that Spanish fairs had less impact in Europe than those of Great Britain or France, mainly because most of them were national or sector-specific (Filipová 2015p. 46). She names the Barcelona and Seville Spain International Exhibition of 1929 as one of only two international exhibitions Spain has held, the other being the 2008 water exhibition at Zaragoza (Filipová 2015p. 47). Allwood and colleagues, by contrast, describe the Barcelona show as the General Spanish Exhibition, a national enterprise under royal and then dictatorial control (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). The two texts therefore disagree about what kind of event the fair was. Muñoz Torreblanca treats it as an international exhibition, while Allwood and colleagues place it within the state's programme of Spanish display.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Our texts say little about an East, South or Southeast Asian presence at the Barcelona fair. Fourteen European nations took part officially, and the only Asian country the texts name is Japan, which was represented by private exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). The texts do not say what Japanese exhibitors showed, who paid for their displays or how they were received.

The texts describe no Asian pavilion, goods, performers or people on display at the fair. They record no Asian visitors, commissioners or observers, and they offer no scholarly reading of an Asian presence in Barcelona (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252). Beyond Japan, they name no Asian country and no colony.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Light and water spectacle — Lighting and the illuminated fountain are the features the texts describe most vividly. Palmer reads them as a symbol of Catalonia's economic change (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Allwood et al. 2001p. 105; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253).
  • Montjuïc and its afterlife — The hill became a permanent park and stadium, planned for the 1936 Workers' Olympics and used for the 1992 Games (Roche 2000p. 107; Roche 2000p. 143).
  • Industry versus culture — The fair was officially about industry, Spanish art and sport, but Palmer says it is remembered for its culture, above all the Arte de España and the Pueblo Español (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253).
  • Diplomacy under dictatorship — Primo de Rivera's government sought European favour and a showcase for Spanish industry (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 251; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 252; Allwood et al. 2001p. 105).
  • Modernist architecture — The German pavilion by Mies van der Rohe became a prototype, and the texts treat Barcelona as a precedent for Chicago's modernism (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Spanish Village and national image — The village was built to sell a vision of Spain to a reluctant Europe and survived the fair (Greenhalgh 1988p. 105).
  • Economic outcome — The city gained reputation but no lasting economic benefit, and the table's loss has no amount (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 253; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Questions for study

  1. How far did the dictatorship's diplomatic aims shape what the fair chose to show, and do Findling and Palmer weigh those aims the same way?
  2. Why did lighting receive more attention than any other feature, and what did electric spectacle mean in a city that had only recently electrified its industry?
  3. Does the survival of the Spanish Village and the Montjuïc stadium show continuity between the fair's aims and the later city, or a break from them?
  4. If Barcelona turned sites of repression into spaces of diversion, as Epps argues, did that change hide continuities of control, as Della Coletta contends?
  5. How should the fair's economic outcome be judged when one table records a loss without an amount and an encyclopedia entry finds no lasting economic benefit at all?
  6. Why did Chicago's organisers take Barcelona's old Spanish town as a model, and what does this show about how fair designs travelled between countries?
  7. Did the fair's commercial programme, as Findling describes it, or its display of Spanish culture, as Palmer describes it, matter more to its organisers, and how far does Allwood's view of Seville as modest change that answer?
  8. Should the 1929 fair be counted as an international exhibition, as Muñoz Torreblanca counts it, or as a general Spanish exhibition, as Allwood and colleagues do, and what depends on that label?
  9. What counts as authentic in the Spanish Village: a set of replicas, as Palmer describes it, or a village built from authentic materials, as Greenhalgh does?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
9Between the Warsp. 96

Allwood surveys the exhibitions of the 1920s and 1930s. It begins with the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924-25, where the stadium, the Neverstop Railway and the amusement park are described, and where advertising designers, more than architects, gave the event its character. The chapter moves to Paris 1925, whose Art Deco name it traces, and to Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau, which was fenced off and nearly denied its prize. Barcelona 1929 and Mies van der Rohe's German pavilion, with its Barcelona chair, follow, along with the 1928 convention that tried to limit how often exhibitions were held. The argument is that these events were increasingly designed for commerce and display rather than education, and that their losses mounted. Chicago's Century of Progress of 1933-34, with its science pavilions and the Sky Ride, is treated at length. The chapter ends with Paris 1937, where the German and Soviet pavilions faced one another across the Champ de Mars, and where the approach of war ended the exhibition's run.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Filipová, Marta, ed. Cultures of International Exhibitions 1840–1940: Great Exhibitions in the Margins. Farnham: Ashgate, 2015.
14The Last Exhibition of the Italian Colonial Empire: Naples 1938–40Giovanni Arenap. 313

Arena examines the Mostra Triennale delle Terre Italiane d'Oltremare, a colonial exhibition planned for Naples that opened on 9 May 1940, less than a month before Italy entered the Second World War and the show closed. He places it in a sequence of Italian colonial shows that begins with Tripoli's trade fair of 1927 and the Turin colonial exhibition of 1928, and continues with the Antwerp colonial exposition of 1930, where the regime stressed imperial Rome and modernised colonial economies. The Naples site in the Fuorigrotta district combined pavilions, gardens planted with colonial species and a plan meant to renew the city, while its historical, geographical and manufacturing sections used photomontages, ethnographic recreations and a 46-metre Fascist Party tower. Arena draws on the bylaws, the artists involved, especially Melkiorre Melis and his Libya pavilion café, and archival drawings. He concludes that the propaganda depended on the close integration of architecture and art, and that much of that decoration was destroyed in the bombing of the war.

Findling's encyclopedia entry treats the 1929-30 Barcelona fair as one half of the General Exposition of Spain, held alongside Seville. It argues that the fair was a state-backed diplomatic and industrial showcase: the Barcelona city and the Spanish government financed it, and General Primo de Rivera's dictatorship hoped it would win favour with European leaders and display Spanish industry. The entry concludes that the fair improved Barcelona's international reputation and its streets and railways but brought no lasting economic gain once the Depression began, and it names the Pueblo Espanol and the German pavilion by Mies van der Rohe as the two features the city remembers.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
entry in the section on fairs of 1924-38, printed pp. 104-106 (between running heads 103 and 105)

Allwood, Allan and Reid's survey gives the Barcelona International Exhibition a short entry that traces its changing purpose. It argues that an electrical exhibition conceived in 1913 was delayed by the First World War and then turned by the post-war Spanish government into a general Spanish exhibition of industry, sport and Spanish art. The authors single out the fair's lighting and its illuminated fountain as its most striking feature, and they describe the Seville fair held alongside it as a modest affair. Their statistics table records attendance of 5,800,000 and a loss without giving the amount.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

Brief discussion 6

Della Coletta, Cristina. World’s Fairs Italian-Style: The Great Expositions in Turin and Their Narratives, 1860–1915. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2006.
notes to pp. 20-4, note 11 (p. 275)

In a note on exhibition sites, Della Coletta cites Brad Epps on the Barcelona fairs of 1888 and 1929, which were sited on places of repression, the Citadel and Montjuic, and which Barcelona turned into spaces of diversion. Della Coletta contends that this change is only a matter of surface, because the rules governing spectacle were as strict as those of state-sponsored discipline.

On the web

Wikipedia

The 1929 Barcelona International Exposition (also 1929 Barcelona Universal Exposition, or Expo 1929, officially in Spanish: Exposición Internacional de Barcelona 1929) was the second World Fair to be held in Barcelona, the first one being in 1888. It took place from 20 May 1929 to 15 January 1930 in Barcelona, Spain. It was held on Montjuïc, the hill overlooking the harbor, southwest of the city center, and covered an area of 118 hectares (291.58 acres) at an estimated cost of 130 million pesetas ($25,083,921 in United States dollars). Twenty European nations participated in the fair, including Germany, Britain, Belgium, Denmark, France, Hungary, Italy, Norway, Romania and Switzerland. In addition, private organizations from the United States and Japan participated.
