Antwerp International Exhibition

Exposition Internationale d'Anvers
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Antwerp International Exhibition (Exposition Internationale d'Anvers) ran from 5 May to 5 November 1894 in ‘t Zuid, on the site of the city's 1885 fair (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). The texts give its attendance as about three million or about five million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133; Findling & Pelle 2008). Historians use it for three things: its Congo pavilion and a group of Congolese displayed in a village, its reconstructed sixteenth-century street Old Antwerp, which became a model for later Belgian fairs, and its place in Belgium's claim to imperial standing (Findling & Pelle 2008; Filipová 2015p. 190; Roche 2000p. 58).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The second Antwerp fair grew out of the first. A private Antwerp group that had supported the 1885 exposition had, in 1891, considered another international exhibition, planned by Janssens as an administrator of that group (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). The plan was to follow Chicago 1893, so that European exhibitors returning from America could show their goods on the Continent, and to celebrate the growth of Antwerp's maritime installations (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Stanard's encyclopedia entry says the fair was meant to promote the city, to show Belgium's advanced industrialisation, and to strengthen Antwerp's place as a major shipping centre (Findling & Pelle 2008). Stanard argues that Antwerp was already a leading European port but had not yet become a major importer of colonial goods, a role it gained only later in the century (Findling & Pelle 2008). For that reason Antwerp's middle classes still had good reason in 1894 to promote their city as a shipping centre (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Organisation, finance and site

Stanard writes that a short-term limited company planned the 1894 fair, much as it had planned 1885, and that Catholics and Liberals were both well represented in it. The quoted sentence reads: "The 1894 fair was directed by a group largely similar to the one that organized the 1885 exposition" (Findling & Pelle 2008). The same entry notes that every later Belgian world's fair was organised and financed by a temporary limited company (Findling & Pelle 2008). The budget was between 4 and 4.5 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008). Investors in the company made a profit of 15 to 25 per cent, and the city gave around 500,000 francs, more than it had given for 1885, while the provincial government contributed comparatively little (Findling & Pelle 2008). Two officials are named in the appendices: Count of Ursel as commissioner general, and Count of Pret Roose as president of the executive committee (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 386; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419).

The fair stood on the 1885 site, and some pavilions from that earlier exposition were reused (Findling & Pelle 2008). The site covered about 290,000 square metres, of which about 110,000 were built over, for 32 participating nations and 12,095 exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron's dictionary entry gives 26 nations for the same number of exhibitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133).

The Congo pavilion and the anti-slavery displays

By 1894 Belgium had become a colonial power, and the Congo was its largest colony (Findling & Pelle 2008). Stanard counts the Congo exhibit among the fair's most remarkable displays (Findling & Pelle 2008). The pavilion held ivory sculptures, samples of rubber, copal, cotton, coffee and cocoa, and a diorama of railways and caravan routes (Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron adds that sixteen Belgian and seventeen Congolese artists made ivory sculptures, and that the pavilion had prefabricated modular houses and models of steamers for the Congo River (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Gendron's entry says: "Mostly due to the participation of the Independent State of the Congo, the Colonial Section of the Exposition attracted particular interest" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133).

The Congo display was tied to the anti-slavery campaign. The Mouvement antiesclavagiste exhibited portraits of Belgian heroes, instruments used by slave traders, and captured Arab booty (Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron links this to the Brussels anti-slavery conference of 1889, to the campaign that began in 1892, and to the spring 1894 victory of Lieutenant Dhanis over the Arab slavers of Zanzibar (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Gendron adds that public opinion had been reluctant to back the African campaign but soon changed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). The pavilion was guarded by soldiers of the Force Publique, the Congo's armed force (Findling & Pelle 2008); Gendron calls the guards black soldiers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133).

Old Antwerp and historical reconstruction

One of the fair's foremost attractions was Old Antwerp, a section of the grounds built to look like a neighbourhood of sixteenth-century Antwerp (Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron writes: "The most striking architectural exhibit was the replication of a neighborhood of old Antwerp" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Its builders used furnishings and utensils of the sixteenth century, craftsmen worked in period dress, and a curfew was sounded each evening at 10 p.m. by trumpets and drums (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133).

The success of Old Antwerp set a pattern. Stanard notes that future Belgian expositions would build similar historic neighbourhoods (Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron lists the Bruxelles-Kermesse of 1897 and 1910, the Vieux Liège of 1905, Old Belgium at Antwerp in 1930, Old Bruxelles in 1935 and Belgium 1900 at Brussels in 1958 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Depelchin treats Old Flanders, the reconstructed historic Flemish city at Ghent in 1913, as the successor of Old Antwerp, the Bruxelles-Kermesse and the Vieux Liège (Filipová 2015p. 190).

Geppert places Antwerp in a wider history of the retrospective principle, the reconstruction of a past neighbourhood inside an exhibition. Antwerp 1894 is listed with Bremen 1890, Amsterdam 1895 and Berlin 1896 as places where the principle was developed and popularised (Geppert 2010p. 52). Structural elements of one of the Antwerp halls were also reused at the Berlin exhibition of 1896 (Geppert 2010p. 44). A French journal quoted by Geppert, critical of the Berlin reconstruction, noted that Old Antwerp had given the Belgian fair its charm and judged the Berlin version much less successful (Geppert 2010p. 56).

Wilson Smith, writing on historic-city exhibits, counts Antwerp among the fairs that took up the costume-and-performance device first seen in Old Edinburgh, alongside Manchester, Berlin and Stockholm (Filipová 2015p. 218). He uses Geppert's idea of exhibitionary networks to explain how such attractions spread from site to site (Filipová 2015p. 218). For Smith, though, the local story stayed specific. He reads Antwerp's reconstruction as the celebration of "a bourgeois mercantile elite" (Filipová 2015p. 219), and he cites Alfons Thijs's essay Old Antwerp 1894, a Bourgeois Dream World (Filipová 2015p. 223; Filipová 2015p. 227). Thijs's essay is not among the texts, so its argument is not described here.

Peoples on display

The most direct human display was the Congolese village. Stanard writes that organisers brought in a group of 144 Congolese, ethnically mixed, together with mules, cows, pigs and other animals from the Congo (Findling & Pelle 2008). They were placed in a village where visitors could watch them go about everyday activities or swim in the nearby Bassin de Batelage (Findling & Pelle 2008). The entry adds: "Organizers made the Congolese available to scientists to be measured, photographed, and studied" (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Other displays of empire were also present. The British erected an Indian City, which Stanard describes as housing South Asian weavers and metalworkers who made goods before the public (Findling & Pelle 2008). A Belgian publication accused several European powers of being jealous of the Congo pavilion's success, and claimed that this jealousy had led them to occupy the rest of Africa (Findling & Pelle 2008). The American section was housed in a large Renaissance-style pavilion (Findling & Pelle 2008). The texts say little about the Asian displays beyond the British section; see the Asia section below.

Visitors and figures

The sources disagree on attendance. Gendron's dictionary entry gives some three million visitors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133), and the attendance table in Allwood, Allan and Reid's general history gives 3,000,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Stanard's encyclopedia gives approximately five million visitors between 5 May and 5 November (Findling & Pelle 2008). A footnote in Depelchin's chapter also gives five million, citing Drèze, against 3.5 million for the 1885 fair (Filipová 2015p. 197). The sources also differ on the number of nations, with 26 in Gendron's entry (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133) and 32 in Stanard's (Findling & Pelle 2008). The texts say little about how visitors reacted to the displays. The nearest evidence is Gendron's account of public opinion, which at first resisted the African campaign and then changed (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133).

The Congo pavilion's other exhibits

Gendron lists more in the pavilion than ivory and produce. The John Cockerill Society showed statues representing natives carrying pieces of a cannon and a modular boat (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Drawings by native children were displayed in one of the pavilion's courtyards (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). A native artist engraved on an ivory plate the signatures of the delegates of seventeen nations that had signed the antislavery act of Brussels of 2 July 1890 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). This ties the exhibit to the diplomacy of the anti-slavery campaign, not only to the colony's trade. Stanard names the two artists of the railways and caravan-routes diorama, Robert Mols and Henri Meunier (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The fair's records and sources

Gendron names two primary sources. Alphonse Hertogs, a councilman of Antwerp in charge of the city's public works and vice-president of the fair's executive committee, published an account in 1896 that gives a detailed budget of receipts and expenditures (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 134). Cockx and Lemmens's 1958 survey of Belgian fairs gives only a brief summary of this one (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 134). A student who wants to test the figure of 4 to 4.5 million francs (Findling & Pelle 2008) would need Hertogs's budget, since none of the texts gives figures from it. Stanard's bibliography lists three works on the Congo displays from 1894: the catalogue of the Congo Free State section; a communication by V. Jacques, Les Congolais de l'exposition universelle d'Anvers, made to the Brussels anthropological society on 24 September 1894; and a work by Ch. Lemaire, Congo & Belgique (Findling & Pelle 2008). The Jacques title shows that the Congolese on show were discussed in a scientific society in the year of the fair.

Streets of Cairo from Paris to Antwerp

A Cairo street was not new in 1894. Paris in 1889 had a reconstruction of one, with native dancers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 61). Chicago's Midway Plaisance, which the text says had no connection with the exposition proper, had a Street of Cairo with a theatre where the American public was astounded by the "genuine native muscle dance" of Egyptian dancers (Allwood et al. 2001p. 67; Allwood et al. 2001p. 67). The dancing led to a national craze for the hootchy-kootchy and to a tradition of sex-based sideshows at later American fairs (Allwood et al. 2001p. 67). Lukas says that the Streets of Cairo appeared again at Antwerp in 1894 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 400). His text gives no description of the Antwerp street, and the texts do not say whether it had dancers. The Paris and Chicago accounts can serve only as comparison.

How historians have interpreted it

The reference works are mainly descriptive. Stanard reads the fair chiefly through Antwerp's port ambitions and the need of its middle classes to promote the city (Findling & Pelle 2008), and treats the temporary company and Old Antwerp as precedents for later Belgian fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 2008). Gendron gives most space to the Congo and the anti-slavery story and offers little explicit interpretation (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Allwood, Allan and Reid name the fair only in a list of Belgian exhibitions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87).

Depelchin places the fair in a series of Belgian showcases after 1885 and argues that Old Antwerp was the precedent for the historicist Ghent fair of 1913 (Filipová 2015p. 184; Filipová 2015p. 190). Greenhalgh argues that Belgian expositions show Belgium's imperial consciousness, and lists Antwerp 1894 among those with extensive imperial sections. The detailed colonial account of Antwerp in that book refers to the 1930 fair (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). Roche argues that Belgium's four pre-1914 colonial expositions, Antwerp 1894 among them, were a major claim for recognition as an imperial nation (Roche 2000p. 58). Geppert treats the fair as one stage in the spread of the retrospective principle and argues that such borrowing made exhibitions more self-referential than tied to local circumstances (Geppert 2010p. 52; Geppert 2010p. 52). Lukas treats the Streets of Cairo, which reappeared at Antwerp in 1894, as an early case of place sampling that fed into later theme parks (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 400). Hoffenberg uses tea sales at exhibitions to argue for direct trade among imperial units without English middlemen (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118).

Stanard and Roche differ on purpose. Stanard stresses port ambition and local pride (Findling & Pelle 2008); Roche reads the fair as part of the Belgian state's claim to imperial standing (Roche 2000p. 58).

Greenhalgh's most detailed claims about Antwerp concern the 1930 fair, not 1894, and the essay's earlier citation of him should be read with that in mind. He describes the Antwerp and Liège fairs of 1930 as a joint celebration (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). The colonial display at Antwerp is called "the most ambitious Belgian effort ever" in that later context (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71). His account of the Palais du Congo's architecture (Greenhalgh 1988p. 71), and of a long list of colonial exhibit categories, which he says was out of character with most exhibitions held after the First World War (Greenhalgh 1988p. 72), belong to the same fair. The French decision to take part is tied to the Paris Internationale Coloniale, which was less than a year away (Greenhalgh 1988p. 73). The texts on the 1894 fair give no comparable detail on the Congo building.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts say little about an Asian presence at the Antwerp fair of 1894. The passages on this fair name no Japanese, Chinese, Siamese or other East or Southeast Asian display.

India

The clearest reference is to a British Indian City. Stanard's entry says that the British erected it and that it housed South Asian weavers and metalworkers who made goods in front of visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008). The entry does not name who organised or paid for it. It places this display in a wider competition among colonial powers at the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008).

Hoffenberg, writing on the tea trade, reports that Indian tea which remained after the Tea House at Chicago in 1893 closed in early 1894 was forwarded to the British Section at the Antwerp International Exhibition (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118). The Tea Association also displayed and sold tea at exhibitions including Antwerp during the following decade. The text gives no year for the Antwerp display (Hoffenberg 2001p. 116).

Hoffenberg also quotes Clarke, writing to Birdwood, who judged the Indian City at Antwerp, held the previous year, to be a success. Clarke gave as one reason the number of South Asian weavers and metalworkers, who made sale items in front of visitors at a reconstructed, imaginary point of artistic origin (Hoffenberg 2001p. 160). The year 1894 is inferred from the text, which dates the Empire Exhibition to 1895 and calls Antwerp's fair the previous year.

Other Asian presence

The Dutch colonies are mentioned among the colonial displays at Antwerp, but the entry names no Dutch colony (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 133). Hoffenberg draws the broader argument from the Indian tea trade: that tea sales at fairs made direct trade between India, Australia and other imperial units visible, without English middlemen (Hoffenberg 2001p. 118).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Congo pavilion — The texts treat the Congo pavilion as the fair's colonial centre, with ivory, colonial produce and anti-slavery exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Old Antwerp — A reconstructed sixteenth-century street became the model for later Belgian fairs' historic neighbourhoods, and for Ghent's Old Flanders in 1913 (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Filipová 2015).
  • Peoples on display — The 144 Congolese in a village, open to measurement and photography by scientists, are the clearest case of human display (Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Organisers and finance — A temporary limited company ran the fair, with a budget of 4 to 4.5 million francs and a city subsidy of about 500,000 francs (Findling & Pelle 2008).
  • Imperial claims — Two texts read Belgium's expositions as efforts to show imperial standing, with Antwerp 1894 among them (Roche 2000; Greenhalgh 1988).
  • Transnational borrowing — The retrospective displays and Streets of Cairo travelled between fairs, from Antwerp to Berlin and later to theme parks (Geppert 2010; Hollengreen et al. 2014).
  • Indian tea and crafts — Indian tea and an Indian City of South Asian craftsmen appeared at Antwerp, though the texts say little about either (Hoffenberg 2001; Findling & Pelle 2008).

Questions for study

  1. The Congolese were placed in a village where scientists could measure and photograph them. What do the texts suggest about how organisers and visitors understood that display?
  2. Old Antwerp was copied by later Belgian fairs. What does this pattern of historic reconstruction suggest about how cities wanted to present themselves?
  3. Roche reads Belgium's colonial expositions as a claim to imperial standing. How far does the evidence about 1894 support that reading, and where does it rest on the later fairs?
  4. Geppert presents the Antwerp displays as copied in Berlin. Why might a contemporary critic have judged the copy less successful, and what does that say about the medium?
  5. The reference works give attendance as three million and as five million. What should a student check before using either figure?
  6. Hoffenberg uses the tea trade to argue for trade without middlemen. Which of the texts' details about the Antwerp display support that argument, and which are missing?
  7. Hertogs's budget is the source for the fair's receipts and costs, but the texts give no figures from it. What would a student need to check before using the figure of 4 to 4.5 million francs in an argument about the fair's finances?
  8. Stanard's bibliography lists three 1894 works on the Congo displays, and Jacques's communication was made to a scientific society. What would those sources show about how the Congolese were presented, and how far do the reference works draw on them?
  9. Wilson Smith reads the Old Antwerp reconstruction as the celebration of a bourgeois mercantile elite, while Stanard reads the fair through the port ambitions of the city's middle classes. Do the two readings describe the same group, and what evidence would separate them?

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
6Eiffel’s Tower and Chicago’s White Cityp. 56

Allwood treats international exhibitions as public relations exercises in which each event had to outdo the last. The chapter sets two cases side by side. Paris 1889, held to mark the centenary of the Revolution, is discussed through the Eiffel Tower, which artists and writers opposed before it became a profitable structure for Eiffel, and through Dutert's Galerie des Machines, with its hinged steel roof. Electricity, Edison's phonograph and the colonial villages are described as the main attractions. Chicago 1893 is presented as a failure of nerve: its lakeside site laid out by Olmsted, its uniform Neo-classical Florentine architecture, and the criticism of Louis Sullivan and the Belgian engineer Vierendeel are set against the Midway, the Ferris Wheel and the Cairo street. The chapter argues that Chicago's classicism shaped American public and business architecture for decades. It closes with the San Francisco Midwinter Exposition of 1894, which carried Chicago's exhibits and style westwards.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Geppert, Alexander C. T. Fleeting Cities: Imperial Expositions in Fin-de-Siècle Europe. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010.

Geppert's study of Berlin's 1896 trade exhibition argues that reconstructed old-city scenes ('retrospective' displays) were copied from one exhibition to the next, and names Antwerp 1894 as one place where the principle was developed. He notes that large structural elements of one of the Antwerp 1894 halls were reused in Berlin, and he quotes a French critic who said that 'Old Anvers' gave the Belgian fair its charm while Berlin's copy was less successful. The argument is that such borrowing made the exhibition medium more self-referential than locally focused. Antwerp 1894 is one stop in this transnational account.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Geppert studies five imperial expositions in Berlin, Paris and London between 1896 and 1931 (Berlin 1896, Paris 1900, London 1908, Wembley 1924, Vincennes 1931), arguing that they formed overlapping transnational networks in which organisers borrowed pavilions, colonial villages and old-town sections from one another. Against the 'identity' reading of national display, he proposes a method of space, time and personae and treats each exposition as a medium. Chapters 2 to 6 are chronological case studies, each built around one fair, its site and its impresario or planners. Chapter 7 traces 'exhibition fatigue' from German debates to the 1928 convention that created the Bureau International des Expositions, and proposes a theory of exposition practice; a short coda considers the photographs, postcards, posters and films that fixed the fairs.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Wikipedia

The Antwerp International Exposition (Dutch: Wereldtentoonstelling van Antwerpen; French: Exposition internationale d'Anvers) of 1894 was a world's fair held in Antwerp, Belgium, between 5 May and 5 November 1894. It covered 65.5 acres (265,000 m2), attracted 3 million visits and made a profit. It took place at the same location as the 1885 exposition.
