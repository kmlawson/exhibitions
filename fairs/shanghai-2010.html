Shanghai World Exposition (Expo 2010)

2010年上海世界博览会
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, called Expo 2010 in several texts, was held on both banks of the Huangpu River under the theme Better City, Better Life (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 408; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11). The Chinese state presented it as a celebration of national prestige after the 2008 Beijing Olympics (Hurp. 1). Visitor figures run from 73.1 million to more than 75 million (Peterson 2020p. 279; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Two texts stress the fair's role in urban renewal and in the displacement of residents and factories from the riverfront (Peterson 2020p. 281; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The organisers' winning bid to the Bureau of International Expositions (BIE) offered the theme A Better City, a Better Life and claimed that the fair would be the first officially recognised world exposition held in a developing country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 407). The fair's website, as quoted by Hollengreen and Rouse, called it "The first of its kind staged in a developing country", with 246 participating countries and 73 million visitors (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11). Hollengreen and Rouse read the theme as a continuation of the classic idea of the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11). The encyclopedia ties the fair to the thirty-second year since China's reforms began in 1978 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 409).

Hur places the fair beside two earlier moments: the centennial of the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910, China's first national exhibition, and Liang Qichao's 1902 novel, which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062 (Hurp. 1). Hur says the novel reads "as if it were a prophecy of the Shanghai Exposition of 2010" (Hurp. 1).

Organisers and finance

The texts do not agree on cost. The encyclopedia, writing before the fair opened, projected a total of about $3.6 billion, with $2.75 billion for construction and $1.35 billion for operations, paid for by the central and Shanghai governments, bonds and bank loans (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 409). Caramellino and colleagues give an appropriation of 2.8 billion euros (28.6 billion yuan) for regeneration (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 320), and Knowles says the central government was ready to spend $45 billion on the grounds and subway lines (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211). Peterson reports an estimated income of 13.01 billion yuan (US$2.3 billion) and a profit (Peterson 2020p. 280), while his footnote cites a 2011 report of a surplus of 1.05 billion yuan (US$164 million) (Peterson 2020p. 280). Aelbrecht says the organisers planned an expo that would also meet the city's master plan (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 496), and that the Lisbon development company Parque Expo was invited to advise Shanghai (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 485).

Site and buildings

Planners chose a declining district of old factories, warehouses and homes along both banks of the Huangpu (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 408). Knowles writes that the site held the country's largest and oldest steelmaking and shipbuilding plants. Ordinarily, he says, the fate of such state-owned factories would be decided in Beijing, but Shanghai insisted the site was the best one for the fair and won the argument (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211). Caramellino and colleagues say that obsolete factories, shipyards and the Baosteel mill were moved to the periphery and that six new subway lines were built (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 320). Peterson counts five new subway stations (Peterson 2020p. 280). Before the fair, the encyclopedia gave the grounds as more than 795 acres, with five theme pavilions on urban concerns (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 409).

Exhibits and attractions

Knowles describes the China Pavilion as celebrating traditional art and architecture, while its central film presented a contemporary, state-managed China (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Pavilions from Venezuela to Mozambique to Uzbekistan showed their leaders shaking hands with the Chinese president Hu Jintao (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211). Denmark built its pavilion around a recreation of the Little Mermaid (Peterson 2020p. 291). The Mexican Pavilion put an at-grade installation called Kite Forest in place of a large building, and hung the mask of the Dance of the Old Men from Jarácuaro below ground, among some thirty other faces and heads (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 73). Mexico also sold a fifteen-day tourist package showcasing Aztec and Mayan culture (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 76). The General Motors pavilion, designed by Rong Wujie, was a crowd favourite (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 192). The US pavilion, Knowles says, offered teamwork and the victory of the little guy, with cowboy hats and basketballs for sale and no mention of democracy (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211).

Who took part and visitors

Participation figures differ. Hollengreen and Rouse and Hur give 246 countries (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11; Hurp. 1), while Peterson gives 192 (Peterson 2020p. 290). Peterson names France, the United Kingdom and Australia among the Western states that spent heavily to present themselves at Shanghai (Peterson 2020p. 280). The visitor figures are Peterson's 73.1 million (Peterson 2020p. 279), Hollengreen and Rouse's 73 million (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11), and Knowles's more than 75 million, most of them Chinese from smaller cities and rural areas (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209). Rydell gives some 70 million, mostly Chinese (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Knowles puts Osaka 1970 at 62 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 210). Geppert, writing before the fair, expected Shanghai to break the records of Osaka 1970 (Geppert 2010p. 8).

Visitors and reception

Chinese visitors judged the US pavilion a success, but reactions in the US press were mixed, and corporate sponsorship reached about $61 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211). Peterson calls the US pavilion's English-language videos preachy (Peterson 2020p. 290). Knowles reports that dissent was muted but present, especially over the forced relocation of thousands of residents (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211). He also records a Chinese critic's rendering of the theme as a promise that the city would improve life (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 210).

Politics and controversy

Peterson says that the land "required the forcible displacement of an estimated 18,000 families" (Peterson 2020p. 281). The encyclopedia, before the fair, had reported that residents were promised relocation to new neighbourhoods elsewhere in the city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 408). The two sources describe different moments: a promise made before the fair, and a displacement that Peterson reports afterwards.

Aftermath and legacy

Australian politicians later held up Brisbane's Expo 88 as a model for the use of an expo site after Shanghai (Peterson 2020p. 143). Aelbrecht records that Shanghai followed the Lisbon model most closely, and that housing was left out of the post-fair plan (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 495p. 496). Since 2010 the BIE has held conferences on best urban practice (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 496). An official told Knowles that there would never be another world's fair in China (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 212). Knowles argues that Dubai 2020 rests on a similar set of arguments and political economy (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 212).

Its place in the series

The encyclopedia's Zaragoza entry places Shanghai 2010 after Aichi 2005 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 404). Peterson argues that Asia, beginning with Osaka 1970 and more recently Shanghai 2010, has taken the role of great host from the West (Peterson 2020p. 280). Hollengreen and Rouse date the rise of the fair in Asia to Osaka 1970 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 10). Another chapter of the volume gives more than 135 million visitors across Hanover, Aichi, Shanghai and Milan (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 192).

How historians have interpreted it

Peterson argues that the fairs now sell the nation more than they represent it (Peterson 2020p. 279). Hellier-Tinoco argues that the living body is treated as the final guarantee of authenticity, and that at Shanghai the body was suggested and then erased, leaving the mask as an icon of exchange value (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 77). Caramellino, De Magistris and Deambrosis argue that the success of Shanghai 2010 celebrates an instrument tied to the city's nineteenth-century urban culture (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 320). Knowles describes the world's fair as a theatre for enacting future visions rooted in host nations' politics (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 212). Denton reads Shanghai's planning hall as a state vision of the city's future, and says that for Shanghai "Expo 2010 was its ticket to global city status" (Denton 2014p. 248p. 249).

Peterson gives one case from Southeast Asia. The Philippines country pavilion at the 2010 Shanghai exhibition was hijacked during a brief power vacuum after a change of government, when business leaders used it to advance their own commercial interests in China (Peterson 2020p. 28). Themes in the scholarship

  • National prestige and the state — The texts present the fair as proof of China's status after the Beijing Olympics (Hurp. 1), and Peterson argues that China could sell itself to its own large audience (Peterson 2020p. 290). Denton ties the planning halls' vision of the future to state policy (Denton 2014p. 248).
  • Urban renewal and displacement — The encyclopedia promised relocation before the event (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 408); Peterson and Knowles report displacement and removal of industry during it (Peterson 2020p. 281; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211).
  • Indigenous display and tourism — Mexico's pavilion displayed a mask without its body and promoted tourism at the same fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 76p. 77).
  • Corporate and national pavilions — General Motors's pavilion aimed to reinforce its brand (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 192), and the US pavilion promoted American corporate power, in Peterson's reading (Peterson 2020p. 290; Molella & Knowles 2019p. 211).
  • Food, security and crowd control — Entry checks and a ban on cooked food shaped picnicking at Shanghai, which Tran links to the milk scandal of the Beijing Olympics (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 443).

Questions for study

  1. Which visitor count should a student use, and what does each source count? The texts give 73.1 million (Peterson 2020p. 279), 73 million (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 11), more than 75 million (Molella & Knowles 2019p. 209) and an earlier projection of about 70 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 409).
  2. How does the promise of relocation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 408) square with the 18,000 families that Peterson says were displaced (Peterson 2020p. 281)?
  3. Does the Mexican pavilion show a living people turned into a commodity, as Hellier-Tinoco argues, or is that reading too strong? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 77).
  4. Was the riverside site chosen for its after-use, as Aelbrecht suggests, or for the city's master plan? (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 496).

Hollengreen, Laura, Celia Pearce, Rebecca Rouse and Bobby Schweizer, eds. Meet Me at the Fair: A World’s Fair Reader. Pittsburgh: ETC Press, 2014.
1.6Embodying Touristic Mexico: Virtual and Erased Indigenous BodiesRuth Hellier-Tinocop. 71

Hellier-Tinoco examines two Mexican exhibits from twenty-first century world's fairs: a 3D film of the Night of the Dead ritual on Janitzio island, shown at Hannover in 2000, and a suspended mask of the Dance of the Old Men from Jarácuaro, displayed at Shanghai in 2010. The chapter argues that both present indigenous P'urhépecha people as authentic, timeless and pre-industrial, when both were reshaped from the 1920s into national icons and tourist attractions. It traces how the Janitzio ritual and the dance were staged in Mexico City theatres, photographed, filmed and sold in guidebooks and postcards, and how the mask later appeared in the Mexican Tourist Board's European campaign of 2006. The chapter calls this process 'performism' and places it within the long history of indigenous bodies at fairs, which serve as the 'final authenticator'. The Hannover pavilion replaced the absent villagers with the bodies of visitors, and the Shanghai gallery kept the mask after the body had been erased, so the fairs turned indigenous display into economic exchange value.

5.4Beyond the Event: World’s Fairs and Urban Transformation Since WWIIGaia Caramellino, Alessandro De Magistris, Federico Deambrosisp. 311

Caramellino, De Magistris and Deambrosis argue that world's fairs should be studied not as enclosed spectacles but as urban transformations that continued long after the events closed. They trace a chronology from the late nineteenth-century exhibitions, which were closely tied to the growth of their host cities, to the postwar fairs, in which planning and architecture became central. Their main cases are Brussels 1958, where the fair helped drive the remaking of the city centre, and Turin 1961, whose durable buildings such as Nervi's Palazzo del Lavoro were linked to the urbanisation of a southern periphery. They also consider the New York fair of 1964-65, which the authors treat as a missed opportunity for urban planning, and Montreal 1967, whose infrastructure reshaped Ile Sainte-Hélène. The chapter then moves through Genoa, Seville and Lisbon in the 1990s and Shanghai 2010, where fairs served as triggers for port, waterfront and metropolitan regeneration. The authors end by noting that European fairs since 2000 have shifted toward smaller, more dispersed projects, while Shanghai 2010 carried the older model of large-scale city regeneration.

7.4A History of Picnics at World’s FairsVan Troi Tranp. 435

Tran argues that picnics, though a visible presence on fairgrounds, have been largely ignored in histories of world's fairs. Using press reports, official reports, caricatures and advice columns, the chapter reconstructs picnicking at Paris 1889 and 1900, Chicago 1893, New York 1964-65, Montreal 1967 and Shanghai 2010. It reads the picnic in several ways: as a social leveler that inverted class distinctions, with millionaires in the Eiffel Tower and workers on the lawns; as a thrifty alternative to crowded and costly restaurants, which guidebooks promoted; and as a source of sociability and calm amid the sensory strain of the fair. It also traces the market for picnic gear and box lunches. The sharpest episode is Auguste Escoffier's attack on picnicking in Paris, set against French ideas of national cuisine and contrasted with the pastoral, Sunday-school image of the American picnic. The chapter ends with Shanghai 2010, where airport-style checks barred cooked food and take-away meals for fear of food poisoning.

In the contents of this work

8.4A World Fair for the Future: A Study of the Legacy of the Expo ‘98 Urban ModelPatricia Simões Aelbrechtp. 485

Aelbrecht argues that Expo '98 marked a third model for world's fairs, after Paris 1889 and Brussels 1958: the fair as a catalyst of urban renewal, with its site planned from the start as a neighbourhood. The evidence comes from the archives of Parque Expo and of the Bureau International des Expositions, from interviews with the planners, and from a close reading of the plan's four public axes, its mixed-use zoning and its double-function public spaces. Aelbrecht shows how Parque Expo chose Vancouver, Brisbane, Seville and Barcelona as models and what it took from each. The second half turns to reception: a small audited loss, the railway line that cut the new district off from its western neighbours, a largely affluent population, and a model that has spread slowly, with Shanghai 2010 the closest follower. The chapter closes with two lessons for future expos: choose a site inside the city's strategic plan, and plan the post-use from the start.

Molella, Arthur P., and Scott Gabriel Knowles, eds. World’s Fairs in the Cold War: Science, Technology, and the Culture of Progress. Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2019.
16Does the World’s Fair Still Matter? Discovering New Worlds after 1989Scott Gabriel Knowlesp. 194Asia

Knowles asks whether the world's fair still matters after 1989, and examines the fairs planned during or after the Cold War, from Plovdiv in 1991 to Shanghai in 2010. The argument is that these fairs were shaped by the political changes of their moment and in turn helped to shape them, as host cities used them for urban renewal, national self-presentation and reform. The chapter moves fair by fair. Plovdiv's young-inventors expositions of 1985 and 1991 are read as a shift from Cold War showcase to a sign of Bulgarian transition, and Seville 1992, Lisbon 1998 and Hannover 2000 as bids for European integration, reunification and a sustainable image. Daejeon 1993 is presented as South Korea's 'new road' of democratic development, and Shanghai 2010 as a display of urban China. Knowles relies on press reports, Bureau of International Expositions assessments and the fairs' own films, and records the American difficulties at Seville and Hannover. The chapter ends by pointing to Dubai 2020 and a possible American return, presenting the fairs as theatres for national visions.

Peterson, William. Asian Self-Representation at World’s Fairs. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2020.
10Conclusionp. 279Asia

Peterson's conclusion looks beyond Milan to the future of national representation at world's fairs. He argues that the 2015 Milan Expo, with its cost overruns, unfinished pavilions and protests, suggests that the grand European exposition is unlikely to be revived. Asia has taken the lead as host, and Peterson weighs Shanghai 2010, with its 73 million visitors and forced displacement of residents, against Dubai 2020, whose site relied on migrant labor. The Chinese pavilion at Milan, with its animated family film and live Hani dancers, is read as a more effective affective encounter than the Korean and Thai pavilions. Drawing on Volcic and Andrejvic, Peterson proposes that states now 'nationalize the sell', so that future expositions are more likely to exploit national identity than to say anything meaningful about it. He ends by arguing that the analytical frameworks for studying these displays must change, though they will remain performative, visual and increasingly virtual.

PrefaceRobert W. Rydellp. i

Rydell's preface opens with Charlotte Brontë's 1851 response to the Crystal Palace and argues that fairs work by 'make-believe', persuading others to occupy a reality. It traces the fair movement from the Great Exhibition, set against the revolutions of 1848, to the Cold War and the Shanghai Expo. Exhibitions, Rydell argues, served elite authority, and women, minorities and workers contested them. The preface ends by introducing the volume's themed 'pavilions' of essays on picnics, architecture, theme parks and souvenirs.

Hur, Hyungju. “Staging Modern Statehood: World Exhibitions and the Rhetoric of Publishing in Late Qing China, 1851–1910.” Doctoral dissertation.
Introductionp. 1Asia

Hur opens with the Shanghai World Exposition of 2010, set against Liang Qichao's novel The Future of New China (1902), which imagined a Great Exhibition in Shanghai in 2062. The dissertation's contention is that the late Qing government's aim of showing modern statehood through world exhibitions was repeatedly challenged. Hur identifies three agents: the Imperial Maritime Customs, largely staffed by foreigners, which selected and shipped China's exhibits; Japan, which used the exhibitions to present itself as Asia's only modern state, including through its colony Taiwan; and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries, whose transnational press attacked the Qing government over national humiliation. The four chapters move from the early history of China's participation to the Osaka Exhibition of 1903, the St Louis Exposition of 1904 and the Nanyang Industrial Exposition of 1910. The argument is that the same objective was received differently as the historical context changed, and that the Nanyang exposition marked a brief reconciliation between the government and its critics.

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
9When the local is the global: case studies in early twentieth-century Chinese exposition projectsSusan R. Fernsebnerp. 173Asia

Fernsebner follows Chinese exposition managers, mostly state officials and commercial elites, through three fairs: St. Louis in 1904, China's first national exposition at Nanjing in 1910, and the Panama-Pacific fair in San Francisco in 1915. Her argument is that these elites sought control over how China was represented. At St. Louis they protested against displays arranged by the Western-staffed Qing Customs Service, including bound-foot shoes and opium pipes. At Nanjing they used the fair to promote economic nationalism and to train a 'new people', drawing on provincial displays of soap, furniture and textiles and on a hall for overseas Chinese. The San Francisco section centres on a complaint about the China Pavilion's teahouse and on 'Underground Chinatown', an attraction that the Six Companies and commissioner-general Chen Qi protested and that closed in March 1915. The chapter concludes that these disputes joined local, transnational and global concerns, and that the same dynamics recur in China's later hosting of Expo 2010.

The encyclopedia's entry on Shanghai 2010, by Susan Fernsebner, was written before the fair opened and describes it largely in the language of its organisers. It presents the exposition as a claim to be the first world's fair in a developing country and as a showcase of China's reform era. It sets the event against a planned riverside site of declining industry, and its argument is that the fair was meant to make the remaking of a city visible and to confirm China's place on the global stage. Because it predates the fair, it reports plans and projections rather than outcomes, and it notes that residents were promised relocation.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Meet Me at the Fair, edited by Laura H. Hollengreen and Rebecca Rouse, is a 2014 volume of new essays on world's fairs. The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. The essays range from London's Crystal Palace in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010, with most attention to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and to New York in 1939 and 1964-65. They are grouped under eight 'pavilions': anthropology and ethnology, American identity, science and industry, design and architecture, operations and urban planning, arts and media, amusements and recreation, and the future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, in about twenty chapters, almost always briefly. The one sustained treatment is chapter 8.2 (the Japan Pavilion), where Walters reads Japan's display as an industrial challenge to American manufacturers rather than a cultural exchange: national industrial rankings, the Shinkansen, Seiko's Tik-Tok, Sony's Videocorder, Matsushita and Hitachi advertising, and Toray's kimonos of synthetic fibre. The Shanghai Expo of 2010 recurs. Aelbrecht (8.4) calls Shanghai the closest follower of Lisbon's Expo '98 model, Caramellino (5.4) reads it as city regeneration, and Schrenk (3.3), Tran (7.4) and Hellier-Tinoco (1.6) mention its GM pavilion, its food rules and a Mexican mask in its gallery. Chinese exhibitors and visitors are rarely discussed in their own right. Older fairs show the region mainly as displayed peoples and places. Santesso (1.3) sees the unfinished Chinese exhibit at Philadelphia in 1876, with working carpenters, as living authenticity, and places Chicago's Javanese (1893) and Chinese (1933) villages in the zoo tradition. Robles (1.4) notes Tonkinese villagers at Paris in 1889, Crompton (2.3) lists Siam and Chinese markets on the Midway, Lukas (7.1) a Chinese theatre and an Igorot village, and Kamerbeek (6.5) the Filipino Village at Buffalo in 1901. Jacobson (6.2) discusses the Japanese Village in Edison's Buffalo films. Japan appears as a source of prints (Whitfield, 2.1) and of Osaka's Expo 70 (Pearce, 3.5), while Telotte (7.2) and Warren (7.5) mention Disney's Tokyo park and Epcot's Japan pagoda.

Peterson's book examines how independent Asian states sought to control their own representation at Western world's fairs between 1915 and 2015. Its case studies are San Francisco 1915, New York 1939-40, Brisbane 1988, New York 1964-65 and Milan 2015, each centred on the pavilions or concessions of Japan, China, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia or South Korea. The introduction argues that Japan, and to a lesser extent Thailand, were arguably the only Asian nations that consistently presented themselves, and proposes a move from a colonial order of display to a performative order of live encounters, drawing on Mitchell and Greenhalgh. About the work as a whole Asia: central

Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. Its argument is that the same official aim was received differently as context changed: the Osaka (1903) and St. Louis (1904) fairs became occasions of national humiliation in the Chinese press, while the Nanyang Industrial Exposition in Nanjing (1910) won brief support once the court promised constitutional reform. Wikipedia

Expo 2010, officially the Expo 2010 Shanghai China, was held on both banks of the Huangpu River in Shanghai, China, from 1 May to 31 October 2010. It was a major World Expo registered by the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), in the tradition of international fairs and expositions, the first since 2005. The theme of the exposition was "Better City – Better Life" and signifies Shanghai's new status in the 21st century as the "next great world city". The Expo emblem features the Chinese character 世 ('world', Chinese "shì") modified to represent three people together with the 2010 date. It had the largest number of countries participating and was the most expensive Expo in the history of the world's fairs. The Shanghai World Expo was also the largest World's Fair site ever at 5.28 square km.
