In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Where the texts mention it

Only one text in the collection mentions the Yeosu fair, and only in passing. In the introduction to Expanding Nationalisms at World's Fairs, David Raizman and Ethan Robey list the host nations of the new millennium: China (Shanghai, 2010), South Korea (Yeosu, 2012), Kazakhstan (Astana, 2017) and the United Arab Emirates, planned for 2020 in Dubai (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1).

The list is set against an earlier history. Between 1851 and 1915, more than twenty major international exhibitions and fairs were held on every continent (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1). In the twentieth century the list of host nations grew to include Brazil (1922), Haiti (1949), Israel (1953, 1956), Canada (1967) and Japan (1970, 1975 and 2005) (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1).

Its place in the argument

The authors do not describe the Yeosu fair itself. They present the recent trajectory of world's fair venues as a barometer of post-Cold War economic development (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1). Yeosu appears nowhere else in the introduction.

The volume itself examines nationalism at nineteenth- and early twentieth-century fairs (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1), so the Yeosu mention lies outside the period it studies. The footnote to the list cites web pages of bie-paris.org on world expo categories, accessed June 14, 2015, but does not say which category the Yeosu fair belonged to (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 12). The texts say no more than this.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Host nations in the new millennium — The introduction lists Yeosu with Shanghai, Astana and Dubai as recent hosts and gives no further detail about the Yeosu fair (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1).
  • Fair venues as a barometer of economic change — The editors read the recent venues as a measure of post-Cold War economic development, a claim about the list as a whole rather than about Yeosu (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1).
  • Period scope — The volume studies nationalism at nineteenth- and early twentieth-century fairs, so the Yeosu mention falls outside its focus (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1).

Questions for study

  1. What did the Yeosu fair show, and who organised it? The texts in this collection do not say, so a student would need a source outside them.
  2. Is a list of host nations enough evidence for a claim about post-Cold War economic development (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1), and what other evidence would test that claim?
  3. The volume examines nationalism at nineteenth- and early twentieth-century fairs (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 1). Does a fair held in 2012 raise the same questions about national display, or does it need a different frame?
  4. Which expo category did the Yeosu fair belong to, as the cited bie-paris.org pages define it (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 12), and how would that change a comparison with the earlier fairs?

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. The editors' introduction, drawing on Benedict Anderson's 'imagined communities', argues that earlier design history treated the fairs as a foil to modernism, and that they were networks of exchange rather than single unified displays. Each chapter is a case study of one national display: Tunisia, Denmark's Nordic Exhibition, Italy at Antwerp, Japan's Hôôden at Chicago, the Musée Centennal in Paris, Finland, Hungary, China's expositions and the American touring art show in South America. The cases run from London and Paris to Chicago, Copenhagen, St. Louis, Nanjing, San Francisco and Buenos Aires.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is substantial but confined to two chapters. Chapter 5, by Hannah Sigur, examines the Hôôden, Japan's pavilion at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, treated as a state project: the commissioners Kuki Ryûichi, Okakura Kakuzô and Yamataka Nobutsura, the architect Kuru Masamichi, and the nihonga paintings of the Tokyo Academy of Art, which presented Japanese art as ancient and authentic. Chapter 9, by Susan Fernsebner, is about China's own participation at St. Louis (1904), Nanjing (1910) and San Francisco (1915): officials and merchants such as Chen Qi, the Six Companies, the protest against the China Pavilion's teahouse and the closure of the 'Underground Chinatown' concession. The introduction names both chapters, and Sigur also covers the 1867 and 1889 Paris fairs, where the French imposed fanciful 'Japanese' facades on Japan's display. Southeast Asia is nearly absent. Fernsebner notes that overseas Chinese at Nanjing used Southeast Asian native goods to show a hierarchy of civilisation, and that 'Nanyang' in the exposition's name refers to Southeast Asia, but no Southeast Asian territory or people is the subject of any essay. Elsewhere East Asia appears in passing: Hanson's chapter 1 uses 'the East' to mean North Africa, the Middle East, Turkey, India and Persia, with China and Japan excluded; Houze notes Japanese exhibits along the Esplanade in Paris in 1900; Guldberg notes Japanese-inspired ceramics and Bing's Japanese prints in Copenhagen.

On the web

Wikipedia

Expo 2012 (Korean: 2012 여수세계박람회; Hanja: 2012 麗水世界博覽會) was an International Exposition recognized by the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) held in Yeosu, South Korea which opened on May 12, 2012 and ran until August 12, 2012. The theme of the Expo was "The Living Ocean and Coast" with subthemes of "Preservation and Sustainable Development of the Ocean and Coast", "New Resources Technology", and "Creative Marine Activities". There were 105 participating countries, international organizations, and 8,203,956 visitors. Expo 2012 opened on May 12 in the southern coastal town of Yeosu, approximately 455 kilometers south of Seoul. It was the second international fair hosted by South Korea, after Expo '93 in Daejeon. The expo site for 80 exhibition halls stretched out alongside the sea with promenades and exhibit spaces extending to the island of Odongdo and the seawalls.
