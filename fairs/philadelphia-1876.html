Centennial International Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Centennial International Exhibition ran in Fairmount Park, Philadelphia, from 10 May to 10 November 1876 to mark a century of American independence (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). It drew nearly ten million visitors (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Historians treat it as the start of the American world's fair tradition (Greenhalgh 1988; Badger 1979): the model for later United States expositions (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), a display of racial and class order (Rydell 1984), and a test of how a federal event could be funded through privately sold shares (Findling & Pelle 1990).

In depth: what our sources say

Origins and aims

John L. Campbell raised the idea at the Smithsonian in 1864 (Burris 2001; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000) and wrote to the Philadelphia mayor in 1866 (Findling & Pelle 1990). Little followed until the Franklin Institute pledged itself to the project in August 1869 (Allwood et al. 2001). Congressman D. J. Morrell led the Act of 1871 that authorised an international exhibition for 1876 (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). The commission had no money for its commissioners, and the federal government disclaimed liability for expenses (Findling & Pelle 1990).

The aims were moral as well as commercial. Rydell argues that the fair was a calculated response to the doubts of Reconstruction and the depression, and that from opening day it worked as "a school for the nation" (Rydell 1984). The reference works say it boosted the nation's pride and self-confidence (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Kaiser stresses that it commemorated independence from monarchical Britain (Kaiser). Allwood sees it as the next big step after Paris 1867 and Vienna 1873 (Allwood et al. 2001).

Organisation and finance

Most of the money came from selling shares, and the texts differ on how much. The Board of Finance could sell up to $10 million of stock (Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001). The dictionary entry says only $2.5 million was sold, almost all of it locally; the city gave $1.5 million and Congress lent $1.5 million in 1875 (Findling & Pelle 1990). Greenhalgh describes state quotas, with New York paying $1,136,660 and Pennsylvania $913,410, and foreign subscriptions that included $600,000 from Japan (Greenhalgh 1988). Rydell notes that railroads dominated the Board's votes: the Philadelphia and Pennsylvania Railroad cast 11,400 of 22,776 (Rydell 1984).

The commission ran like a business. LaPrade describes a rigid administrative hierarchy, devised by men with Civil War and business experience, which made the fair one of the most regulated systems the world had seen (Hollengreen et al. 2014). The director-general, A. T. Goshorn, was a former Cincinnati white-lead businessman (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Heller judges the administration efficient and honest (Findling & Pelle 1990).

Money still ran short. The encyclopedia entry gives a loss of $1.9 million, and the stockholders had to repay the federal government's $1.5 million (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Its statistics appendix prints a loss of $4.5 million (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood's table gives $5.166 million (Allwood et al. 2001), and Badger's appendix gives receipts of $3,666,715 against a cost of $8,980,000 (Badger 1979).

Site and architecture

Hermann J. Schwarzmann, a German immigrant of 27 with engineering and architectural training, laid out the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008; Allwood et al. 2001). Of the 450 acres of Fairmount Park handed over in 1873, 285 became the fairgrounds (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Badger gives 236 acres (Badger 1979) and Allwood 284.5 (Allwood et al. 2001). Maloney argues that Schwarzmann's engineer's plan beat entries by Olmsted and Vaux, and that it favoured buildings and infrastructure over gardens (Maloney 2012).

The first competition winners, Collins and Autenrieth, lost the commission; in 1874 Henry Pettit and Joseph M. Wilson received the Main Building and Machinery Hall, and Schwarzmann took Memorial and Horticultural halls (Findling & Pelle 1990). The Main Building was cast iron and covered 20 acres; Machinery Hall was wood (Findling & Pelle 2008). Giberti argues that the rational, engineered space of the Main Building left representation to the arrangement of displays (Giberti 2002). At the opening, the New York Times reported unfinished work in Memorial Hall and Agricultural Hall (Findling & Pelle 1990).

The landscape was the fair's lasting legacy. Maloney credits the grounds with setting the precedent for the landscapes of later fairs (Maloney 2012). Its lawns and carpet bedding became fashionable, and its Japanese garden gave many Americans their first look at a style that stayed popular (Maloney 2012). Horticultural Hall, touted as the largest conservatory in the world, was torn down in 1955; Memorial Hall survives (Maloney 2012; Findling & Pelle 2008).

Exhibits and attractions

Goods were grouped by country and by type. Giberti traces this dual system to the Paris exhibition of 1867 and says the organisers wanted the show to be transparent, systematic and tabular (Giberti 2002). The texts give different numbers of main departments: ten in Rydell (Rydell 1984), seven in the encyclopedia and in Benedict (Findling & Pelle 2008; Benedict 1983), and eight in the dictionary entry (Findling & Pelle 1990). The encyclopedia and the dictionary both say the scheme later served as the model for the Dewey Decimal System (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).

The most popular attraction was the 700-ton Corliss engine, which Grant and Dom Pedro started (Findling & Pelle 2008; Burris 2001). It drove machinery through 23 miles of shafting and 40 miles of belting (Findling & Pelle 1990). George Pullman bought it and installed it in his company town in 1881 as a display of power (Gilbert 1991).

Several texts recall Bell's telephone demonstration (Ganz 2008; Samuel 2007; Roche 2000), although Allwood says few visitors took notice (Allwood et al. 2001). Sobe and Rackers argue that the Sholes typewriter was upstaged by the telephone, and visitors could buy samples of typed text for 25 cents (Lawn 2009). Memorial Hall was the first full-fledged international art exhibit in the republic, though Walter Smith complained that it had nothing from the Louvre or the Vatican (Findling & Pelle 2008).

The Brazilian court, designed by the Philadelphia architect Frank Furness, was one of the most memorable enclosures in the Main Building (Giberti 2002). Giberti describes a painted wood structure with glass tiles, short thin columns under a heavy arcade of lobed horseshoe arches, and an interior of Brazilian birds and bugs, gold and diamonds, local furniture and the emperor's decorations, which he calls a cabinet of curiosities (Giberti 2002). Çelik notes that Furness had never been abroad and so had seen neither Islamic monuments nor the Ottoman and Egyptian pavilions of Europe, yet he built the court in a neo-Islamic style (Çelik 1992p. 167–168).

The English potter H. Doulton put a circular, domed temple of terra-cotta tile in the nave, enclosing a pyramid of faience, and it was given a place on the central avenue (Giberti 2002). Giberti reads this, and an Austrian wax obelisk, as a way of monumentalizing the commodity, part of what he calls the technique of more (Giberti 2002).

Nations and colonies on show

Japan was the clearest success. Its Main Building exhibit covered 17,831 square feet against China's 6,628, and Japan sent 7,112 packages against China's 477 (Hur). Hotta-Lister say Americans preferred hand-made Japanese goods with traditional designs to machine-made Western ones, and that Japan then set up an agent in New York to import silk and tea directly (Hotta-Lister 1999). Shepherdson-Scott say the exhibit helped open export markets for goods made to American taste (Shepherdson-Scott).

China's exhibit was judged against Japan's. Prince Gong ordered preparations, but Robert Hart, the British head of the Customs, doubted their value (Hur). Li Gui's travelogue introduced the fair to Chinese readers (Hur). Hur dates the point at which Japan began to surpass China in quantity, quality and variety to this fair (Hur). Edward Bruce wrote that if Japan was China's student, "the pupil has surpassed the teacher" (Hur).

Franz Reuleaux, the German commissioner, reported that the fundamental principle of German industrial production was "billig und schlecht – cheap and nasty" (Geppert 2010). The phrase fuelled a German debate on a world exhibition of its own, from 1878 to 1882, and Germany stayed out of the Paris fairs of 1878 and 1889 (Geppert 2010). Geppert credits Philadelphia with introducing the pavilion system, in which each nation built its own pavilion (Geppert 2010);

Spain's commission was cut when Minister Manuel de Orovio dissolved it in 1875 and reduced the budget by almost half (Boone 2019). Spain showed thousands of deluxe books rather than school buildings or teaching materials, and won 93 education prizes, the most after the United States (Lawn 2009). New South Wales wool merchants mostly stayed away because of high US tariffs, while Victoria sent manufactured goods that won awards (Hoffenberg 2001).

Peoples on display

The Smithsonian and the Indian Office supplied the ethnological displays (Rydell 1984; Roche 2000). Mason's directions placed American Indians in a passive role, on their way to extinction (Rydell 1984). The texts differ on space: the Indian artefacts in the Government Building took about one-seventh of its floor (Benedict 1983), while Burris says nearly half the building went to Native displays (Burris 2001). Rydell and co-authors link the displays, which cast Indians as primitive counterpoints to civilised whites, to the federal violence of the Indian wars (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Plains Indians' defeat of Custer at the Little Bighorn fell during the fair (Burris 2001; Roche 2000).

Burris argues that the displays were shaped by the tension between the dangerous and the fascinating Indian, and reports finding nothing to confirm displays of living Indians (Burris 2001). Hinsley says a few live Native Americans appeared, but ethnographic villages became central only at Paris in 1889 (Karp & Lavine 1991).

Black Americans were largely excluded (Rydell 1984; Burris 2001; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Roche 2000). The only works by Black artists were by Edmonia Lewis and Edward Bannister (Rydell 1984). Frederick Douglass was refused a place on the platform (Rydell 1984) and an opening-day speech (Rydell 1993). The concession called "The South" was run by a white Atlanta businessman (Burris 2001). The 1926 Sesquicentennial approved a small display of African-American progress, which 1876 had refused (Rydell 1993).

Visitors and controversy

The figures differ. The encyclopedia gives 9,789,392 visitors by closing day, 8,004,325 of them paying (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990). Allwood gives 9,910,966 (Allwood et al. 2001), and Badger's appendix gives 8,004,274 paying (Badger 1979). Benedict and Roche give 9.9 million (Benedict 1983; Roche 2000). Admission was a fifty-cent note (Hollengreen et al. 2014).

Photograph tickets were issued to exhibitors and workers. Robert Wagan's was number 19,730 of 33,887, and a self-registering punch at exhibitor gates kept these holders apart from paying visitors (Hollengreen et al. 2014). LaPrade argues that the system gave each constituency an identity specific to the exhibition (Hollengreen et al. 2014).

Outside the grounds, Shantytown along Elm Avenue was more than a mile of restaurants, saloons and curiosity shows (Rydell 1984; Benedict 1983). Benedict puts such amusements beyond the official classification (Benedict 1983), and Davis argues the zone was a lively cultural space, not just cheap sideshows (Hollengreen et al. 2014). Rydell says the Centennial City was seen as a threat to the fair's message, and that after deaths and a fire the city demolished 27 more buildings (Rydell 1984).

The women's pavilion was a hard-won concession. Elizabeth Duane Gillespie's committee gave $93,330.87 to the fair's finances (Weimann 1981). When told in 1875 that there was no room in the Main Building, the women were offered their own building, which went to the architect of the Main Building; Gillespie later called this a great mistake (Weimann 1981). Roche says the building presented women through domestic roles, and suffragists opposed it (Roche 2000). On July 4, Susan B. Anthony and associates presented a Declaration of the Rights of Women to acting Vice President Thomas W. Ferry (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Weimann 1981; Samuel 2007). Weimann says the pavilion had no great symbolic significance (Weimann 1981).

Boone reads the Spanish triumphal arch, with medallions of Columbus, Cortés, Pizarro, Ponce de León and De Soto, as a claim on American history (Boone 2019). When Spanish officials objected to a Cuban flag at an awards ceremony, the organisers removed it (Boone 2019).

Howells visited a week after the opening and reported that "the first impression was certainly that of disorder and incompleteness" (Giberti 2002). Giberti, citing Pettit, says that people came mainly to see and be seen, so that a largely middle-class audience could view itself as an emerging class of consumers (Giberti 2002).

Elizabeth Cady Stanton called the Woman's Pavilion an afterthought and said it was no true exhibit of women's art (Weimann 1981p. 4). Weimann names the master of ceremonies and other gentlemen as the men who received the Declaration of Rights for Women in Independence Hall, where the account above gives the acting Vice President (Weimann 1981p. 4).

Installing the exhibits

Installation was late and improvised. Henry Pettit drew the first real site plan in October 1873 (Giberti 2002). By opening day 249 structures stood on the grounds: 15 built by foreign governments, 24 by American states and territories, 56 by private exhibitors and 41 by concessionaires (Giberti 2002). In the last weeks more than 150,000 packages arrived, a third of them bound for the Main Building (Giberti 2002). Giberti also describes its columns, girders and trusses as almost entirely wrought iron, against the cast iron given above (Giberti 2002). On 1 May, ten days before the opening, Pettit described about two thousand workmen, porters, janitors, exhibitors and attendants at work inside the building, and fifty-eight janitors were removing twenty-five tons of refuse a day (Giberti 2002).

The Bureau of Installation allotted space by two rules: to create as many long vistas as possible, and to accent the corners of intersections with exhibits of marked interest (Giberti 2002). Pettit justified the second as the only reliable way to hold visitors who moved as fast as Americans did (Giberti 2002). At the crossing of the nave, the second rule overrode the first. A sequence of diamonds, jewellery and silver plate was placed there in an order that Pettit admitted was at variance with the classification, because it produced the display that was wanted in that spot (Giberti 2002). The most attractive Department II goods were pushed forward, and the educational displays of Department III were buried in the depths of the building (Giberti 2002).

The sunken parterre and the rhododendrons

The long sunken parterre that ran west from Horticultural Hall down Fountain Avenue was the grounds' most popular floral display. Raised walks let visitors see the bed shapes from above (Maloney 2012p. 26). George Thurber named its largest bed, a chain-work design of golden feather and centaurea with vinca and petunias in the links, as the most elaborate on the grounds (Maloney 2012p. 29).

The rhododendron show in the Flower Tent was the strongest floral exhibit. The London nurseryman Anthony Waterer brought about 1,500 plants of some eighty varieties (Maloney 2012p. 30). Meehan gave it almost two pages in the Gardeners' Monthly, judging that nothing shown in a floral way in the United States had drawn so much attention (Maloney 2012p. 30). No other major flower exhibition was mounted, perhaps because of poor planning and the rigours of shipping fresh flowers (Maloney 2012p. 30). Native plants fared worse. The Dutch nurserymen Krelage & Son planted a half circle of butterfly milkweed (Asclepias tuberosa), which Thurber judged a complete failure and Meehan said shamed Americans (Maloney 2012p. 26). Meehan observed that native plants received very little attention at the Centennial (Maloney 2012p. 36).

The opening ceremonies

Badger shows how the choice of poets for the opening reflected the committee's view of the past. Sidney Lanier was asked for a cantata and Bayard Taylor for an ode, while Longfellow declined the honour and Bryant, Holmes and Lowell were approached in turn without success (Badger 1979p. 26). Holmes later agreed to write a hymn for the Independence Day celebration, but the committee finally asked the sixty-nine-year-old John Greenleaf Whittier for a short hymn for the opening (Badger 1979p. 26). Walt Whitman, whom Badger calls the most capable writer of an American song of the period, was ignored, since Leaves of Grass had been banned in several cities (Badger 1979p. 26). Badger also records how Frederick Douglass reached the platform. He received hearty applause when he finally reached the stage, after first being held back by a skeptical guard force (Badger 1979p. 27). This differs from the platform refusal recorded above (Rydell 1984).

Spain's display in Memorial Hall and Agricultural Hall

Spain hung its historical paintings salon-style on the west wall of Memorial Hall, sharing the room with Sweden. Antonio Gisbert's Disembarkation of the Puritans in America hung at one end, beside Dióscoro Puebla's First Landing of Columbus (Boone 2019p. 15). Earl Shinn, writing as Edward Strahan, found it surprising that a Spanish painter should choose the Puritans, and he thought Columbus the proper subject (Boone 2019p. 15). The painting had been commissioned by Miguel Aldama, a Cuban landowner, as the pendant to a Cortés picture, but when his payment was delayed Gisbert sold it to the Madrid banker José María de Salamanca, who sent it to Philadelphia (Boone 2019p. 39). The New York Times named it one of the three most important Spanish works, then gave it faint praise, saying it was less inspired by the old Spanish masters than any other picture in the room (Boone 2019p. 40). Boone notes that critics did not separate Pilgrims from Puritans, nor object to the painting's wrong name for them (Boone 2019p. 40).

The Spanish arch in the Main Building drew hostile notice. The Atlantic's critic called its portal of "preposterous size and ugliness" (Boone 2019p. 30). López Fabra's memoir described the arch as dedicated to the discovery of America, a debt owed to the Spanish nation. Boone reads the Spanish word debido, meaning owed, and the loose sense of America, which covered both the United States and the continents to the south, as a claim on the United States (Boone 2019p. 28). In Agricultural Hall a second, neo-Gothic arch carried a crown and the initials of Alfonso XII, and Spain's space lay across the main transept from the United States' half (Boone 2019p. 31). The director of the Academia de San Fernando refused to lend his collection, and no country except Holland showed seventeenth-century paintings (Boone 2019p. 45).

How historians have interpreted it

Historians agree that the fair was a turning point but disagree about its meaning. Rydell gives the sharpest critical reading. In the 1984 book, the Centennial opens a series of expositions, from 1876 to 1916, that Rydell analyses as vehicles for ideas of empire, race and class (Rydell 1984). With Findling and Pelle, Rydell argues that the advance of industry under an Anglo-Saxon elite was presented as national progress (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). In the later book on Depression-era fairs, Rydell calls American international fairs organised responses to class conflict (Rydell 1993).

Badger sees the fair mainly as the precedent that made the American fair a mass, commercial and national institution (Badger 1979). Burris judges it a cultural success and a historical leap for a young nation, despite its losses (Burris 2001). Giberti treats it as a problem of design in which the organisers still ordered the world on Enlightenment lines while visitors saw it in a more distracted way (Giberti 2002).

They differ on the fair's place in history. Mandell mentions it only in passing and says that no large exhibition after 1851, this one included, rivalled London's show in cultural impact (Mandell 1967). Greenhalgh and Allwood present it as a beginning (Greenhalgh 1988; Allwood et al. 2001). Kaiser reads it as the belated entry of the United States into the world of exhibitions (Kaiser).

On Native Americans, Burris stresses ambivalence (Burris 2001), Rydell stresses racial hierarchy and violence (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and Roche stresses the "taming of the West" (Roche 2000). All of them agree that Black Americans were excluded (Rydell 1984; Burris 2001; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Roche 2000). Boone argues that the making of a single American identity required the exclusion of a Spanish view (Boone 2019), and Hur reads China's participation as a way of performing a modern state (Hur).

Giberti disputes Rydell's reading of the second installation plan, the installation by races. Rydell cites it as proof of a racist programme. Giberti answers that race was not an explicit issue in the official literature, and that the plan concerned race in the ethnic or political sense, which drew the world "literally drawn and symbolically quartered" between the four industrial powers (Giberti 2002). Boone offers a different account of national myth. The 1876 Colonial Revival, with its New England Colonial Kitchen, where women in eighteenth-century dress greeted visitors, proposed a national history of English-speaking Protestants as the founders of the nation (Boone 2019p. 40).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Japan

Japan's presence is the most fully described. Shepherdson-Scott calls the 1876 display Japan's first exhibit in America, which helped open export markets for goods made to American taste (Shepherdson-Scottp. 541). The Meiji government sent carpenters to build a national pavilion, and Tokugawa Akitake and Saigō Tsugumichi led the commission (Hurp. 42). The exhibit filled a large area of the Main Building (Hurp. 42). Greenhalgh lists Japan's subscription at $600,000 among the foreign contributions (Greenhalgh 1988p. 38). Komatsu says about half the cost of assembling the exhibits went to craft objects, and that the Exhibition Bureau sent design sketches to craftsmen (Lawn 2009p. 74). Japan also built a dwelling and a government building called the Japanese Bazaar (Maloney 2012p. 22). The local press mocked the Japanese workers, but visitors admired the finished buildings (Maloney 2012p. 22).

American reactions differed. Hotta-Lister say visitors preferred hand-produced Japanese articles with traditional designs to machine-made Western goods (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 211). Rydell quotes the Inquirer's view that Japan "renders her verdict in favor of American machinery," and says the response drew on existing racial attitudes (Rydell 1984). Kaiser argues that the Japanese sections impressed observers and served Japan's domestic modernisation (Kaiserp. 48). The Japanese student Kikuchi Takeo, then in the United States, admitted: "I couldn't help but blush" (Hurp. 18).

China

Prince Gong ordered the ministers to prepare for China's participation, and told Robert Hart to have Maritime Customs staff manage the exhibit on site (Hurp. 14). Hur says the foreign-dominated Customs Service (IMCS) still collected and shipped the goods (Hurp. 15). Rydell adds that the exhibit was established by Chinese customs officials (Rydell 1984). Li Gui, a Ningbo customs secretary, was personally asked to attend by Detring (Hurp. 23). Li Gui praised the Chinese exhibits as beautifully designed though somewhat cluttered; Hur says local fairgoers were less impressed (Hurp. 25). Santesso notes that the exhibit was unfinished on opening day, and that Bruce called the workmen's presence "really an advantage" (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 43). Rydell reports the Inquirer's talk of a "Chinese problem" (Rydell 1984).

The comparison with Japan was made at the time. Hur gives China 6,628 square feet and 477 packages against Japan's 17,831 and 7,112 (Hurp. 43). Li Gui, as quoted by Hur, said Japan's exhibit doubled China's, but Hur's own areas show nearly triple (Hurp. 43). Bruce wrote that the pupil had surpassed the teacher (Hurp. 43), and Hur dates Japan's lead in quantity, quality and variety to this fair (Hurp. 44).

Other peoples and the wider frame

Asia was framed as a market. Philadelphia papers called it an almost unlimited field for American goods (Rydell 1984). Cashmere shawls from India were shown in the textile gallery (Allwood et al. 2001p. 34), and another text records India's participation (Geppert 2010p. 313). Spain planned for Philippine goods to travel directly to the fair, and Philippine hemp appeared in the Spanish display (Boone 2019p. 22). At the opening, crowds hooted at the Turks, Egyptians, Spaniards, Japanese and Chinese (Burris 2001p. 93).

Interpretations differ. Rydell reads the fair's displays as racially ordered (Rydell 1984). Hur reads China's presence as a performance of statehood, with China cast as an inferior other tied to past glory (Hurp. 18). Maloney credits the Japanese gardens with giving many Americans their first look at a lasting style (Maloney 2012p. 35), and Greenhalgh links Japanese art shown here to later American architects (Greenhalgh 1988p. 148).

A Japanese visitor's view of the crowds

Allwood quotes a Japanese visitor who wrote home that the crowds were like sheep: "The crowds, some like sheep, run here, run there." He added that nobody could see anything, and that everyone went home tired (Allwood et al. 2001p. 45).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Order and classification — the organisers grouped goods by country and by type on a dual system, and the texts treat this as a claim about how the world should be ordered (Giberti 2002; Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Race and installation — displays were arranged by race, and Native Americans and Black Americans were placed in subordinate roles or left out (Rydell 1984; Burris 2001; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • National image — Japan and China were judged against each other, and Germany and Spain saw their showings as matters of national standing (Hur; Geppert 2010; Boone 2019).
  • Private finance and railroads — state quotas, share sales and railroad votes shaped who paid for the fair and who held sway over it (Greenhalgh 1988; Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990).
  • Women's participation — a separate pavilion was won after a long campaign, and the texts disagree on how much it achieved (Weimann 1981; Roche 2000; Ganz 2008).
  • Technology and spectacle — the Corliss engine and Bell's telephone were the fair's most cited machines (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Ganz 2008; Lawn 2009).

Questions for study

  1. If the organisers' classification by country and type expressed Enlightenment ideas of order, how far did visitors' experience of the fair follow that order? (Giberti 2002)
  2. Was the Centennial a celebration of national unity or a display of divisions by race, class and gender? Which sources make the stronger case for each reading? (Burris 2001; Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Weimann 1981)
  3. Why did the texts judge Japan's and China's showings so differently, and what did that judgement reveal about the expectations of the fair's American audience? (Hur; Hotta-Lister 1999)
  4. The texts give different figures for attendance, loss and acreage. How should a historian choose between them, and what does the disagreement itself show? (Findling & Pelle 2008; Findling & Pelle 1990; Allwood et al. 2001; Badger 1979)
  5. Why did the women's pavilion win both praise and suspicion from women's organisations, and what did a separate building mean for women's standing at later fairs? (Weimann 1981; Roche 2000; Ganz 2008; Gilbert 1991)
  6. Giberti argues that the installation by races was about nationalism and empire, not biology, while Rydell reads it as a racist plan. Giberti notes that Spain, among others, was given honorary positions in the installation but no geographically meaningful place. How far does a nation's placement settle what the organisers meant? (Giberti 2002; Boone 2019p. 48)
  7. Pettit wanted visitors to read the classification in the layout, yet the bureau gave up on that for the glance of a crowd. What did visitors actually learn from the Main Building, and what did the crowd take away? (Giberti 2002; Giberti 2002)
  8. The Spanish display was judged a museum rather than a shop, and its prices were often missing, while the Times said the other national courts were fitted up like retail stores. Would American critics have judged the Spanish display differently had it been priced and arranged like a shop? (Boone 2019p. 30)

A book about this fair, chapter by chapter 6

Giberti, Bruno. Designing the Centennial: A History of the 1876 International Exhibition in Philadelphia. Lexington: University Press of Kentucky, 2002.

Giberti's book is a study of the 1876 Centennial exhibition in Philadelphia, read through the problem of order. Its argument is that early world's fairs displayed goods through a dual system of classification by country and by type, and that the Centennial tried to impose this system on its real space but never fully ordered it. Chapters follow the classification, the architecture (including the 1873 building competition and the engineers who shaped the buildings), the installation in the Main Building, visitors' ways of seeing, the American System of Awards that replaced medals and juries, and the permanent institutions that grew out of the fair in Philadelphia. Precedents from London 1851, Paris 1855 and 1867, and Vienna 1873 frame the argument.

1The Order of the Exhibition

Giberti argues that the organisers of the 1876 Centennial were preoccupied with classification, and that this preoccupation shaped the whole exhibition. The chapter traces the classificatory logic from the French national expositions of 1798-1849, through London in 1851, where Playfair's thirty classes gave way to a geographic installation, to Paris in 1855 and 1867, where Napoleon's dual system of country and type produced a wheel-like plan. Vienna in 1873 is the counter-case, a developmental order realised as national courts along one nave. The Philadelphia scheme, drafted by a committee that included William Blake, combined these models in ten departments and a decimal notation modelled on Philadelphia's street numbers. The classification was then cut to seven departments to fit the buildings, and the official catalogue was late and disordered, which Giberti presents as evidence that the exhibition was never fully ordered. The chapter ends by arguing that the organisers had to keep revising their project in the face of conditions they could not foresee.

2The Architecture of the Exhibition

Giberti argues that the architecture of the Centennial was shaped by the split between architecture and engineering, and that engineers had more influence on the fair than architects did. The chapter takes three precedents, the Crystal Palace of 1851, the elliptical hall of Paris in 1867 and Vienna's industrial palace of 1873, and reads each for its vista, its plan and its flaws. The central case is the 1873 competition, which drew forty-three entries and whose four prizes all went to Philadelphia firms, though the committee then declined to endorse any winner. Giberti follows the unpremiated Pavilion Plan of Vaux and Radford, a timber-and-iron hall of uniform bays that the chapter treats as the most modern scheme entered, and then the process by which Pettit and Wilson, both engineers, produced a rectangular iron-and-brick building with shedlike wings. The chapter concludes that the Centennial was an engineer's landscape, whose utilitarian buildings anticipated twentieth-century modernism while architecture was still weak as a profession.

3The Installation in the Main Building

Giberti examines how the Centennial's Main Building was planned, built and filled with exhibits. The chapter starts with the 1873 site plan and the formal axial layout of the grounds, which drew on the Vienna and Paris fairs and set buildings across the fairground in the new pavilion pattern. It then turns to the Main Building itself, a prefabricated iron-and-wood shed that Giberti reads as a rationalised industrial loft, and to the two schemes for installing displays inside it. The first, a dual grid of nations and departments taken from Paris, was abandoned; the second, an installation by races centred on the four industrial powers, was only partly realised. Giberti argues that the organisers wanted the arrangement to make the classification visible, yet the last-minute scramble for space and the demand for striking displays along the avenues undid that transparency. The chapter closes by suggesting that the exhibition's ideal of legibility gave way to the glance of a mobile crowd.

4Ways of Seeing the Exhibition

Giberti turns from the planning of the Centennial to the visitor's experience of it, asking how the exhibition looked and felt from inside. Walking the nave, visitors faced a street of national sections that contemporaries compared to a shopping arcade, and most reported bewilderment rather than clear understanding. The chapter then examines particular national courts, including Sweden, France and Frank Furness's Brazilian court, and the ways exhibitors built their displays, from goods piled on the floor to vitrines, compound showcases and walk-in structures such as the Doulton temple. It works through the technique of more and the model room, and argues that mannequins, wax figures and even the living body blurred the line between goods and people. Views from the towers and the balloon overview let visitors see the whole, but the chapter concludes that the exhibition encouraged a distracted, mobile glance and turned objects into commodities loosened from their origins.

5The American System of Awards

Giberti explains how the Centennial replaced the medal-and-jury system of earlier fairs with an American System of Awards based on signed judges' reports. The chapter begins with Judges' Hall and with the defects of juries that critics such as Beckwith and Francis Walker had identified, then sets out the new system's articles, its bronze medal and the Grouping for the Judges' Work that replaced the original classification. Its central case is the revolt of the Group 27 judges on the Fine Arts, who could not rank paintings as they ranked sewing machines, and the Committee on Appeals, which added 628 further citations. Giberti shows how the mass of undifferentiated reports levelled goods, and reads the art judges' protest as an attempt to protect the aura of the artwork from the market. The chapter follows the system to Chicago in 1893 and 1933 and concludes that the new art institutions of the period express anxiety about the value of things.

6The Exhibitionary Complex in Philadelphia

Giberti examines the institutions that grew out of the Centennial in Philadelphia: the Permanent International Exhibition of 1877 to 1881 in the Main Building, the Pennsylvania Museum and School of Industrial Art in Memorial Hall, and John Wanamaker's Grand Depot department store. Giberti argues that the exhibition was an ambiguous institution, neither quite museum nor quite market, and that its problems of order, architecture, installation, display and evaluation reappeared in each successor in a different form. The evidence is mainly documentary: the International Exhibition Company's bulletins and finances, the museum's acquisitions and trustees' reports, Wanamaker's own statements, and photographs of Memorial Hall and the Chestnut Street store. Giberti sets the museum's vitrines and Moore Memorial Room against the circular counters of the Grand Depot, and reads the store's plan as an echo of the dual classification at the 1867 Paris exposition. Introduction

The introduction presents world's fairs as a formative part of modern life, drawing around 100 million visitors to American fairs between 1876 and the First World War and shaping amusement parks, technologies and city design. It then sorts the scholarship on fairs into six schools, from producer-centred readings of cultural hegemony and audience-centred accounts of how visitors made their own meanings, to counter-hegemonic studies of the people displayed, Benedict's potlatch model with Susman's liminality, documentary histories of technology, and the collectors' tradition. Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, moving from the Crystal Palace model through Philadelphia in 1876 to Chicago in 1893 and Atlanta in 1895, and promoting overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The authors close by asking whether fairs, now tied to transnational corporations, will address the nuclear and ecological crises of the present or leave us whistling in the dark.

1Fairs in the Age of Industrialism’s Advance

Rydell, Findling and Pelle trace the world's fair from London's Crystal Palace of 1851, which drew over six million visitors and set the model for later fairs, to the American fairs of the industrial era. Their main cases are the failed New York Crystal Palace of 1853, the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial, where foreign exhibits were arranged by race and Smithsonian displays cast Native Americans as savage counterparts to civilised whites, and a run of southern fairs in Atlanta, New Orleans and Nashville that sought industrial and export markets. Chicago's 1893 Columbian Exposition receives the fullest treatment: its planning and White City design, the Midway's commercial exoticism, the anthropology displays of Putnam and Boas, and the Colored People's Day that Douglass and Ida B. Wells contested. The authors argue that fairs from 1876 to 1897 were run by business elites to persuade middle-class visitors of Anglo-Saxon superiority. The chapter ends with the turn after 1898, when the acquisition of an overseas empire gave fairs a new imperial purpose.

Weimann opens with the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876, where the Woman's Pavilion was planned, funded and managed by women after the Centennial Board had initially left them out. 5The Impactp. 153

Çelik argues that world's fairs affected architecture far beyond the fairgrounds, because Western architects learned Islamic forms from the pavilions and Ottoman architects reconsidered their own traditions in the same exhibitions. The chapter starts with drawing: Western plan, section and elevation conventions, as used by Parvillee, became the norm in Ottoman publications such as Usul-u mimari-i Osmani (1873). It then turns to neo-Islamic buildings in Istanbul designed mostly by European architects, from the gate of the Ministry of Defense to the Public Debt Administration Building by Antoine Vallaury, and to neo-Arabic architecture in Cairo. The central section examines Western case studies: Owen Jones's interior for the Crystal Palace in 1851, Frank Furness's Brazilian pavilion at Philadelphia in 1876, the Trocadero Palace in Paris in 1878, Adler and Sullivan's Transportation Building in Chicago in 1893, and Eugène Henard's Palace of Electricity in Paris in 1900. The conclusion is that the pavilions were forerunners of neo-Islamic styles in the Ottoman Empire and Egypt, while in the colonies the ruling power appropriated Islamic architecture for its own repertoire.

Rezende examines the Independence Centennial International Exhibition held in Rio de Janeiro in 1922, the first international exhibition on Brazilian soil. She places it within a republican government's need for foreign investment and public consent after the First World War, and within the urban reforms that Mayor Carlos Sampaio carried out in the name of sanitation. The central case is the razing of the Morro do Castelo, whose earth was used to fill the bay for the exhibition site, a process illustrated magazines presented as progress. Rezende then turns to the national section, especially the Pavilion of Statistics and the Festivities Palace, which were designed to teach a largely illiterate public how Brazil had advanced under the republic, and whose pantheon of heroes passed over the monarchy. A further section sets the amusements against the foreign warships in Guanabara Bay, using a journalist's remark about two exhibitions, to show the contradictions of modernity. The conclusion argues that the exhibition made the nation itself an object for public appreciation.

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
3Commissioners and the State: Power and Controversy at the Exhibitionsp. 63Asia

This chapter asks how exhibition commissioners justified their authority as a new cultural elite, and why that authority drew criticism. Hoffenberg argues that commissioners presented themselves as disinterested experts serving the state, yet their work of selecting, pricing and displaying objects exposed them to charges of self-interest and commerce. The main evidence is the Crystal Palace of 1851, where the executive commissioners forbade price tags, and the London International of 1862, which radical papers denounced as a jobbery run by a 'Science and Art clique'. Colonial cases, including the Melbourne Centennial Exhibition, the Adelaide Jubilee show of 1887 and the sales rules at Calcutta in 1883-84, show how on-site selling set commissioners against local merchants. Hoffenberg then compares state funding in Australia and India, which was direct and generous, with England's ad hoc royal commissions, and traces critics from Babbage to the radical press who attacked the commissioners from opposite sides. The chapter ends by treating exhibitions as contests for public opinion, in which the shows revealed the tensions they were meant to conceal.

2.4Picturing Female Patriotism in Three Dimensions: High Street at the 1926 SesquicentennialLydia Mattice Brandtp. 127

Brandt examines High Street, the reconstructed colonial street of replica houses that the Women's Committee built in the south-west quadrant of the 1926 Sesquicentennial International Exposition in Philadelphia, where it became the fair's most popular destination. The argument is that the women's organisations used the replicas to present the home as the moral foundation of citizenship, at a moment when the male organisers were embroiled in scandal. The committee's conservative view of women's civic role drew on earlier female-led preservation and municipal housekeeping movements. The chapter then turns to the individual buildings: the Washington House, furnished from an inventory of the president's possessions; the Jefferson boarding house, fitted with reproductions based on Monticello; and the Shippen House, finished by Good Housekeeping as a modern home behind a colonial facade. Brandt shows that these interiors promoted Colonial Revival goods available for purchase, so that a woman's consumption became part of her civic duty. The chapter concludes that the committee made the colonial past stylish and attainable, and presented its own members as proof that the founders' values survived in modern homes.

Maloney, Cathy Jean. World’s Fair Gardens: Shaping American Landscapes. Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012.
Introductionp. 1

Maloney introduces the book's subject: the horticulture and landscape design of United States world's fairs between 1850 and 1940, and their lasting influence on public parks and gardens. He argues that the fairs' temporary grounds left durable green spaces, from Jackson Park in Chicago to Flushing Meadows in New York, and that the need to create mature landscapes in a few months drove advances in tree transplantation, irrigation, lighting and cold storage. The chapter traces the spread of the fair from London in 1851 to the United States, where cities with room to grow offered campus-like sites, and notes the designers, nurserymen and horticultural societies who shaped these fairs. It then explains the criteria for choosing the nine featured fairs, from Philadelphia in 1876 to New York in 1939-40, and sets out a table of their sites, attendance and finances. Maloney concludes that understanding the original design intent of these landscapes may help people appreciate and preserve the surviving remnants.

World's Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition, New Orleans, 1884-1885: North Meets South in a Winter Gardenp. 39

Maloney turns to the World's Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition in New Orleans, which ran from December 1884 to June 1885 and struggled to present the New South as both industrial and agrarian. He argues that its grounds, laid out within about a year on a former marsh and plantation site, reflected engineering and military expediency, and that railway delays, failed cold storage and summer heat undermined its horticultural displays. The chapter describes the Mississippi Valley Horticultural Society, which ran the horticultural department and renamed itself the American Horticultural Society at the fair, and then the Mexican garden, the live oak avenues, the fruit displays and the cacti. It follows the fair's sale to a company headed by George Pullman and the slow development of Audubon Park, whose plan was commissioned from John Charles Olmsted. Maloney concludes that the fair achieved only some of its goals, though its puttyless glazing, lighting and cactus displays left a legacy for later exhibitions.

The introduction sets the fair against the early modern curiosity cabinet and against scholarship that reads fairs as displays of imperial and racial power, and argues that the fair is polyvalent: a shrine to science, a marketplace and a site of nation-building. Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.

Rydell's first chapter treats the 1876 Centennial as the starting point for a series of American international expositions from 1876 to 1916, which he analyses as vehicles for ideas of empire, race and class. He argues that the Philadelphia fair was a calculated response to the doubts of Reconstruction and the depression, and that its ideology of progress was organised by race (through installation by races and the treatment of Native Americans, Black Americans, Asians and Africans) and by class (through the mobilisation of workers and the removal of the 'Centennial City' along Elm Avenue). This chapter carries the book's larger case about expositions and American cultural hegemony.

Rydell's study examines the American world's fairs held between 1876 and 1916, from Philadelphia's Centennial to the San Francisco and San Diego expositions of 1915, and argues that they gave visitors a ritual confirmation of progress that legitimised white supremacy and the authority of corporate, political and scientific leaders. Each chapter takes one exposition or a group of them: Philadelphia, Chicago 1893, the southern fairs, Omaha, Buffalo, Saint Louis, Portland and Seattle, and San Francisco and San Diego. Midway villages, anthropology displays and federal exhibits are read as lessons in racial hierarchy and imperial expansion. The introduction sets out the argument, and the conclusion presents the fairs as tools by which the United States began to manage the world from its own imperial perspective.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand of the book, not its centre; African Americans, Native Americans and Mexicans receive as much attention. The Philippines is the most concentrated theme: it is the central case of chapter 6, 'The Coronation of Civilization' (Saint Louis, 1904), where Igorot and Negrito villages on the Philippine Reservation are set against Scouts and Constabulary. Chapter 5 (Buffalo) and chapter 7 (Portland and Seattle) also follow Filipino villages. China and Japan recur in chapters 1, 2 and 8, most fully in chapter 2 (Chicago 1893) and chapter 8 (San Francisco and San Diego). Asia appears as participant, as the subject of displays organised by others, and as visitors whose treatment was contested. Japan built its Ho-o-den at Chicago with Japanese workers, a Japanese delegation toured Seattle in 1909, and China's official Forbidden City was reassembled at San Francisco. Chinese villages were staged at Chicago, Omaha and New Orleans, Japanese and Chinese villages at San Francisco, and the Mysterious Asia bazaars at Saint Louis included streets in Rangoon. Rydell reads these displays as lessons in racial hierarchy rather than as Asian agency.

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

Wikipedia

The Centennial International Exhibition, officially the International Exhibition of Arts, Manufactures, and Products of the Soil and Mine, was held in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, from May 10 to November 10, 1876. It was the first official world's fair to be held in the United States and coincided with the centennial anniversary of the Declaration of Independence's adoption in Philadelphia on July 4, 1776. It was held in Fairmount Park along the Schuylkill River on fairgrounds designed by Herman J. Schwarzmann. Nearly 10 million visitors attended the exposition, and 37 countries participated in it.
