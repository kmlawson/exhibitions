Christchurch · 1906–1907

New Zealand International Exhibition of Arts and Industries

OverviewIn depthChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The New Zealand International Exhibition of Arts and Industries was held in Christchurch, on the South Island, from 1 November 1906 to 15 April 1907 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). The 2008 encyclopedia calls it the country's largest exhibition to date and says it was wholly financed by the government (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). Premier Richard Seddon proposed it but died before it opened (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The texts show a colonial event that displayed Maori culture, with Maori taking part (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

In 1903 Richard Seddon, the premier, proposed an international exhibition for the colony (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The 2008 encyclopedia says the idea came from Seddon, who first planned an inter-colonial exhibition in Hagley Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). The two accounts differ on the first scope of the plan. Seddon laid the foundation stone in Hagley Park on 18 December 1905 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The 1990 dictionary says the fair was to show the colony's industrial progress to the rest of the world (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The 2008 encyclopedia places it 16 years after the Dunedin exhibition of 1889-1890 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187).

Organisers and finance

The government paid for the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). The 1990 dictionary says government funds were provided to ensure a national, as opposed to provincial, character (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). Sir Joseph G. Ward was president and W. Hall Jones commissioner (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420). The fair lost money. The 2001 history gives a loss of £81,430 for the buildings only (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207), and the 1990 dictionary says it did not show a financial profit (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196). The 2008 encyclopedia's statistics table is too garbled in this extraction to give a readable Christchurch row (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 415).

Site and buildings

The site was 14 acres in Hagley Park, facing the Avon River and Park Terrace (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). A main building a quarter-mile long had a facade by the Christchurch architect J.C. Maddison in a French Renaissance style (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). The 1990 dictionary says the buildings covered nearly 14 acres, had towers 160 feet high, were finished in white stuccoline, and cost nearly £90,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195).

Exhibits and attractions

The 1990 dictionary says the New Zealand courts dominated the exhibition, and rates the British art and educational exhibits as very impressive (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). Its art gallery held a collection valued at over £150,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The 2008 encyclopedia says the fine arts section gave unprecedented space to British art, with arts and crafts organised by Walter Crane, and that the Colonial Art section was cursory. It argues that the British display in fact stifled the development of New Zealand art (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). Amusements included Wonderland, with camel rides (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 189), and a water chute, helter-skelter, haunted castle and penny arcade (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196).

Maori and Pacific participants

The 2008 encyclopedia gives the fullest account of Maori participation. It describes a Maori pa, or fortified village, set up in Hagley Park, where Maori from around the country lived and performed for paying visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). The entry says the organiser, Augustus Hamilton, had ethnographic intentions, but that the pa meant different things to the Maori involved, who generally liked the fair and met other tribes (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). Fijians and Cook Islanders were also present (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). Hori Pukehika, Tuta Nihoniho and Neke Kapua helped build and run the pa, and said that it celebrated the survival and adaptability of their culture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). The 1990 dictionary describes the Maori Pa as a replica of a fortified village with traditional dance and song (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196). The main entrance bore the greeting Haere Mai, the Great Hall held plaster Maori figures, and Maori names were given to the passages and avenues (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195).

Visitors and reception

The texts do not agree on the total. The 2008 encyclopedia gives 2 million visitors, a daily average of 14,000, and calls the fair "an unparalleled success" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). The 1990 dictionary gives "nearly 2 million visitors" by the close (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196), and the 2001 history's table gives 1,967,032 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). The 1990 dictionary says the fair was a success in other ways and that the city of Christchurch flourished (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 196).

Empire and colony

The 2001 history begins its comment with the words "Very much a colonial event" (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). It notes that the fair was housed in one main building, a move away from the decentralised international expositions (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). The 2008 encyclopedia says New Zealand exhibitions never attracted "a great number of foreign countries" and were barely international in scope (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). Its separate courts were for the United Kingdom, Canada, Fiji and several Australian states, and its exhibits came from the United States, South Africa and eight European countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). The 1990 dictionary says the Canadian, Fijian and New South Wales governments had generous space (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 195). The 2008 encyclopedia reads the fair as an expression of a fledgling national identity, marked by dominion status in 1907, but within a loyalist vision of New Zealand as the better Britain of the South Pacific (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). Citing Jock Phillips, it says New Zealand presented itself as Maoriland by appropriating aspects of Maori culture to serve settler identity (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 188). The 2015 volume's introduction lists Christchurch 1906-07 among colonial exhibitions organised to promote settlement, investment and development under British authority (Filipová 2015p. 14). Roche's 2000 book lists it among exhibitions that marked the development of dominions and colonies (Roche 2000p. 58). A British government table records £8,000 of English government direct expenditure, in sterling, on the 1907 New Zealand International exhibition, under the Colonial Office and Board of Trade (Hoffenberg 2001p. 286; Hoffenberg 2001p. 286).

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Bell, Leonard. Colonial Constructs: European Images of Maori, 1840–1914. Auckland: Auckland University Press, 1992.
6Wilhelm Dittmerp. 222

Bell examines Wilhelm Dittmer, a German-born painter and illustrator who lived in New Zealand from 1898 to 1905 and later published Te Tohunga (1907), a lavishly illustrated book of Maori legend. The chapter argues that his Maori images, from the oil portrait Mana of the chief Te Heu Heu Tukino to the paintings of elderly figures and young women, conform to colonial stereotypes of a dying and passive people, even where they borrow forms from Maori art. Bell compares Dittmer with Goldie and Lindauer, showing how Mana turns a powerful political figure into a mask, while Dittmer's mature women, unlike Goldie's, look out with strength. The bulk of the chapter traces the European sources of the Te Tohunga drawings in Symbolism, Art Nouveau and Jugendstil, and shows how the text reshapes Maori legends such as Hinemoa and Mahuika to suit European tastes. Bell concludes that the book, underwritten by Prime Minister Joseph Ward, helped to fashion a second, indigenous culture for the settler nation.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. The Philippines is shown through Igorrots on the Pike at St Louis 1904 (chapter 8), an ASEAN plaza at Vancouver 1986 (chapter 13), and the Manila 1992 bid, which was dropped (chapter 14). Angkor Wat reproductions appear at Paris 1889 and 1931 (chapters 6 and 9), and Thai temples at St Louis, Seville 1992 and Hanover 2000 (chapters 8 and 15). The Tonkin colonial exhibition of 1902-03 in French Indo-China (chapter 7) showed Japanese, Chinese and Siamese exhibits, and Javanese villages formed part of the Chicago Midway (chapter 6). Singapore appears only as one of the Straits Settlements in a list of colonies (chapter 5), and Malaya and Borneo not at all.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Mega-Events and Modernity (2000) is Roche's sociological study of the modern world's mega-events, chiefly international expos and the Olympic Games. It argues that these events shaped public culture, cultural citizenship and the inclusion or exclusion of populations from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, and it proposes a multi-perspectival method combining a dramaturgical view of the event, a political-sociological account of its production and a long-run view of modernity. Part 1 (chapters 2-4) covers expos as instruments of national and imperial cultural policy, their mass audiences and racial and gender exclusion, and the inter-war Olympics. Part 2 (chapters 5-7) covers expos and Olympics in host cities and tourism, the media and television, and global citizenship in the Olympic movement. Chapter 8 is theoretical.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia receives substantial but uneven attention, centred on Korea. The Seoul Olympics of 1988 are the main case: chapter 6 (Mega-events and media culture) gives them the longest treatment, testing Katz and Dayan's media-event theory against Larson and Park's account of the Games as political communication by a military government. Chapter 5 (Mega-events, cities and tourist culture) presents Seoul as a 'media city', and chapter 7 (Mega-events and global citizenship) cites Seoul among repressive host states. Japan appears in chapter 4 (The Olympics, internationalism and supernationalism), where the IOC's handling of the 1940 Games is narrated, and in chapter 3 (Mega-events and cultural citizenship), where Japanese and Chinese national pavilions are said to project a positive national identity. China figures in chapter 5 through the Beijing 2000 bid and in chapter 7 through human rights and doping. Southeast Asia appears only in colonial and imperial displays. The Philippines are present in chapter 2 (Expos and cultural power), where a 47-acre Philippines site at St Louis 1904 is noted as a sign of American control, and in chapter 3 among the peoples shown in 'native village' displays. Malaya, Burma and Hong Kong appear only in the list of colonies at Wembley 1924/5. The Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Siam and Singapore are not discussed. Expos held in Asia, such as Osaka 1970 and Taejon 1993, appear only in passing.

Wikipedia

The New Zealand International Exhibition (the biggest in the country to that time) opened on 1 November 1906 in Hagley Park, Christchurch, New Zealand. Nearly two million people visited the exhibition during the next few months. The idea for the International Exhibition came from the prime minister, Richard Seddon, who was working on the government's 1903 budget and noticed a line item for the country to be represented at the St. Louis World's Fair in 1904. In Parliament, Seddon announced his idea as an intercolonial fair for New Zealand "on a scale unprecedented south of the Line". The initiative approved by cabinet contained just three facts: it was to be larger than any prior event, to be held in Christchurch's Hagley Park, and be held soon. The government financed the exhibition and would cover any losses.
