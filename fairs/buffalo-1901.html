Pan-American Exposition

The Pan-American Exposition opened in Buffalo, New York, on 1 May 1901 and closed on 2 November that year (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 157; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162). Its theme was the electric power of Niagara Falls, and its promoters hoped to improve trade and relations with Latin America (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 157). It ended under the shadow of President McKinley's assassination, with a deficit of more than $3 million (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 165). Historians study it as a case of how American expositions displayed imperial power, above all through the Philippine exhibit and the midway villages (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76; Rydell 1984).

Origins and aims

Buffalo businessmen first discussed the idea in 1895, at the Atlanta Cotton States Exposition. Low-cost power from Niagara Falls arrived in November 1896, a company was formed, and a site was chosen in 1897 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 165). The directors first chose Cayuga Island, near the falls. The fair was eventually built on the Rumsey property and part of Delaware Park, an area of 350 acres served by trolley lines on three sides (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 157).

The stated aim was to illustrate the material progress of the New World in the nineteenth century, and to improve relations and trade with Latin America (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 165; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 157). Congress passed a resolution in July 1898 backing a Pan-American exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 157). The Spanish-American War delayed the fair, and the 1901 dates were chosen because of the Paris exposition of 1900 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 165; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell (1984) argues that the promoters hoped to teach Latin Americans that the United States was a friend, while the directors also promised an age of American dominance in international affairs (Rydell 1984). Pan-Americanism, the idea of closer political and commercial ties among the countries of the Americas, was the programme's name (Roche 2000p. 59).

Organisation and finance

Congress paid for a federal government building, New York State gave $300,000 for its own exhibit, and local subscriptions raised $1.5 million (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 158; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 165). The texts give different federal figures. Allwood, Allan and Reid record a $500,000 grant from Congress (Allwood et al. 2001p. 80), while Badger's appendix gives a federal appropriation of $1,015,000 (Badger 1979p. 132).

William I. Buchanan became director general in late 1899. He found the city unexcited, the finances insecure and the Latin American role much smaller than planned (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 166; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 158). The corporate board was chosen by Buffalo bank directors from businessmen who had subscribed at least $300,000 in bonds, and the railroads, led by the Vanderbilt Lines, gave heavily (Rydell 1984). The federal government confined its money to artifacts and natural resources (Rydell 1984). The financial outcome is reported differently. Gould and Brackett (35) and Gould (39) give a deficit of more than $3 million, a further $2.5 million of stock lost, defaulted bonds and lawsuits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170). Badger's table gives receipts of $5,534,643 against costs of $8,860,757 (Badger 1979p. 131). The statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid, however, records a loss of $578,000 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Site, lighting and colour

Carrere's general plan, Karl Bitter's sculpture of more than 2,000 pieces and Charles Y. Turner's colour scheme gave the fair its unity. The Spanish Renaissance style was chosen to suggest the historical continuity of life in the Americas (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 160).

The Electric Tower was the central feature, powered by Niagara hydro-electricity and designed by J. G. Howard. Allwood, Allan and Reid give its height as 389 feet and say that over 200,000 other lights outlined the buildings (Allwood et al. 2001p. 79). Other texts give 375 feet (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 167; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000), and Kamerbeek gives 400 feet and 35,000 bulbs (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 381). Maloney calls the fair arguably the first attempt to light a landscape artistically, and says that landscape lighting became a key element of later fairs, notably Chicago in 1933 (Maloney 2012p. 95).

The fair was known as the Rainbow City (Maloney 2012p. 86). Its colours were bright and were copied from Central and South American designs, and the Temple of Music was painted red and salmon pink (Allwood et al. 2001p. 79). The strongest colours were at the entrances and the palest near the Electric Tower. The directors' pamphlet described this order as "the whole symbolizing progression from a less civilized stage to a higher" (Rydell 1984). Rudolph Ulrich, Olmsted's superintendent at Chicago in 1893, became chief landscape architect in August 1899 (Maloney 2012p. 87). Fifty horticultural firms planted about 20 acres of display gardens in more than 200 beds (Maloney 2012p. 92).

Nations and colonies on show

The Latin American theme was the fair's own, but it drew little participation. Separate buildings went to Canada, Chile, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras and Mexico. Other countries had space in halls or sent only a commissioner (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 168). Rydell, Findling and Pelle count only seven nations that built their own pavilions (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Roche notes that pan-Americanism was used at Buffalo together with displays of produce and people from Hawaii and the Philippines, and that it legitimated American control over those places (Roche 2000p. 59).

The colonial displays became the centre of the fair. In 1899 the organisers asked the federal government for a display from Cuba, Puerto Rico, Alaska, Hawaii and the Philippines (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76). Greenhalgh writes: "In 1901 at the Buffalo Pan American World’s Fair, for the first time there were worthwhile possessions to display" (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76). The Philippines became the centrepiece (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Greenhalgh argues that the Pan-American label meant empire in this setting, because America possessed some but not all of what was shown (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76).

Frank F. Hilder, a government ethnologist (a scholar of human societies), gathered more than 500 artifacts and photographs in the Philippines in 1900 and died before the fair opened (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 168). The texts spell his name differently: 38 gives Frank E. (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000) and 07 gives F. P. (Greenhalgh 1988p. 76). His collection was kept intact in the government building, and it reportedly drew more visitors than the other federal exhibits (Rydell 1984). The federal government refused to fund a Philippine village or a display of Emilio Aguinaldo, the leader of the Philippine insurrection, so local exposition authorities built a Filipino Village on the midway (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Buffalo businessmen put up $40,000 for it (Rydell 1984). Its success led the federal government to plan a larger Philippine installation for St Louis (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Peoples on display

The midway, the amusement strip beside the main grounds, held ethnological villages that showed living people in re-created native settings. Rydell (1984) lists Darkest Africa with ninety-eight Africans, an Old Plantation with fifty Black southerners, a Mexican village run under conditions set by President Diaz, and an Indian Congress that included Geronimo. He argues that the contracts stressed racial characteristics to justify Anglo-Saxon domination (Rydell 1984). Rydell (1993) argues that live ethnological displays, set beside foodstuffs and machine tools, gave visitors an impression of foreign markets for American goods (Rydell 1993p. 22).

Disease spread through the villages, with tuberculosis at the Filipino Village and the Indian Congress (Rydell 1984).

Roswell Park, the exposition's medical director, reported eleven cases of measles in the Eskimo village, a mumps outbreak in the Hawaiian village and eight cases of tuberculosis in the Indian Congress (Rydell 1984). Quarantines were imposed on several villages, but visitors largely ignored them (Rydell 1984). Richard Barry, who wrote a popular sketch of the Pan, argued that southern Black performers were better for the Old Plantation than northern ones, whom he thought too ready for the show business (Rydell 1984). Jim Parker, the Atlanta waiter who helped subdue McKinley's assassin, was offered a place in an unnamed midway attraction and refused (Rydell 1984). Allwood counts 700 representatives in the Indian Congress (Allwood et al. 2001p. 79), and Greenhalgh says the keynote of the Old Plantation was the difference between Southern and Northern Black people (Greenhalgh 1988p. 99).

Visitors, assassination and closing

The gates opened on 1 May, but the official ceremonies were held on 20 May, Dedication Day. Over 100,000 people came that day, while daily visitors hovered around 10,000 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 166; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 159). Attendance passed 40,000 a day by August (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 161). On President's Day, 5 September, over 116,000 people came, and McKinley's speech on reciprocity was received with cheers (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 168; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 161).

On 6 September Leon Czolgosz shot McKinley at about 4.07 p.m. in the receiving line at the Temple of Music (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 169; Allwood et al. 2001p. 80). Doctors failed to find one of the bullets, and McKinley died of gangrene on 14 September (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162). Rydell, Findling and Pelle suggest that McKinley might have lived had the X-ray machine on display been used (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Czolgosz was executed on 29 October (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162). The fair reopened, a planned Buffalo Day on 1 November drew few visitors, and the fair closed on 2 November (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162). In all, more than 8 million people came, 5.3 million of them paying (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162). Benedict's table gives 8.1 million (Benedict 1983p. 31), and Badger's appendix gives 8,304,073 (Badger 1979p. 131).

Aftermath and legacy

After the fair, Buffalo lost some of its prestige and confidence, and some historians believe that the fair marked the city's decline. The wider aim of Pan-Americanism was mostly unrealised (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 163). Ganz argues that the financial shortfalls at Buffalo and at Philadelphia in 1926 made congressional leaders reluctant to approve new fairs (Ganz 2008). Buffalo's organisers gave women a converted country club, which served as a social space rather than for exhibits (Ganz 2008). The New York State Building is the only structure still on the original site, and it houses the Buffalo and Erie County Historical Society, which holds the architect's model of the Electric Tower (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 69; Maloney 2012p. 96).

The Edison films of the fair

Edison's cameramen James H. White and Edwin S. Porter made "more than twenty films" at Buffalo (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 349). Kamerbeek counts eighteen films of the exposition and ten more on the assassination and funeral (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 383). White came to Buffalo from the Paris exposition of 1900, where he had tested the rotating tripod head that the Buffalo panoramas used (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 350). The Edison catalogue gives 106,000 people on the ground on the opening day, 20 May, against the figure above of over 100,000 (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 350).

The panorama Pan-American Exposition by Night began in daylight and kept turning at the same speed after dark, so that day seemed to change into night (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 351). Jacobson reads the margins of such films more closely than their attractions. In Esquimaux Leap-frog, electric lines and two smoking chimneys fill the top of the frame above the village, giving three planes: the performers, the village beyond a small lake, and the technology behind both (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 354). The crew did not film Niagara Falls, although the falls generated the power that lit the grounds (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 352; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 359).

The assassination on film and in the press

The crew's film The Mob Outside the Temple of Music panned across a sea of hatted heads turned towards the shooting (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 384). Czolgosz had given the police the name "Fred Nieman" on his arrest, and Kamerbeek reads the alias as a figure who came out of the crowd unnoticed (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 385). The New York Times headline of 8 September called him a rabid anarchist, and the Cleveland Plain Dealer ran an article linking him to Emma Goldman (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 386). McKinley was taken to the exposition hospital in an electric ambulance, and telegraph messages sought out Vice-President Roosevelt (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 385).

Auburn Prison had a poor record with the electric chair before it held Czolgosz. William Kemmler survived the first current in 1890 and had to be shocked a second time (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 388). Auburn's warden secured permission from the family to dispose of the body, and had the remains destroyed with quicklime and sulfuric acid, so that the assassin would not become a martyr (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 386).

The Ethnology Building and the midway

Carrère placed the Ethnology Building at the centre of the grounds, and Turner gave its exterior a colour midway between crude and refined (Rydell 1984). Inside, the walls ran from earthy red at the base to ethereal blue at the top, which the Buffalo Enquirer likened to the ascent of man from savagery to civilisation (Rydell 1984). Artifacts were arranged by region, with oil paintings of racial types on the walls (Rydell 1984).

Benedict's plan for a living display of Pan-American peoples shrank, because of cost, sanitation and fear of rivalry with the Indian Congress, to a hexagonal stockade with Iroquois dwellings (Rydell 1984). About 75 per cent of visitors to the exposition were represented in the building on an average day, but Buchanan called the department's results "far from satisfactory" (Rydell 1984). Greenhalgh calls Buffalo the extreme case of a site laid out to move visitors from low to higher levels of humanity (Greenhalgh 1988p. 102).

The midway drew between two and three million dollars of local and showmen's money (Rydell 1984). Taylor turned down an African slave market for the Pan and gave the African village to Xavier Pené, who had run the Dahomeyan village at Chicago (Rydell 1984). Greenhalgh thinks the African settlement most probably held Dahomeyans (Greenhalgh 1988p. 101). Pené's show was advertised as "In the Midway but not of it" (Rydell 1984). The Evolution of Man show presented a trained chimpanzee, Esau, as the missing link in Darwin's chain (Rydell 1984).

Gardens, cold storage and night lighting

Frederic Taylor put the Horticulture Building's cold storage in the basement, and electric refrigeration was installed in May 1901 on an upper floor so that visitors could watch it at work (Maloney 2012p. 91). The tulip display filled six beds with 42,000 plants, mostly submitted by F. R. Pierson of Tarrytown (Maloney 2012p. 92). The rose garden of Ellwanger & Barry held two thousand hardy roses, and by one account a single bed bore sixty thousand blooms on 4 July (Maloney 2012p. 94). The water-lily basins, about 125 feet across, were kept at 80 degrees by steam pipes (Maloney 2012p. 94).

At night the exposition's electrical bureau placed 3,000 incandescent lamps in the flowerbeds late in the season (Maloney 2012p. 95). The Buffalo Express reported that the lamps showed the form of the beds, but that the colour of the flowers, their chief beauty, was lost (Maloney 2012p. 96). Della Coletta gives the tower as 400 feet with 40,000 small bulbs (Della Coletta 2006p. 170), against the figure above of 35,000 bulbs from Kamerbeek.

Women at the fair

Weimann says that at Buffalo an existing clubhouse was pressed into service as a woman's building, and that its architecture clashed painfully with the rest of the fair (Weimann 1981p. 598). At Omaha in 1898, by contrast, a board of twenty-seven women had presided over a boys' and girls' building (Weimann 1981p. 597). Maloney notes that the converted country club enjoyed a central place among the flower beds (Maloney 2012p. 92).

What became of the site

The promoters meant to keep only the New York State Building, but Buffalo citizens and aldermen pressed to keep other buildings and the grounds as parkland (Maloney 2012p. 96). Critics said the city's existing parks were for rich men rather than the poor, and on 11 December the South Park District Taxpayers Association concluded that no more parks should be acquired until the city could care for those it had (Maloney 2012p. 96). The New York State Building is the only structure left on its original site, and the rest of the grounds was subdivided for expressways and housing (Maloney 2012p. 96; Allwood et al. 2001p. 80). By March 1902 the exposition was in ruins, and the Electric Tower and City of Living Light had been demolished (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 359).

How historians have interpreted it

The main readings differ over what the fair was for. Rydell (1984) reads it as an evolutionary allegory, in which architecture, sculpture and colour placed peoples on a path from savagery to civilisation. In Rydell's account the Latin American message served commercial and colonial ambitions (Rydell 1984). Greenhalgh places the fair within a racial hierarchy, in which villages from Japanese and African to Cuban, Hawaiian and Philippine formed a sequence and Native Americans were shown as entertainment rather than equals (Greenhalgh 1988p. 101). Rydell, Findling and Pelle present Buffalo as the first full working-out of an imperial theme that they trace through American fairs. In their reading the Filipino Village presented Filipinos as needing American uplift, and the fair was designed to justify US colonial rule while anti-imperialism was rising (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

The readings also differ over the assassination. Gould writes that the shooting, rather than the lighting or the colour, is the fair's most lasting historical association: "The most lasting historical association of the Pan-American Exposition is with the shooting of the president" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170). Gould argues that Buffalo's hopes of economic revival and Latin American partnership largely failed, and that modern scholars read the fair as cultural chauvinism and racial hegemony in McKinley's imperial era (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170). Rydell (1984) argues that the assassination, rather than unsettling the fair's message, made it a visible reminder of cultural and political continuity (Rydell 1984). Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that the fair did more to change America than it changed Buffalo, since McKinley's death brought Theodore Roosevelt to the presidency (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Kamerbeek reads the killing as exposing a fear of an invisible mob, which the Edison company's staged film of the execution showed restored to order (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 385).

Jacobson reads the Edison films of the fair as spreading its ideology of progress. Electric lines and smokestacks in the background of the native-village films show the Niagara technology that underwrote the performances in the foreground (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 354). Kamerbeek reads the fairground as an idealised, controlled city (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 383). Gould and Brackett agree that the Pan-American aims went largely unrealised (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 163). Maloney reads the fair mainly as a turning point in exposition landscaping, tracing its gardens to popular taste for colour (Maloney 2012p. 86).

Several texts mention the fair only in passing: Benedict among the midway attractions (Benedict 1983p. 56), Sobe and Rackers as a source of typewriter displays (Lawn 2009p. 95), and Badger for the fair's statistics (Badger 1979p. 131). The texts that discuss the fair at length agree that its Latin American aims largely failed and that its colonial displays were central. They disagree about the figures, and about whether the assassination changed the fair's meaning or only made it more visible.

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

Asian peoples and countries appear at the Buffalo fair mainly through a Japanese precinct and through the Philippine exhibit and village. The texts name no Asian commissioner, visitor or observer at this fair.

Japan

Peterson describes a Japanese precinct at Buffalo associated with Kushibiki, a presenter who developed at Buffalo a representational formula that he later expanded at the Saint Louis fair and applied at San Francisco (Peterson 2020p. 63). The Buffalo precinct was a miniature Japanese village, resembling the pre-modern Asakusa district of Tokyo, with a restaurant, a tea house, shops selling Japanese goods, cultural performances and geisha performers (Peterson 2020p. 63). Photographs show women serving tea and posing at key sites around the grounds (Peterson 2020p. 66). The texts do not say who financed the precinct. Peterson's argument concerns Kushibiki's skill in presenting both himself and Japan to American audiences in ways that were attractive and beguiling, and treats the Buffalo precinct as one step in that longer story (Peterson 2020p. 63).

Edison filmmakers White and Porter recorded the Japanese village and an Esquimaux village among their subjects at the fair (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 349). Greenhalgh says Buffalo followed the Chicago pattern with exotic villages, Japanese, African and Eskimo, and that the Philippine village, with one hundred people, was the largest of them (Greenhalgh 1988p. 101).

The Philippines

The Philippine displays are the Asian presence that the texts treat at length, and they formed part of the fair's colonial core. The government ethnologist Frank F. Hilder collected artifacts and photographs in the Philippines in 1900. The federal government funded the collection only as far as artifacts and natural resources (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Hilder's collection was kept intact in the government building, and it was one of the most popular aspects of the federal exhibit (Rydell 1984; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 168).

The federal government refused funds for a Philippine village, so local exposition authorities built a Filipino Village on the midway. It occupied an eleven-acre enclosure with a detachment of US soldiers at its gate (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984). Buffalo businessmen paid $40,000 for it, and Pony Moore brought one hundred Filipinos to Buffalo (Rydell 1984). Visitors rode in carts pulled by water buffalo, and the village presented Filipinos as indifferent to work and in need of American uplift (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Inside the huts, visitors saw comfortable chairs, tables and modern cooking stoves, and a Filipino woman at a sewing machine (Rydell 1993p. 22). Rydell (1984) argues that the Philippine exhibit was the clearest case of the fair's imperial purpose (Rydell 1984).

Filipinos at the fair

Emilio Aguinaldo was considered for display in the Filipino Village after his capture in April 1901, but the idea was dropped as impractical (Rydell 1984). The village had a Catholic church and a theatre, where the Filipino band played the American national anthem (Rydell 1984). The anthropologist Frederick Starr thought the concession a good one, and was taken with a Filipino girl born at the fair and named after Buchanan (Rydell 1984). The Buffalo Courier expected the villagers to carry news of American goodness home, and the next day reported that several of them had cried as they left the midway (Rydell 1984). Late in October a twenty-five-year-old woman died in hospital after catching tuberculosis in the village (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Rainbow City colour — The texts read the bright scheme as a deliberate ranking of peoples, with the strongest colours at the entrances and the palest near the Electric Tower, and as a break from the classical rule of American fairs (Rydell 1984; Greenhalgh 1988p. 129).
  • Electric power and lighting — Niagara power was the fair's theme, and the tower and landscape lighting are read as signs of technological progress; the texts disagree on the tower's height (Allwood et al. 2001p. 79; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 167; Maloney 2012p. 95).
  • Philippine and colonial display — The Philippine exhibit and the Filipino Village formed the imperial core of the fair, paid for by the federal government in part and by local businessmen in part (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Midway villages and racial hierarchy — Ethnological villages turned living people into exhibits along a path from savagery to civilisation (Rydell 1984; Greenhalgh 1988p. 100; Rydell 1993p. 22).
  • Pan-American trade and Latin America — Promised friendship and trade with Latin America drew few pavilions and largely failed, and the idea served American power (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 163; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Roche 2000p. 59).
  • McKinley's assassination — The texts agree the shooting shaped the fair's later memory and disagree about its meaning (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 170; Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 385).
  • Finance and failure — A deficit, lost stock and lawsuits followed, and the texts give different figures for the cost and outcome (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 162; Allwood et al. 2001p. 207; Badger 1979p. 131).

Questions for study

  1. How far should the Latin American theme be read as a sincere offer of friendship and trade, and how far as a cover for American power? The texts answer this differently (Rydell 1984; Roche 2000p. 59; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 163).
  2. What did it mean for a colonial display to be paid for partly by local businessmen rather than by the federal government?
  3. Does McKinley's assassination change the meaning of the fair's imperial message, or only make that message more visible?
  4. Why did the organisers combine colour, lighting and racial ordering in one plan, and what does the Rainbow City show about how visitors were taught to rank peoples?
  5. The texts disagree on attendance, cost and the height of the Electric Tower. How should a student choose between sources that give different figures for the same thing?
  6. Why do several texts treat Buffalo only in passing, as a step towards St Louis or as a list entry, and what does that suggest about how the history of world's fairs has been written?
  7. Buchanan judged the Ethnology Building's results weak because no one had a commercial motive for them (Rydell 1984), while Benedict valued the midway's disclaimed science (Rydell 1984). What did each of them think a fair was for, and how did that shape the claims the fair made about race?
  8. Weimann records an existing clubhouse used for the women's building, yet at Omaha a board of women presided over a boys' and girls' building (Weimann 1981p. 597). Why did the Buffalo organisers not give women a building of their own, and what do the texts say about who decided?
  9. The films were made at the margins of the fair, but the crew did not film Niagara Falls (Hollengreen et al. 2014p. 359). What did the fair want its visitors not to see, and how far did the films follow that choice?

Greenhalgh, Paul. Ephemeral Vistas: The Expositions Universelles, Great Exhibitions and World’s Fairs, 1851–1939. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1988.
4Human showcasesp. 82Asia

Greenhalgh examines the practice of displaying people at international exhibitions between 1889 and 1914, when colonised and other non-Western peoples were housed in reconstructed villages and watched by paying crowds. He sorts the displays into four types (imperial, educational, commercial and ambassadorial) but argues that they overlapped, and that the imperial type was the most numerous and lavish. The chapter works through the Paris 1889 and 1900 native villages, the Jardin d'Acclimatation's peopled exhibits that preceded them, Imre Kiralfy's London spectacles, and the Chicago, Buffalo and St Louis fairs, where Native American, Filipino and Dahomeyan residents were placed within a supposed evolutionary hierarchy. Commercial examples include the Cairo Street and the Indian tea-houses, and the Senegalese and Dahomeyan troupes, who toured under private entrepreneurs, receive detailed attention. Greenhalgh concludes that the displays did lasting damage by teaching mass audiences that non-European peoples were inferior, and that this harm was never repaired.

6.2Film, Technology, and Imperialism at the Pan-American Exposition, 1901Brian R. Jacobsonp. 349

Jacobson examines the films Edison's cameramen James White and Edwin Porter made at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo in 1901. The argument is that the films reproduce the fair's logic of transforming nature through technology, and that their margins reveal more than their foreground attractions do. Wikipedia

The Pan-American Exposition was a world's fair held in Buffalo, New York, from May 1 through November 2, 1901. The fair occupied 350 acres (0.55 mi2) of land on the western edge of what is now Delaware Park, extending from Delaware Avenue to Elmwood Avenue and northward to Great Arrow Avenue. It is remembered today primarily for being the location of the shooting of United States president William McKinley at the Temple of Music on September 6, 1901. The exposition was illuminated at night. Thomas A. Edison, Inc. filmed it during the day and captured a pan of it at night.
