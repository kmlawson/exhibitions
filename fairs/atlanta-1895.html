Atlanta · 1895

Cotton States and International Exposition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Cotton States and International Exposition ran in Atlanta from 18 September to 31 December 1895, on 189 acres in Piedmont Park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). Atlanta's business leaders staged it after the depression of 1893 to promote the cotton states and the New South, and to open Latin American markets (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134). Historians study it for Booker T. Washington's opening address, for its Negro Building, the first of its kind at a US exposition, and for its Midway villages, which Rydell reads as displays of racial hierarchy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135; Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of the depression that began in 1893. William A. Hemphill, business manager of the Atlanta Constitution, called for a larger fair, and some 300 businessmen met to plan it on 28 December 1893 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). The company's charter set out to show the products and resources of the Cotton States and of other countries, to develop trade with Latin America and to attract outside investors (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). Garfinkle, in the encyclopedia edited by Findling and Pelle, calls it the third, largest and most ambitious of Atlanta's cotton expositions, planned during the panic of 1893 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134). Rydell places it in the national search for new markets. He quotes the organisers' circular, which argued that overproduction made trade beyond the home market a necessity (Rydell 1984). The "New South" was the programme of making the poor South a partner in national and international economic growth (Rydell 1984).

Organisation and finance

Finance was the fair's weak point, and the texts give conflicting figures. Garfinkle reports a guarantee fund of $209,000 from private subscription and the city council, with further money from Congress, concession fees and space rent, and says the company ultimately spent $2,000,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134). Duncan, in the dictionary edited by Findling and Pelle, gives citizens' subscriptions of $134,000, a city appropriation of $75,000 and convict labour from Fulton County worth about $100,000. Samuel M. Inman gave $50,000 when the sheriff threatened to close the gates. Duncan puts the company's outlay at nearly $1.5 million, and about $2.5 million with exhibitors' costs (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). Rydell describes how the directors, railroads and brewing companies bought $250,000 of the first $300,000 in bonds, and Inman bought half of a later $100,000 issue (Rydell 1984). Congress appropriated $200,000 for a government building (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139), but it made the money conditional on a separate building for African American exhibits, the Negro Building (Rydell 1984).

Site and architecture

Grant Wilkins laid out the grounds around a plaza and a 13-acre lake, Clara Meer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). The reference works disagree on the architect's share. Garfinkle credits Bradford L. Gilbert with ten of the thirteen principal structures (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135), while Duncan gives him eleven of the thirteen main buildings (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). Garfinkle describes an architecture inspired by early Romanesque design, with Georgia pine framing (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). Duncan says Elsie Mercur's Women's Building used a colonial motif (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135).

Exhibits and attractions

Both reference works give 6,000 exhibits from 37 states and 13 foreign countries (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140). The Midway, the amusement avenue of concessions south of the main grounds, held a German village, a Moorish palace, a Dahomey village, a Japanese village with a tea garden, an Old Time Plantation and the Streets of Cairo, together with the Phoenix Wheel and the Phantascope film projector (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). Rydell argues that the Atlanta midway copied Chicago's: Edmund A. Felder, who had begun his exposition work as Daniel Burnham's assistant in Chicago, took charge of the midway attractions, and the Ferris wheel became a phoenix wheel (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).

Black participation and Booker T. Washington

Two events defined the fair's racial politics. The first was the Negro Building. Garfinkle calls it the most notable exhibit, and reports that the fair's success was partly due to early solicitation of African American support (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). The text quotes the Official Guide: "This is the first instance in the history of expositions where a building has been especially devoted to the Negro race" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). Duncan calls it the first exhibit of its kind in U.S. exposition history (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140). Benedict notes that it was designed and constructed by Blacks, and that its exhibits included a banking system, the Grand Fountain, organised by an ex-slave (Benedict 1983p. 41).

The second was Washington's opening address, later known as the Atlanta Compromise. Garfinkle presents it as the first occasion in the post-Civil War South on which a Black speaker addressed an integrated public audience (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). Washington told the audience that "in all things that are purely social, we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress" (Rydell 1984). Rydell notes that the directors invited him only after a committee persuaded the reluctant president, Collier, who had preferred a separate ceremony (Rydell 1984). Burris judges that many blacks saw the federal money as a token meant to secure cheap labour for an industry expanding overseas, and that audiences were segregated and blacks could buy refreshments only in the Negro Building (Burris 2001p. 168; Burris 2001p. 168).

I. Garland Penn, a black schoolteacher and attorney, headed the Negro department after Washington declined the post because of his duties at Tuskegee (Rydell 1984). Penn proposed that Washington speak at the opening (Rydell 1984). Before the fair opened, John C. Buckner, an Illinois legislator, opposed federal aid until lynching was stopped, and a meeting at Big Bethel Church in Atlanta protested the small colored exhibit, Jim Crow cars and convict labour (Rydell 1984). Once the fair was open, Rydell says that all audiences in the Auditorium were segregated and that private restaurant concessions refused black customers (Rydell 1984). Garfinkle describes Washington's speech as the first time a Black speaker addressed an integrated audience in the post-war South (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). Rydell's account makes that claim hard to accept as it stands (Rydell 1984). The editor of the People's Advocate, a local black paper, warned that "The Fair is a big fake" (Rydell 1984).

Latin America and overseas markets

Latin America was the fair's chief foreign target. Isaac W. Avery, the fair's commissioner to South America, visited seven Latin American countries from October 1894 over nine months (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). Fair America, by Rydell, Findling and Pelle, says he persuaded Latin American leaders that their interests lay with the new industries of the South, though some feared US intervention (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell records that Avery wrote of establishing United States banks with agencies in the Latin American states, and of direct steamship lines (Rydell 1984). Mexico, Venezuela, Costa Rica, Argentina and Chile built official buildings (Rydell 1984). Rydell says the Latin American display at Atlanta was unmatched until Buffalo in 1901 (Rydell 1984).

Collier wore a miniature of Simón de Bolívar in his lapel throughout the exposition months, as a sign of his aim to free Latin America from European influence (Rydell 1984). T. H. Martin, editor of Dixie, proposed joint expositions in South America and Mexico. He sent 2,000 letters to American manufacturers; 735 questionnaires came back, 616 endorsed the plan and 103 said they could send exhibits at once (Rydell 1984). Businessmen from across the country met in Cincinnati in September 1895, formed the National Association of Manufacturers and endorsed Atlanta's fair (Rydell 1984). The directors raised awards to Latin American exhibitors whose goods mattered to American manufacturers (Rydell 1984).

Visitors, reception and aftermath

The attendance figures do not agree. Garfinkle says fewer than 800,000 paid to see the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134). Duncan gives 779,560 people who paid (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140). Fair America gives 780,000 visitors in the three-month run (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell gives 1,286,863 people who traveled to the exposition (Rydell 1984), and his endnote says these figures count total visitors (Rydell 1984). The New York Times reported in December 1895 that the fair's image was "infinitely greater than was expected by even the most enthusiastic and patriotic of its managers" (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Unfinished buildings kept early attendance low (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135). The fair fell short of financial success by only $25,000, though organisers claimed it stimulated investment and construction (Findling & Pelle 2008). Duncan doubts that claim (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140). ## How historians have interpreted it Rydell's reading is the most far-reaching. Rydell argues that the southern fairs joined sectional reconciliation to a racial order and to overseas expansion, so that Washington's speech belonged to a fair that fixed class relations inside a racial hierarchy (Rydell 1984). Rydell concludes that the southern fairs projected progress defined as Anglo-Saxon racial superiority together with international economic expansion (Rydell 1984). Rydell also argues that the directors used Negro departments as instruments of social control (Rydell 1984). Fair America, by Rydell, Findling and Pelle, reads the fair as a response to the crisis of the 1890s. Its authors see the fairs as part of a nationalizing synthesis that linked progress to overseas economic expansion (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Burris accepts that Atlanta gave blacks more visibility than the northern fairs but asks that the gain be strongly qualified (Burris 2001p. 168). Greenhalgh argues that anti-suffrage forces took over women's sections after 1893, and gives Atlanta as an example (Greenhalgh 1988p. 183). The historians agree that Washington's speech and the Negro Building were central. They differ on whether they gave African Americans a platform or served social control. Garfinkle stresses early African American support for the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135), while Rydell and Burris stress its limits (Rydell 1984; Burris 2001p. 168).

Villages on the Midway

The Indian Village on Midway Heights was organised by C. P. Jordan of the Rosebud Indian Agency. It re-enacted the events of the early 1890s that ended at Wounded Knee. Many of the Sioux on show had joined the Ghost Dance movement and had fought United States troops, and they lived in lodges that federal troops had raked with Gatling gun fire, transported to the Midway and set up again (Rydell 1984). A woman and her young son, both shot in those lodges, were among the exhibits (Rydell 1984).

The Mexican Village was a three-acre attraction built by James Porteus's Mexican Village Exposition Company, with a working replica of a coffee plantation. Managers planned a bullfight, but protests about cruelty to animals cancelled it (Rydell 1984). Cooper claimed that the "live subjects were nowise disconcerted" by their display. Rydell calls the claim untrue (Rydell 1984).

Smithsonian displays and the Dabney controversy

Charles W. Dabney, assistant secretary of agriculture and chairman of the federal board for the government exhibit, told a Richmond Times reporter that the Smithsonian would show the evolution of the Negro from the earliest animals, through the ape and chimpanzee, to the South African Bushman (Rydell 1984). Dabney then wrote to Goode to say that he had been misquoted and had spoken only of anthropoid apes (Rydell 1984). Goode called the newspaper story ludicrous and denied planning any such exhibit (Rydell 1984).

The Atlanta and Nashville anthropology displays still ranked peoples by grades of culture (Rydell 1984). W. J McGee described the Seri as probably the most primitive Indians remaining in North America (Rydell 1984). Mason's chief contribution was an exhibit of modern savagery first made for Chicago, and he placed life-size figures of Native American, African and Polynesian women in economic roles at the lower grades of culture (Rydell 1984). Goode hoped the southern displays would encourage public museums in the cities of the South (Rydell 1984).

Money, labour and the railways

Badger's appendix of federal participation lists $200,000 for Atlanta, $130,000 for Nashville and $5,840,329.64 for Chicago (Badger 1979p. 132; Badger 1979p. 132).

The sound of chain-gang hammers filled the grounds while the fair was built (Rydell 1984). Atlanta's officials estimated that using chain-gang labour to dig about a million yards of earth saved them more than $100,000 (Rydell 1984). Duncan counts the Fulton County convict labour as a contribution worth about $100,000, which makes it a valuation rather than a saving (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139). Rydell's notes cite a March 1897 exchange between John W. Thomas and Stuart R. Knott, which puts the gain to the Southern Railway from the Atlanta fair at $500,000 (Rydell 1984).

Women's Building

Weimann says that Elise Mercur of Pittsburgh won the design competition over more than thirty other applicants (Weimann 1981p. 597). Rebecca Felton was appointed chair of the Atlanta board and raised money for the building by producing a special women's edition of the Atlanta Journal (Weimann 1981p. 597). The board had forty-two women, among them Sallie Cotten and Lily Irene Jackson of West Virginia, and Weimann concludes that the Columbian Woman's Building's splendour was never repeated (Weimann 1981p. 597).

China and the Chinese Village

Rydell gives the fullest account of the Chinese at Atlanta. Georgia's Chinese residents, few in number, protested against the Chinese concession. They accused its organisers, Leong Lam and Kee Owyang, of profiting from illegal transport of Chinese and from selling women into prostitution. Rydell notes that the villagers had little control over their own treatment (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984). At the fairs, Congressional action exempted employees of the Chinese Villages from the restrictions of the Geary Act, although Treasury officials kept them under close watch (Rydell 1984). Collier, the fair's president, had met difficulties with immigration legislation while trying to secure the concession (Rydell 1984). When Chinese bound for Midway Heights arrived at Atlanta's depot, more than 2,500 onlookers gathered to watch them (Rydell 1984).

Japan and the Midway villages

Garfinkle lists a Japanese village with a tea garden among the attractions of Midway Heights (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135), and Duncan names Mexican and Japanese villages among the fair's attractions (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140). The texts do not name the Japanese village's organisers or say what it displayed. Rydell argues that the Old Plantation, like the villages of Chinese, Japanese and Mexicans on the Atlanta and Nashville midways, conferred colonial status on its inhabitants (Rydell 1984). He adds that the villages, with their Asians, Native Americans and Africans, presented the New South as an Anglo-Saxon society ready to lead the nation in the imperial duties of subjugation and uplift (Rydell 1984).

Asia in the speeches and markets

The texts give no South Asian display at Atlanta, and name no Asian commissioners, visitors or observers. India appears only as a comparison. Judge Emory Speer, speaking straight after Washington, held up English rule over forty million dark-skinned people in India as a model (Rydell 1984). Collier, writing in Dixie, the Atlanta manufacturers' journal, said the race question was hardly less perplexing in India, where caste is entrenched (Rydell 1984). Rydell also records that the southern fairs' message included exports through southern ports to Latin America and Asia (Rydell 1984). The Nicaraguan canal that the promoters favoured was to open trade routes between southern ports and Asia (Rydell 1984).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Race and the New South — The texts treat the fair as a showcase for a racial order in which Black labour was held to serve the region's economic growth (Rydell 1984; Burris 2001p. 168).
  • Black participation and the Negro Building — The Negro Building is the fair's best-known exhibit, but the texts disagree on whether it extended opportunity or served segregation (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140; Rydell 1984; Burris 2001p. 168).
  • Latin American markets — Avery's campaign and the official Latin American buildings made trade with South America a central purpose (Rydell 1984; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Finance and the company — The company's outlay and funding figures conflict between the reference works, and the directors controlled the bonds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 139; Rydell 1984).
  • Midway villages and colonial display — The villages put Asian, Mexican and African peoples on show as lesser peoples (Rydell 1984; Rydell 1984).
  • Women's buildings — The women's building was designed by Mercur and became a site for anti-suffrage display (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 135; Greenhalgh 1988p. 183; Weimann 1981p. 597).
  • Visitors and reception — Attendance figures conflict between 779,560, 780,000, under 800,000 and 1,286,863 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 134; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 140; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell 1984).

Questions for study

  1. Why do the texts give different figures for the company's outlay, the number of buildings Gilbert designed and the attendance, and which figure should a student rely on?
  2. Did the Negro Building and Washington's address give African Americans a platform, or did they confine them, and what evidence would decide between the readings of Garfinkle, Burris and Rydell?
  3. How far were the Midway villages entertainment, and how far did they claim scientific standing, as Rydell argues?
  4. Why did Latin American markets matter more to the planners than Asian ones, given that the Nicaraguan canal was linked to Asian trade?
  5. How did Atlanta's women's building compare with Chicago's, and what did the anti-suffrage use of women's sections mean for women's exhibits?
  6. How did later fairs, such as Philadelphia in 1926 and Dallas's Texas Centennial, draw on Atlanta's example, and why did their officials fear a speech like Washington's?
  7. Dabney's report and Goode's denial disagree on what the Smithsonian planned to show. Which account can a student trust, and what would the exhibit catalogues need to show to settle it?
  8. Garfinkle describes an integrated audience for Washington's speech, while Rydell says the Auditorium audiences were segregated. What evidence would decide between them?
  9. Weimann describes a women's board of forty-two and a building that lacked the Chicago splendour. How far did the anti-suffrage use of women's sections, which Greenhalgh describes, shape what that board chose to show?

Burris, John P. Exhibiting Religion: Colonialism and Spectacle at International Expositions, 1851–1893. Charlottesville: University Press of Virginia, 2001.
Conclusion: A Parliament for the World’s Religions?p. 167

Burris's conclusion follows what happened after the 1893 Parliament, from the Christian and academic commentary that interpreted it to the Atlanta exposition of 1895, which gave African Americans a separate building but segregated audiences. It then turns to the 1993 centenary meeting in Chicago, retitled 'A Parliament for the World's Religions', which drew neo-pagans and Native American groups but few mainline Protestants, Catholics or Jews. Burris describes the scientist Gerald Barney's appeal to religious leaders on population and food, an Orthodox metropolitan's rejection of the idea that religion could reshape a scientific and economic order, and the failure of Hans Küng's 'Global Ethic' statement to win the assembly's endorsement. Burris argues that the 1893 exclusions rested on a hierarchy of primitive and civilised peoples inherited from the exposition tradition, and that the field of religion has not fully broken from it. The chapter ends by urging the discipline to keep its categories from absorbing the political climate in which they were formed.

Rydell, Robert W. All the World’s a Fair: Visions of Empire at American International Expositions, 1876–1916. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.
3The New Orleans, Atlanta, and Nashville Expositions: New Markets, “New Negroes,” and a New SouthAsia

This chapter examines the southern expositions at New Orleans (1885), Atlanta (1895) and Nashville (1897) as a regional version of the fair's ideology of progress, one that tied the New South's recovery to new markets in Latin America and Asia and to the subordination of black Americans. Rydell argues that these fairs were not simple imitations of Philadelphia and Chicago, since their directors addressed the 'Negro question' through separate Negro departments. The evidence includes Booker T. Washington's Atlanta address, the Negro buildings and the responses of black leaders, who both accepted and resisted the terms, and the Old Plantation and Midway villages that displayed Mexicans, Chinese and Native Americans. The chapter also covers Isaac Avery's campaign to win Latin American exhibitors, the railroads' and northern investors' stake in the fairs, and the Smithsonian's evolutionary displays. It concludes that the southern fairs joined race and progress in a New South ideology that prepared the way for Plessy v. Ferguson and immigration restriction, and that the Parthenon at Nashville became its lasting monument.

In the contents of this work

Rydell, Robert W., John E. Findling and Kimberly D. Pelle. Fair America: World’s Fairs in the United States. Washington, DC: Smithsonian Institution Press, 2000.
Introduction

The introduction presents world's fairs as a formative part of modern life, drawing around 100 million visitors to American fairs between 1876 and the First World War and shaping amusement parks, technologies and city design. It then sorts the scholarship on fairs into six schools, from producer-centred readings of cultural hegemony and audience-centred accounts of how visitors made their own meanings, to counter-hegemonic studies of the people displayed, Benedict's potlatch model with Susman's liminality, documentary histories of technology, and the collectors' tradition. Rydell, Findling and Pelle argue that American fairs carried the political reconstruction of the nation after the Civil War into culture, moving from the Crystal Palace model through Philadelphia in 1876 to Chicago in 1893 and Atlanta in 1895, and promoting overseas expansion and racial hierarchy as remedies for class conflict. The authors close by asking whether fairs, now tied to transnational corporations, will address the nuclear and ecological crises of the present or leave us whistling in the dark.

In the contents of this work

1Fairs in the Age of Industrialism’s Advance

Rydell, Findling and Pelle trace the world's fair from London's Crystal Palace of 1851, which drew over six million visitors and set the model for later fairs, to the American fairs of the industrial era. Their main cases are the failed New York Crystal Palace of 1853, the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial, where foreign exhibits were arranged by race and Smithsonian displays cast Native Americans as savage counterparts to civilised whites, and a run of southern fairs in Atlanta, New Orleans and Nashville that sought industrial and export markets. Chicago's 1893 Columbian Exposition receives the fullest treatment: its planning and White City design, the Midway's commercial exoticism, the anthropology displays of Putnam and Boas, and the Colored People's Day that Douglass and Ida B. Wells contested. The authors argue that fairs from 1876 to 1897 were run by business elites to persuade middle-class visitors of Anglo-Saxon superiority. The chapter ends with the turn after 1898, when the acquisition of an overseas empire gave fairs a new imperial purpose.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Rydell, Findling and Pelle write the history of world's fairs held in the United States, from the Crystal Palace in London in 1851 and Philadelphia in 1876 to the New York and San Francisco fairs of 1939-40, the postwar fairs and a closing look at Seville in 1992 and Hannover in 2000. The book runs in six chapters, from industrial fairs of 1876-97 and imperial fairs of 1898-1915 through interwar and atomic-age fairs to a conclusion on whether fairs can help the species survive.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Asia is a substantial but uneven theme, concentrated in Chapter 2, 'Fairs of the Imperial Era', where the Philippines is the central case. East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia appear occasionally, as examples within a wider argument about display. About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Burris's book is a cultural history of how the international exposition tradition helped create the academic field of religion. Its cases are the Great Exhibition of 1851 in London, the Paris expositions of 1867 and 1889, the New York Crystal Palace of 1853, the Philadelphia Centennial of 1876 and the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, whose World's Parliament of Religions is the central event. Burris argues that fair displays of peoples as primitive or civilised carried social evolutionism into comparative religion, and that the Parliament's exclusions of Native Americans and African Americans followed the fair's cultural hierarchy. The work is limited to Britain and the United States. It has an introduction, five chapters, a conclusion on the 1993 centenary meeting, and notes, bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a substantial strand, concentrated in chapter 4, 'Exhibitionism, American Style', and chapter 5, 'Exhibiting Religion at Chicago's Columbian Exposition'. On the web

Wikipedia

The Cotton States and International Exposition was a world's fair held in Atlanta, Georgia, United States in 1895. The exposition was designed "to foster trade between southern states and South American nations as well as to show the products and facilities of the region to the rest of the nation and Europe." The Cotton States and International Exposition featured exhibits from six states, including various innovations in agriculture and technology, and exhibits about women and African Americans. President Grover Cleveland presided over the opening of the exposition remotely by flipping an electric switch from his house in Massachusetts on September 18, 1895. The event is best remembered for the "Atlanta Compromise" speech given by Booker T. Washington on September 18, promoting racial cooperation.
