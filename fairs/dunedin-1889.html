Dunedin · 1889–1890

New Zealand and South Seas Exhibition

OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The New Zealand and South Seas Exhibition was held at Dunedin, South Island, in 1889-90, and closed in mid-April 1890 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118). A private company organised it with government backing, as the official celebration of the colony's first fifty years under British rule (Filipová 2015p. 241; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118). Rebecca Rice argues that its art displays set colonial works in a historical frame, so that critics saw colonial art as past (Filipová 2015p. 241; Filipová 2015p. 244). The texts give no opening date.

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The exhibition was held to commemorate the jubilee of the 1840 proclamation of British sovereignty over New Zealand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Thomson dates the celebration from annexation and the Treaty of Waitangi (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). He presents the fair as a sign of recovery: it "marked the return of business confidence after the lean years of the long depression" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Thomson traces the idea to the 1887 Adelaide Jubilee Exhibition, which gave D. Hastings Harris his first inspiration (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117).

Organisers and finance

Thomson says business support was lukewarm until R.E.N. Twopeny, a newspaper editor who had been involved in the Christchurch exhibition, lent his voice to the cause (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). A guarantee company with an initial capital of £10,000 was formed in October 1888 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). The government gave a £10,000 grant, free use of the state-owned rail and postal services, and recognition of the fair as the jubilee celebration (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Rice also describes a private company whose specialist committees secured government support for the buildings and for several exhibits (Filipová 2015p. 241). McCarthy says the company was private and received a government subsidy (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). He names Twopeny and Jules Joubert as the leading officials. Both had fair experience in Australia, and both had been involved in a Christchurch exhibition of 1882 that suffered a substantial loss (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Appendix C names John Roberts as president, Twopeny as executive commissioner and Joubert as general manager (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 419).

McCarthy reports a modest profit and an attendance of more than 600,000 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Thomson gives 625,248 admissions by mid-April and a profit of £579 once local investors' capital is counted. Shareholders lost much of their investment, but most were local businessmen whose losses were more than compensated by increased spending in the city (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118).

Site and buildings

Thomson describes a site of twelve and a half acres, a mile from the city centre, donated by the Otago Harbour Board (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). He says James Hislop designed temporary buildings, mostly of corrugated iron, that covered 75 per cent of the site (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). McCarthy says the site was chosen after some controversy on Harbour Board property, and that the art galleries were brick, with a domed classical entrance by the local architect James Hislop (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Rice says a purpose-built brick building held the Fine Arts Gallery (Filipová 2015p. 241).

Exhibits and attractions

The Fine Arts Gallery was the main art display. Rice says it held some 1,500 works in six galleries, with New Zealand works grouped together (Filipová 2015p. 241). McCarthy calls it the most important art display ever held in the colony of New Zealand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109). Eleven works by John Gully were lent by their owners and not entered for awards. Rice reads them as a retrospective that made Gully's colonial work a thing of the past (Filipová 2015p. 241; Filipová 2015p. 245). Thomson says the company secured the best paintings from Melbourne and showed them beside New Zealand works (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Rice says the government was asked to request the loan of British pictures from the Melbourne Centennial Exhibition of 1888-89 (Filipová 2015p. 241).

Thomson names the Early History, Maori and South Seas Court, with the Natural History court and the government-backed Armaments and Fisheries courts, as among the most popular (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Rice says it set out to show the colony's history and prehistory in text, picture and artefact (Filipová 2015p. 241). McCarthy says it reflected interest in ethnology and in Pakeha explorers and pioneers (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). The Maori court brought together displays by collectors F.R. Chapman, John White, Gilbert Mair, Walter Buller and Augustus Hamilton (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109). Buller lent a pataka, a food storehouse, and Hamilton exhibited a partly reconstructed wharenui, or meeting house (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109). Rice notes five oil portraits of Maori by Eleanor Mair, lent by her husband Gilbert Mair, and Lindauer's The Maori at Home (1885), which had been the centrepiece of Walter Buller's collection at the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London in 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 244). She argues that hanging them in the historical court encouraged viewers to read them as historical or ethnographic records rather than as art (Filipová 2015p. 244).

McCarthy describes an anthropometrical laboratory in the science court, run by H.O. Forbes of the Canterbury Museum and modelled on the 1884 Health Exhibition in London, which measured 10,000 visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Thomson adds a small amusement area with a switchback railway and sideshows (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). McCarthy also lists a concert hall and a garden and fernery (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

Who took part

Exhibits came from European countries, Great Britain, the Australian colonies, Japan, and several Pacific Islands, including Fiji, Samoa, the Solomons and Hawaii (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Thomson says exhibitors came from Europe, America and Asia, but that the most important were from New Zealand, Australia and the South Pacific (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). On Maori, McCarthy finds, compared with the first Dunedin exhibition of 1865, little evidence of a Maori presence at the exhibition either as exhibitors or as visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109). The Maori presence in these texts comes through objects lent by Pakeha collectors, a reconstructed wharenui, and portraits of Maori by Eleanor Mair and Gottfried Lindauer (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109; Filipová 2015p. 244).

Empire and colony

The texts describe a colonial exhibition held in a British colony and presented as the jubilee of British sovereignty (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Thomson links the colony's £10,000 grant and its free use of state rail and postal services to that jubilee (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). The organisers wanted the fair to "promote closer economic and political relations with the Australian colonies and the Pacific Islands" (Filipová 2015p. 241). McCarthy gives the same aim (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108), and Thomson describes it as closer relations with the Australian colonies and the Pacific Islands (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Rice doubts that the aim was achieved (Filipová 2015p. 241). The entries named by McCarthy came from Britain, the Australian colonies, Japan and the Pacific Islands (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

Rice rejects the reading of fairs as vehicles for winning support for imperial policy, which she attributes to Rydell, Findling and Pelle, as a fit for a small colony such as New Zealand (Filipová 2015p. 233; Filipová 2015p. 234). Rice notes that New Zealand first entered the fine art galleries of international exhibitions as one of Britain's colonies at London in 1886 (Filipová 2015p. 234). The passages describe no native village and no display of people. The Maori presence is shown through collections, paintings and portraits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

Visitors and reception

Thomson gives 625,248 people passing the gates by mid-April, a largest one-day attendance of 18,434 at the closing, and a New Zealand population of just over 660,000 at that time (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118). Rice says that more than 600,000 attended, more than the colony's population (Filipová 2015p. 241). Rice says the 1889 reviewers were less congratulatory than those of 1865 (Filipová 2015p. 242). The primary fine arts reviewer, presumably Malcolm Ross, noted that New Zealand artists had a natural advantage in landscape, but wrote that the colony was most behindhand in genre painting (Filipová 2015p. 242; Filipová 2015p. 242; Filipová 2015p. 243). The judges wanted more work with the figure, and Rice notes that historical and genre works were largely absent, a fact that judges and reviewers both lamented (Filipová 2015p. 243; Filipová 2015p. 243).

Its place in the series

The texts disagree about how to count the Dunedin fairs. Rice calls the 1865 and 1889-90 exhibitions the colony's two major international exhibitions of the nineteenth century (Filipová 2015p. 235). McCarthy calls the 1889-90 fair the third world's fair in New Zealand (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108), and Thomson counts Dunedin 1865 and Christchurch 1882 as two small international exhibitions before it (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). McCarthy's Christchurch entry says the 1906-07 fair was held sixteen years after the Dunedin exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 187). Neale says Joubert, who was general manager at Dunedin, came next to Tasmania (Filipová 2015p. 253). She also notes that the recent Dunedin exhibition, held outside the capital Wellington, was mentioned in the Launceston debates as a precedent (Filipová 2015p. 253). The Hobart entry says the successes of the Dunedin exhibition and the Launceston exhibition motivated the Tasmanian International Exhibition of 1894-95 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 133). A biographical appendix records private exhibition ventures by Twopeny and Joubert, including one at Christchurch dated 1892, whereas McCarthy places their Christchurch exhibition in 1882 (Hoffenberg 2001p. 285; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108).

Aftermath and legacy

McCarthy says that four major works from the exhibition, including a large canvas of a market scene in Brittany by Stanhope Forbes, were bought by public subscription for Dunedin (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109). A public gallery was set up in part of the former exhibition buildings to house them (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 110). McCarthy calls the fair, as the 1865 fair had been, a considerable stimulus to building the infrastructure of the colonial art world (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 110). Rice notes that the Early History court closely reflected the collection of Thomas Morland Hocken, which would form the basis of the Hocken Library (Filipová 2015p. 243).

How historians have interpreted it

Three authors interpret the exhibition at length: Rebecca Rice, Conal McCarthy and David Thomson. Rice reads 1889 as the point at which the colonial moment was seen as past. Critics showed a new historical consciousness and anxiety about the next phase of national development in art, and she explains this with James Belich's concept of recolonisation (Filipová 2015p. 232; Filipová 2015p. 232; Filipová 2015p. 244). McCarthy reads the fair as a further stimulus to colonial art infrastructure (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 110). Thomson reads it as a gesture of confidence in the future, signalling hope after fifteen years of depression (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118). Marta Filipová's editor's introduction adds that the fine art displays at fairs on the geographical margins show the ambiguities of colonial culture (Filipová 2015p. 12).

Asian and Pacific participants

The texts name few Asian participants. McCarthy lists entries from Japan among those from European countries, Great Britain, the Australian colonies and several Pacific Islands (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108). Thomson says exhibitors came from Asia as well as Europe and America, but that the most important were from New Zealand, Australia and the South Pacific (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117). Neither text says which Asian exhibits were shown, who sent them, or whether any Asian people visited or took part in the displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Jubilee and loyalty — the fair was held to mark the jubilee of British sovereignty over New Zealand, and Thomson describes it as the official celebration of the colony's first fifty years (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118).
  • Trade and Pacific ties — the organisers' aim was closer economic and political relations with the Australian colonies and the Pacific Islands, which Rice doubts was achieved (Filipová 2015p. 241; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 108; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117).
  • Business confidence and finance — the fair marked a return of confidence after the depression, backed by a guarantee company and a government grant, and most shareholders lost money (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 117; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 118).
  • Maori and ethnographic display — the Maori court was built from collectors' objects, and McCarthy finds little Maori presence among exhibitors or visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 109; Filipová 2015p. 244).
  • Colonial art and historical consciousness — Rice argues that the 1889 displays and critics treated colonial art as history, with anxiety about the next phase (Filipová 2015p. 241; Filipová 2015p. 243; Filipová 2015p. 244).

Questions for study

  1. Thomson reads the fair as a gesture of confidence, and Rice as a sign of anxiety. What in the passages supports each reading, and can both hold at once?
  2. The Maori court was among the most popular courts, yet McCarthy finds little Maori presence among exhibitors or visitors. Who, on the evidence of these texts, were the displays for?
  3. Rice counts the two Dunedin fairs as the colony's two major international exhibitions; McCarthy calls the 1889-90 fair the third world's fair in New Zealand. What does a count like this depend on?
  4. The texts credit both the government and the company with obtaining Melbourne loans. Why might a colony borrow art from another colony, and how would the texts show it?
  5. Which evidence in the four passages would show whether the fair advanced trade or political ties with Australia or the Pacific Islands?

Filipová argues that exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia were not simply copies of London or Paris, but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion and nation-building. The introduction opens with George Wallis's report on the New York Exhibition of 1853, which compares American and European manufactures, to show how early the exhibition became a vehicle for national comparison. It then maps the volume's sections, on exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design, and international ambitions, and their case studies, from Budapest and Barcelona to Wolverhampton, Dunedin, Osaka and Rio de Janeiro. It extends the period beyond the conventional 1851 to 1939 frame, and concludes that marginal exhibitions show how universal values were combined with regional and national agendas, linking local modernities to a global one.

In the contents of this work

10A Red-Letter Day: Evaluating Progress in New Zealand’s Art at Dunedin’s International Exhibitions, 1865 and 1889Rebecca Ricep. 231

Rice compares the fine art sections of the two international exhibitions held in Dunedin, the New Zealand Exhibition of 1865 and the New Zealand and South Seas Exhibition of 1889-90, to ask what they show about progress in New Zealand art. Her evidence is mainly the critical reception. In 1865 reviewers, chiefly 'Rambler' (identified as William Fox), praised colonial watercolours as equal to the best work at home, and read the display as proof of colonial taste. By 1889 the verdicts were sharper: reviewers faulted New Zealand painters for relying on landscape and for having little genre or history painting. Rice explains the change through James Belich's idea of recolonization and through the new historical consciousness of 1889, shown in the Early History, Maori and South Seas Court and in a group of John Gully's works. She concludes that the 1865 exhibition helped build an art infrastructure, but that by 1889 the colonial moment was seen as past, which brought anxiety rather than triumph.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The volume, edited by Marta Filipová, gathers fifteen chapters on international exhibitions held in the regional and provincial margins of Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia between 1840 and 1940. Its argument is that these smaller events were not copies of London or Paris but adapted the medium to local agendas such as regional identity, municipal promotion, colonial and national self-presentation, linking local modernities to a global one. The case studies range from Budapest, Barcelona, Berlin and Bohemia to Wolverhampton, Rio de Janeiro, Ghent, London and Edinburgh, Dunedin, Launceston, Nancy, Helsinki, Naples and Osaka. The chapters are grouped in five parts: exhibition as a concept, constructing identities, historicity and modernity, art and design exhibited, and international ambitions.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive substantial attention, but it is concentrated in one chapter. Chapter 15, by Jeffer Daykin, on the 1903 Osaka Exposition, is the only study of an Asian host, and treats Japan as host, exhibitor and colonial power. Its Formosan section presented Taiwan as a foil that made Japan the civilised spectator, while the Jinrui Kan concession outside the grounds displayed live Chinese, Korean and Okinawan people, alongside Malayan and Javanese people among others. Chinese and Korean protests, backed by official pressure, led to the removal of the Chinese and Korean exhibits. Daykin also notes the Netherlands-India section's bilingual catalogue. Elsewhere the coverage is occasional. Muñoz Torreblanca (chapter 2, Barcelona 1888) notes a Tagalog cigarette-making group in a tobacco company pavilion and a thin Spanish colonial display that lacked the Philippine material shown at Madrid in 1887; the Chinese, Japanese and Turkish presence there was token. Depelchin (chapter 8, Ghent 1913) places a Philippine village on the edge of 'Old Flanders' and Japan among the international hall's participants. Rezende (chapter 7, Rio 1922) lists Japan among thirteen nations with pavilions and a Japan Day, alongside Japanese immigrants in Brazil. Filipová (chapter 6, Wolverhampton) notes Japanese arts and crafts in 1902, and Japanese enamels and Chinese weapons in the 1884 display. Albert (chapter 5, Bucharest 1906) mentions only a reproduction of Port Arthur. Siam, Indochina, Burma and Borneo do not appear in the passages.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

About the work as a whole Asia: slight

Hoffenberg's book examines popular exhibitions in England, Australia and India from the Great Exhibition of 1851 to the Festival of Empire in 1911. Its main argument is that exhibitions were social texts, written by commissioners and read in varied ways by visitors, and that they helped imagine a tripartite empire of England, India and settler Australia while masking inequalities of class, race and labour. The scope covers London, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Calcutta shows, along with the afterlife of their collections. The book is organised in eight chapters on commissioners, state power, commerce, the Australian and Indian courts, machinery, visitors and ceremonies, followed by a short epilogue, three appendices, notes and a bibliography.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia figure only slightly, and in passing, in a book centred on India, Australia and England. The preface describes a Chinese village with tea houses at the 1884 International Health Exhibition. Japanese visitors to London in 1862 are noted for their curiosity (chapter 7). At Adelaide in 1887 Japanese earthenware sold among 'Eastern goods', and the manager of the Japanese Village paid the commission a share of receipts (chapter 3). Chinese teas are the rivals that Indian tea merchants sought to displace in Australia (chapter 4), and Chinese commissioners in national costume lead the Adelaide opening procession (chapter 8). Malays and the inhabitants of Perak appear once, in a list of peoples at London in 1862 (chapter 6). East and Southeast Asian people appear as visitors, sellers or passing comparisons rather than as commissioners of their own courts, and the book does not discuss their participation. Chapter 5 says the 1886 Colonial and Indian Exhibition represented Australia and India in a self-contained imperial world without non-British societies, including China and the Ottoman Empire. Otherwise the regional material concerns India and Australia, with South Asia the main colonial case throughout.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Edited by Martin Lawn, this volume argues that world's fairs from London in 1851 onward shaped education through their displays of objects, classifications and comparisons between nations, and that the museums they spawned carried these materials into schools. Its 'materiality of education' approach studies objects, captions, displays and technologies, not fairs as temporary spectacle. The chapters, by different authors, cover Britain (London 1851, Wembley 1924, Glasgow 1888-1988), the United States (Chicago 1893 to San Francisco 1915), Germany, Spain, Argentina and Japan, and the introduction ends with a chronology of exhibitions from 1851 to New York 1939.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. Chapter 4, Komatsu on the Educational Museum in Ueno Park, is the only chapter built around East Asia, and it gives Japan's own participation close attention. It covers Japanese government and private exhibitors at Vienna in 1873 and Philadelphia in 1876, the first Domestic Exhibition of 1877, and the Educational Museum, where foreign apparatus was displayed beside Japanese copies made for domestic sale. Komatsu traces the museum's decline after 1885 and the replacement of Pestalozzian teaching by Herbartian method. Elsewhere the region appears only in passing, and there is no sustained account of Southeast Asia or of Chinese, Korean or Siamese participation. Chapter 5 (Sobe and Rackers) describes a Philippine ethnographic exhibit at St Louis in 1904, where one group judged 'most advanced' took daily lessons, as an instance of 'colonial schooling' shown to spectators. Chapter 6 (Grosvenor, Wembley 1924) lists Malays, Burmans and Hong Kong Chinese among the 'Races in Residence' and Chinese 'Christian natives' in mission bungalows, all within a racial hierarchy of empire, with India and Ceylon discussed far more fully. The introduction's chronology names Hanoi 1902-03 and Nanking 1910 without comment, and Lawn's chapter 1 notes a New South Wales museum's purchase of Chinese 'articles' in 1885. Glasgow's 'Oriental' architecture in chapter 9 is a style, not a reference to Asia.

Wikipedia

The New Zealand and South Seas Exhibition was an international exhibition held in Dunedin, New Zealand from 26 November 1889 to 19 April 1890. A previous exhibition had been held in 1865, the first world's fair in New Zealand, and a later exhibition opened in 1925.
