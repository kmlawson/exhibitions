Intercolonial Exhibition (Melbourne)

OverviewIn depthThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther reading

The Intercolonial Exhibition held at Melbourne in 1884 is known from two reference works, which list it by name, year and city in appendices of fairs excluded from their main bodies (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399). Both appendices describe their entries as fairs held after 1851 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). Neither text gives its dates, organisers, site, exhibits or visitors, and neither says why it matters (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

The record in the reference works

Both reference works list the fair only once. Findling and Pelle's encyclopedia gives it as 1884 Melbourne Intercolonial Exhibition in Appendix D, "Fairs Not Included" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424). The historical dictionary has the same entry in its own Appendix D (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399). Both appendices say the fairs they list fell short of the qualifications for their main bodies: many were very specialised, and others lacked a suitable international scope (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). The texts do not say which of these reasons applied to the Melbourne fair.

Other Melbourne fairs in the lists

Both appendices also list other Melbourne fairs: an 1854 International Exhibition, an 1861 Victorian Exhibition, 1866 and 1866-1867 Intercolonial exhibitions, an 1872 exhibition of natural products and works of art, and a 1875 Intercolonial Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395). The two lists differ over the 1866-1867 fair, which one gives as Intercolonial Exhibition of Australia and the other as of Australasia (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 396). Hoffenberg's list of major Australian exhibitions includes the 1866-67, 1875 and 1880-81 Melbourne fairs but not 1884. The texts do not connect the 1884 fair with any of these others.

Empire and colony

The texts show no imperial or colonial connection for this exhibition. They give its name, year and city only. They do not say who governed Melbourne at the time, which colonies or dominions took part, or what the organisers hoped the fair would do (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Excluded from the main body — both reference works place the fair in appendices of fairs that fell short of their inclusion qualifications (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395).
  • A single list entry — each work says nothing about the fair beyond its name, year and city (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 399).
  • Other Melbourne fairs — the same appendices list Melbourne fairs from 1854 to 1875, which the texts keep separate from the 1884 fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422p. 424; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 395p. 398).
  • Silence in Hoffenberg — the monograph's list of major Australian exhibitions includes Melbourne fairs of 1866-67, 1875 and 1880-81 but not 1884.

Questions for study

  1. Both reference works list the 1884 Melbourne fair, yet no other text in the set describes it. Which sources, such as official catalogues or newspapers, would show what it contained and who attended?
  2. The appendices say excluded fairs were very specialised or lacked international scope (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 422). Which of these, if either, fits the 1884 Melbourne fair, and what evidence would decide it?
  3. Several Melbourne fairs carry the name Intercolonial Exhibition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 423p. 424). How should a reader tell the 1884 fair apart from the earlier ones?
  4. Hoffenberg's list of major Australian exhibitions covers Melbourne fairs of 1866-67, 1875 and 1880-81 but not the 1884 fair. What would explain that omission?

Hoffenberg, Peter H. An Empire on Display: English, Indian, and Australian Exhibitions from the Crystal Palace to the Great War. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001.
7Imperial and National Taxonomies: Entertaining and Policing Exhibition Visitorsp. 203Asia

Hoffenberg turns from exhibits to the people who visited exhibitions and to the ways commissioners classified, entertained and policed them. Visitors to the Great Exhibition of 1851, the London International of 1862 and the Colonial and Indian Exhibition of 1886 are treated as a visible social mass that organisers sorted by nation, race, class and gender. The evidence includes reduced fares for London workers, the appeal to guilds from neighbouring colonies for the Melbourne Intercolonial of 1866 to 1867, and the controversial zenana day at the Calcutta International of 1883 to 1884, when women in purdah were admitted to the grounds. Maori and Fijian performers at Sydney in 1879, and Aboriginal visitors from mission stations at Adelaide in 1887, are set against the static ethnographic models shown elsewhere, and the separate entrances at Nagpore in 1865 show how race and rank were policed at the door. Hoffenberg argues that exhibitions offered social integration and imperial federation in economic and cultural terms, a citizenship that was real but not political. The chapter closes by linking these practices of ordering visitors to later management of migration in the twentieth century.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.
