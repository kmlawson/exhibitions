The West Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition (Sŏchosŏn mulsan kongjinhoe 西朝鮮物産共進會) was held in 1913 in Korea, then under Japanese colonial rule (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7). Young-sin Park calls it Korea's first kongjinhoe, a local industrial exhibition, and places it among the local exhibitions that were precursors of the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915 (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 6). The passage does not name the city, the organisers or the number of visitors (Parkp. 7).

Background

Park sets the local exhibitions within the colonial government's cultural events. The government held events such as a field day, lantern parades, a bicycle race and a writing contest (Parkp. 7). Local industrial exhibitions were held in this context, to develop local industries and mobilise local people for government-driven events (Parkp. 7). Park's account also sets them against the 1910 annexation, which gave Japan economic and political hegemony over East Asia and lent legitimacy to its claim to be the agency of modernising a backward region (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7).

The West Chosŏn exhibition

Park gives the event a name, a year and a status, and little more. The name in the text is "the West Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition" (Parkp. 7). The text says it was held in 1913 (Parkp. 7). Park does not give its location, organisers, exhibits or visitors. The same sentence refers to a series of local industrial exhibitions without dating any of them (Parkp. 7).

Local exhibitions and the 1915 exhibition

Park treats the local exhibitions as precursors of 1915. They "were important precursors of the 1915 exhibition" and extended the idea of expositions to a wider Korean public (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 6). Park says that the contrast between 'modern' and 'premodern' space was the most important feature of the 1915 exhibition, and that local industrial expositions would adopt the same strategy (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 6).

The article also records a separate 1913 step. In a note, Park writes that Governor-General Terauchi Masatake approved a plan for a 'great commemorational exposition' in Seoul that year, and changed its title from pangnamhoe to kongjinhoe (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7). The same note's title is tied to the 1915 exhibition (Parkp. 7). The passage therefore concerns Seoul and the 1915 event, not the West Chosŏn exhibition (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Local industrial exhibitions — Park treats kongjinhoe as local versions of the national exhibition and as its precursors (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 6).
  • Colonial cultural events — local exhibitions were staged alongside a field day, parades, a bicycle race and a writing contest, to develop local industries and mobilise local people (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7).
  • Modern and premodern space — the contrast between modern pavilions and old Korean space was the main feature of 1915, and local exhibitions would adopt it (Parkp. 6; Parkp. 6).
  • Annexation and legitimacy — Park places the local exhibitions in the context of the 1910 annexation and Japan's claim to modernise Korea (Parkp. 7; Parkp. 7).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the colonial government pair local industrial exhibitions with a field day, parades and a writing contest, and whom did it expect to persuade?
  2. The passage gives the West Chosŏn exhibition a name and a year but no city, organisers or visitors. Which sources would establish these facts?
  3. Park calls local exhibitions precursors of the 1915 exhibition. What evidence would show that the West Chosŏn exhibition shaped the planning of 1915, rather than only preceding it?
  4. How did the modern and premodern contrast work differently in a local exhibition from its use in the national exhibition of 1915?

Brief discussion 1

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
Introduction, passage on local industrial exhibitions before 1915 (printed p. 6)

Park's article on the 1915 Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition gives one paragraph to the West Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1913, which it calls Korea's first kongjinhoe. The text gives the event's Chinese-character name and says it was held in 1913, but it names no city, organisers, exhibits or visitors. The paragraph serves Park's larger argument about 1915: local exhibitions of the early 1910s are presented as precursors that used the same contrast between modern and premodern space, and the 1910 annexation is the setting for both.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
