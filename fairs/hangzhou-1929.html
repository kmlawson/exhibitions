West Lake Exposition

西湖博览会

The West Lake Exposition (Xihu bolanhui) was a major world exposition held at West Lake in Hangzhou. It opened in June 1929 and ran for 137 days (Denton 2014p. 47). It was primarily a commercial venture, supported by the Nationalist Party (KMT) and used to promote Chinese national products at home and abroad (Denton 2014p. 47; Denton 2014p. 86). Denton's chapter is the fullest treatment among the sources (Denton 2014p. 47). It argues that the exposition's Revolutionary Memorial Hall anticipated the revolutionary narratives of later Communist museums (Denton 2014p. 51).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

The exposition opened in June 1929 and lasted 137 days (Denton 2014p. 47). Denton describes it as a major world exposition, set up on the lines of the world's fairs held since the late nineteenth century (Denton 2014p. 47). Its purpose was to show the products of the host country and to place them in a global circulation of goods (Denton 2014p. 47). Denton calls it primarily a commercial venture, although not without political, and especially nationalist, implications (Denton 2014p. 47). It also served as a showcase for the Nationalist regime, which used it to display the material, educational and cultural progress made under its rule (Denton 2014p. 50).

Site and buildings

The fair occupied the West Lake shoreline, which was already imbued with literati and revolutionary memory. New and repurposed buildings transformed it again (Denton 2014p. 47). Its halls covered silk, hygiene, education, agriculture, art and industry, and it also had a natural history museum (Denton 2014p. 47). Private companies, among them the German dye firm Indanthren, set up booths (Denton 2014p. 47). The memorial site lay near Solitary Mountain at the north end of the Bai Juyi Causeway. It had a memorial archway, a library, a memorial tower, the hall proper and a Sun Yat-sen Hall (Denton 2014p. 48). The Sun Yat-sen Hall was hung with KMT symbols and served mainly as an auditorium for speeches and ceremonies (Denton 2014p. 48).

The Revolutionary Memorial Hall

Li Chaoying, the Zhejiang Provincial Party chair who directed the hall, wrote that it should stimulate revolutionary emotion and help construct a nation on the Three People's Principle (Denton 2014p. 48). Visitors first signed in and bowed three times before an image of Sun Yat-sen. They then followed a prescribed order through eight display rooms (Denton 2014p. 49). The displays covered martyrs, Sun Yat-sen, battles of the national army, national humiliations and the party's achievements (Denton 2014p. 49). Denton identifies four themes: revolutionary development, party affairs, the people's movement and Sun Yat-sen's posthumous teaching (Denton 2014p. 49). The martyrs room that opened the hall honoured Zhejiang heroes such as Xu Xilin and Qiu Jin (Denton 2014p. 50). The hall was the only free exhibition hall on the grounds (Denton 2014p. 51).

Visitors and reception

Denton gives the exposition more than twenty million visitors (Denton 2014p. 47). Not every visitor accepted the hall's political intention. Zhang Jiya wrote cynically that anyone who refused to bow before Sun's portrait might be taken for a counter-revolutionary (Denton 2014p. 48). Guan Juxian, in an essay Denton cites, judged the hall the most important on the grounds (Denton 2014p. 50). He argued that its force lay in its meaning rather than its objects. In Denton's quotation, "It is not the resplendent artifacts themselves but their deep inherent meaning that stirs in us memories of past injuries and stirs us to continue the unfinished work" (Denton 2014p. 51).

Politics, ceremony and consumerism

The opening and closing ceremonies used flag raising, homage to Sun Yat-sen and the national and party anthems (Denton 2014p. 50). KMT leaders and affiliated academics gave speeches, and one note names Dai Jitao as a speaker on the fair and cultural development (Denton 2014p. 275). Denton argues that the revolutionary discourse was at odds with the fair's spectacle of neon lights, fireworks, cinemas and dance halls (Denton 2014p. 50). The organisers tried to mitigate this tension in speeches and writings (Denton 2014p. 50). Consumerism was nonetheless framed as patriotism, in the call to buy national products (guohuo) (Denton 2014p. 51).

Aftermath and legacy

Mao-era museum displays do not mention the fair, because of its commercial and KMT agenda (Denton 2014p. 51). In recent years it has attracted renewed attention, especially in Hangzhou, where it is a source of local pride (Denton 2014p. 51). The text dates the renewal of West Lake expositions to 2000 (Denton 2014p. 51). A later note says a second West Lake Exposition, partly commemorating the first, was held in 2002 (Denton 2014p. 275). Denton does not reconcile the two dates. Hangzhou has a West Lake Expo Museum near the original site (Denton 2014p. 51p. 276). Denton finds much of the hall's Nationalist narrative in later CCP exhibitions, including the progress narrative, the guiding role of the party, the people as a source of legitimacy and Sun as father of the revolution (Denton 2014p. 51). The main difference is commerce: tying revolution to capitalist commerce would be inconceivable in Mao-era museums (Denton 2014p. 51).

Denton's later chapter describes a Republican-economy hall in the Nanjing Presidential Palace museum, which argues for incipient Chinese capitalism (Denton 2014p. 85). A photograph of the West Lake Exposition appears in that display. Denton reads the contemporary nostalgia for the fair as suggesting that the Republican era had global economic aspirations that resonate with China's present economic transformation (Denton 2014p. 86). Photographs of the fair also appear in other PRC modern-history museums, including the Presidential Palace in Nanjing (Denton 2014p. 275).

Foreign visitors

About one thousand of the visitors were foreigners, from places including the United States, Britain, Japan, Korea, Indonesia and India (Denton 2014p. 47). Denton gives no further detail on these visitors. The text says nothing about Japanese, Korean or Southeast Asian participation or exhibits, or about colonial displays.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Revolutionary memory in a commercial fair — Denton places the Revolutionary Memorial Hall within a fair whose main purpose was commerce, and treats the two as sitting side by side (Denton 2014p. 47p. 51).
  • Consumption as patriotism — buying national products was framed as patriotic, which Denton sets against the hall's revolutionary spirit (Denton 2014p. 51p. 50).
  • The museum against the fair — citing Tony Bennett, Denton contrasts the museum's structured pedagogy with the fair's popular spectacle, and notes that the hall was free of charge (Denton 2014p. 51).
  • Continuity with PRC museums — the hall's party-led narrative, martyrs and father figure reappear in CCP exhibitions (Denton 2014p. 51).
  • Nostalgia and local identity — the fair's revival in Hangzhou presents the city as having a commercial past (Denton 2014p. 51).

Questions for study

  1. Why did Denton frame consumerism as patriotism at a fair organised around revolutionary memory, and what does this tell us about how the KMT understood national economic development? (Denton 2014p. 51)
  2. Denton argues that the free Revolutionary Memorial Hall is in opposition to the fair's popular spectacle. How far does the hall's free entry support that claim? (Denton 2014p. 51)
  3. The text says Mao-era museums omit the fair. Why might a later state revive an exposition it once ignored, and what does the 2000 renewal suggest about the present? (Denton 2014p. 51)
  4. Denton gives 2000 and 2002 for the later West Lake expositions. How would you check these dates against another source? (Denton 2014p. 51p. 275)

2Exhibiting the Revolution: The Museum of the Chinese Revolutionp. 45Asia

Denton traces how the Museum of the Chinese Revolution in Beijing, the state's most official exhibition of party history, has represented the revolution since the 1920s. The chapter starts with the Nationalist Revolutionary Memorial Hall at the 1929 West Lake Exposition in Hangzhou, and argues that its party-led narrative of progress, its martyrs and its father figure of the revolution reappear in the Communist museum. It then follows the exhibits from the 1961 History of the Chinese Revolution, shaped by factional fights over Mao's place, through the Cultural Revolution merger and the post-Mao revisions of 1979 and 1990, to the Modern China hall of 1996 and Road to Revival in 2011. Denton shows how each version cut text, added art and dioramas, and retreated from Mao while keeping the telos of the party's victory. The chapter closes by arguing that the final exhibit elides the famine, the Cultural Revolution and Tiananmen, and presents modernization as the end point of revolutionary history.

Denton uses the 1929 West Lake Exposition as the starting point for her study of the Museum of the Chinese Revolution in Beijing. She presents the fair as a commercial exhibition sponsored by the Nationalists, whose Revolutionary Memorial Hall ran through the martyrs, party ideology, the people and Sun Yat-sen. She argues that this Nationalist narrative of revolution reappears in later Communist museums, even though the fair itself, with its consumer culture, is absent from Mao-era displays. In a later chapter she reads a photograph of the fair in a Nanjing museum as evidence of how the market economy of the reform era gives the Republican past a commercial foundation.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Denton's Exhibiting the Past surveys how museums and memorial sites in the People's Republic of China represent the national past, from dynastic times onward. The introduction sets out a narratological method that reads exhibits as visual stories and compares Chinese museums with memory museums elsewhere. The thirteen chapters are organised by museum type rather than by theme: premodern history, the Museum of the Chinese Revolution, revolutionary history under the market, martyrs' memorials, military museums, sites of Japanese atrocity, memorial halls for model heroes and leaders, ethnographic museums and theme parks, red tourism, and municipal urban planning halls. Denton argues that state museums remain politically directed while adapting to market reform, nationalism and the decline of socialism. The conclusion ties these displays to neoliberal narratives of past, present and future.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the book's whole subject, because every chapter treats museums and memorial sites in the PRC, so its coverage is central. The concern is how China displays its own past, not how Asian states or peoples take part in international exhibitions. Chapter 6 (Heroic Resistance and Victims of Atrocity) is the most concentrated treatment of another East Asian country: Japan is the aggressor in the War of Resistance Memorial Hall at Wanping, the Nanjing Massacre Memorial Hall and the Unit 731 Museum in Harbin. Chapter 1 (National Origins and Local Identity) treats Korea through the contested kingdom of Koguryo/Gaojuli. Taiwan and Hong Kong recur in chapters 1, 3, 5 and 6, as partners in a shared memory of resistance or as commercial context. Exhibitions in Asia and Asian visitors appear mainly in chapter 2, on the 1929 West Lake Exposition in Hangzhou, which the book says drew some one thousand foreign visitors, among them from Japan, Korea and Indonesia. Southeast Asia is slight. Vietnam appears as a comparison in chapter 2, through Hanoi's revolution museum, and in chapter 9, through its ethnic museum, while Indonesia, Thailand and Burma are cited only in a note to chapter 5 on military museums. No Southeast Asian pavilions, displays or peoples on show are discussed.
