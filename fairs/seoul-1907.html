Seoul Exhibition

京城博覽會 (Kyŏngsŏng pangnamhoe)
OverviewIn depthAsian participantsThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Kyŏngsŏng Exposition (Keijō hakurankai) opened in Seoul on 1 September 1907 on the Meiji-machi (Tonghyŏn) site in the Japanese settlement (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 94). Japanese organisers envisioned a collaborative exhibition, but Japanese merchants and the Residency-General dominated it (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 93). It drew about 200,000 visitors, nearly three-quarters of them Korean (Parkp. 3; Henry 2014p. 95). Henry calls it a precursor of the 1915 and 1929 expositions, which aimed, like it, to convince Koreans that Japanese rule could benefit them (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 94).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Background and aims

Park's opening section traces how the exposition idea reached Korea (Parkp. 3). King Kojong set up a Provisional Exposition Department in 1902, and a Provisional Exposition Display Hall, which Park calls Korea's first public exhibition site, followed in 1903 (Parkp. 6). The Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition held at Pusan in 1906 was the first event officially titled pangnamhoe, and after it came the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition (Parkp. 6). Henry agrees that Great Han officials took measures to host expositions before the protectorate, but says that such events took place only in the protectorate period (Henry 2014p. 93). His note calls the Pusan fair Korea's first exposition (Henry 2014p. 241). Henry places the Seoul fair months after Japanese officials forced a second protectorate treaty on the Korean government (Henry 2014p. 93). He says the organisers hoped to counter the opposition that the treaty had provoked, including the violent activities of the righteous armies (Henry 2014p. 93).

Organisers, finance and site

Henry says the fair was discussed as a collaborative event between Japanese and Korean businessmen, but was clearly dominated by the Japanese (Henry 2014p. 93). A note gives its budget as fifty-two thousand yen, nearly half of it shouldered by the Korean government (Henry 2014p. 241). A colonial report described the site as central between the Japanese and Korean neighbourhoods, but Henry argues that the Meiji-machi ground lay in the heart of the settler community (Henry 2014p. 94). The display halls favoured goods imported by settlers. Twenty-three indigenous vendors exhibited at ten displays, while Japanese merchants put on over 160 exhibits of metropolitan products (Henry 2014p. 94).

Exhibits and attractions

Henry writes that "the event in 1907 resembled a market where basic" goods were exhibited for inspection and for purchase (Henry 2014p. 94). Amusements were prominent. The main exhibition hall had a rooftop beer garden and teahouse, a music room with phonographs, and a stage where geisha and kisaeng performed three times a week, while a military band played on Sundays (Henry 2014p. 94). Electrified arches stood at the Meiji-machi intersection and along Chongno (Henry 2014p. 94). Prizes went to winners from a systematically categorised list of products (Henry 2014p. 95), and a lottery offered giveaways on 12 and 13 November (Henry 2014p. 95).

Visitors and reception

Park gives about 200,000 visitors (Parkp. 3). Henry gives the same number of participants, nearly 75 percent of them Korean, and says organisers urged them into a spirit of competition (Henry 2014p. 95). The crown prince Sunjong attended the awards ceremony on 9 November (Henry 2014p. 95). Henry describes a following day, designated as Ladies' Day, when five thousand women won lottery prizes, including Korean women who had broken the taboo on going out in daytime (Henry 2014p. 95). He argues that the carnival atmosphere diluted the message of industry. On the lottery days many visitors never entered the display halls and went straight to the door-prize booths (Henry 2014p. 96). The texts say little about how people responded. Henry notes that extant sources give little idea of how Koreans or Japanese responded to the ideology of progress (Henry 2014p. 96).

The colonial setting

Henry links the fair to the protectorate's coercion. The second protectorate treaty was signed months before the opening, and Kojong's protests failed before his dethronement in late 1907 (Henry 2014p. 93). The decision to disband the Korean army in 1907 caused armed uprisings and prevented officials from collecting local products that would attract Korean visitors (Henry 2014p. 96). Park refers only to the social and political turmoil of 1907 as the background to the Kyŏngsŏng event (Parkp. 6).

Its place in the series

Henry calls the 1907 fair a small-scale precursor of the expositions of 1915 and 1929 (Henry 2014p. 93). Park places it after the Pusan fair and before the local industrial exhibitions, or kongjinhoe, which Park calls precursors of the 1915 exhibition (Parkp. 6). Park also says the 1907 fair set features that later colonial expositions used, and that the contrast between Western-style pavilions and Korean folk arts reappeared in 1915 in a more complicated form (Parkp. 6). Henry notes that the 1915 palace site moved the city's centre of gravity from the Japanese settlement, the location of the 1907 fair, to the Korean northern village (Henry 2014p. 98). In 1908 officials allocated five hundred thousand yen to a Korean Exhibition for Industrial Promotion, nearly ten times the budget of 1907. The government failed to raise the funds, and the event was postponed indefinitely (Henry 2014p. 96). The Tokyo Industrial Exposition of 1907, whose Crystal Pavilion displayed Koreans, is a different event and should not be confused with this one.

How historians have interpreted it

Two texts interpret the fair. Todd A. Henry reads it as an episode of material assimilation (Henry 2014; Henry 2014p. 92). In his account, the colonial government sought to produce a public milieu in which Koreans could identify with the progress of an empire that relegated most of them to the lower classes (Henry 2014p. 92). Henry concludes that the 1907 fair's assimilatory effects were limited, because Japan's imperialist tactics and Koreans' unpredictable engagements undercut its message, and visitors pursued their own pleasures (Henry 2014p. 95; Henry 2014p. 96). Young-sin Park treats the 1907 fair mainly as the origin of a display form, the staging of a contrast between modern and premodern space (Parkp. 6). For the expositions after annexation, Park concludes that they reflected Korea's failed struggle against Japanese domination and Japan's intention to justify colonial annexation (Parkp. 7).

Japanese participation

The fair was a Japanese enterprise in its exhibitors and its audience. Japanese merchants put on over 160 exhibits of metropolitan products, while twenty-three indigenous vendors exhibited at ten displays (Henry 2014p. 94). According to a note, the Japanese crown prince Yoshihito visited on opening day (Henry 2014p. 242). Henry and Park say nothing about Chinese or Southeast Asian participation or visitors.

Themes in the scholarship

  • Settler dominance — Japanese merchants and the Residency-General shaped the site, the halls and the prizes, while Korean exhibitors were few (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 94)
  • Market and festival — Henry describes the fair as a market and festival with amusements, a lottery and a Ladies' Day, and argues that many visitors came for pleasure (Henry 2014p. 94; Henry 2014p. 96)
  • Material assimilation — Henry reads the fair as an attempt to make Koreans identify with colonial progress, which the crowds' behaviour partly subverted (Henry 2014p. 92; Henry 2014p. 96)
  • Modern and premodern space — Park sees the contrast between Western-style pavilions and Korean folk arts as a model that later colonial expositions used (Parkp. 6)
  • Protectorate politics — the second protectorate treaty, Kojong's dethronement and the disbanding of the army form the setting for the fair (Henry 2014p. 93; Henry 2014p. 96)

Questions for study

  1. Henry calls the fair small-scale, and Park calls it the first major exposition in the capital. What makes an exhibition major, and what does each label leave out? (Henry 2014p. 93; Parkp. 6)
  2. The Korean government paid nearly half of a 52,000-yen budget, yet Japanese interests dominated the fair. What did collaboration mean in practice? (Henry 2014p. 241; Henry 2014p. 93)
  3. If the lottery drew visitors away from the displays, how far can the fair be judged by its aim of teaching industry? (Henry 2014p. 96)
  4. Henry says the sources cannot show how people responded. What kinds of evidence would answer that question? (Henry 2014p. 96)
  5. How did moving the exhibition from the Japanese settlement to the palace change its message? (Henry 2014p. 98)

Chapters that treat it at length 2 in 2 works

Henry, Todd A. Assimilating Seoul: Japanese Rule and the Politics of Public Space in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2014.
3Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Groundsp. 92Asia

Henry argues that the expositions staged on the grounds of Kyŏngbok Palace were a central means of 'material assimilation', by which the colonial government sought to make Koreans identify with Japanese rule as a route to progress. The chapter follows three events. The small 1907 Keijo exhibition, dominated by Japanese merchants and overshadowed by the protectorate's forced treaty, was mostly a market and a festival. The 1915 Industrial Exhibition cleared many palace buildings and used Western-style halls, museum-style classification and a contrast between a backward Korean past and a modern Japanese present to teach visitors to see development. The 1929 Korea Exposition, financed by coerced donations and rural tour groups, drew nationalist critics who condemned its 'pure Korean style' halls. Drawing on newspapers, police records and visitor accounts, Henry shows that crowds came mainly for amusements and giveaways, that tour guides struggled to make the displays meaningful, and that some visitors turned the event to their own ends. The chapter concludes that the expositions exposed the unevenly shared rewards of colonial modernity.

Park, Young-Sin. “Making ‘Modern’ Korean Subjects: The Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition of 1915.” Journal article.
The Introduction of an International Exposition into Korea as a Symbol of Modernityp. 3Asia

Park traces how the idea of the international exposition, pangnamhoe, entered Korean public life between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The main evidence is the Pobingsa mission to the United States in 1883, Pak Chŏng-yang's report on the Tokyo national exhibition of 1881, Yu Kil-chun's book Sŏyu kyŏnmun of 1895, and newspaper articles of 1884 and 1900. Park argues that Korean understanding came mainly through Japan but also through direct contact with the West. Expositions became a symbol of civilisation, self-strengthening and national prosperity. Korea's participation at Chicago in 1893 and Paris in 1900, the Japanese-Korean Merchandise Exposition at Pusan in 1906 and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907 are presented as steps towards the kongjinhoe, or industrial exhibition, that prepared the 1915 event. The chapter ends by reading these early exhibitions as expressions both of Korean reform and of Japanese justification for annexation.

Kwon, Hyeokhui. “An Analysis of Korean Intellectual Responses to the Exhibition of Koreans at Japanese Expositions: Nationalism and the Discourse on Northeast Asian Solidarity at the Turn of the Century.” Journal article.
Introductionp. 19Asia

Kwon introduces the human displays at Japanese expositions, which combined social Darwinist evolutionary schemes with freak-show entertainment and colonial ideology. Kwon argues that earlier work on the 1903 Anthropological Pavilion in Osaka has focused on the Japanese organisers and overlooked Korean responses, as well as the 1907 Crystal Pavilion in Tokyo. The article traces how Korean intellectuals moved from hope in Northeast Asian solidarity with Japan to nationalist resistance, using newspapers and magazines of the period.

In the contents of this work

The 1907 Crystal Pavilion and Its Representation of Koreansp. 24Asia

Kwon turns to the 1907 Crystal Pavilion at the Tokyo Industrial Exhibition, which Japanese merchants built beside a small Korea hall to draw visitors. It was a fantasy and terror attraction with a dark cave, mirror illusions and a final room where a Korean man and woman sat in traditional dress, and no anthropologists took part. Kwon reconstructs the pair's identities from competing Japanese and Korean newspapers, which disagree over names, origins and whether the woman had come willingly. A table sets out how the display was described, and Korean newspapers likened it to the exhibition of animals. Kwon concludes that the Crystal Pavilion was both educational and commercial, like the 1903 display, and that both were withdrawn after Korean protests stirred ethnic feeling against Japan.

In the contents of this work

The Anthropological Pavilion and Korean Responsesp. 27Asia

Kwon's longest section follows the protests that brought about the withdrawal of the 1903 Korean display. Japanese officials, worried about relations with Korea and China, ordered the display removed after a protest by three Koreans living in Japan, who appealed to shared race and culture. Kwon notes that these protesters were pro-Japanese bureaucrats, and sets their stance beside the Chinese students' fierce objections to a Chinese display cancelled before it opened. Turning to 1907, Kwon argues that Korean newspapers and the T'aegŭk hakpo responded with far more emotion, condemning the display as racist and as national shame. The Japanese Yomiuri, by contrast, framed the affair as women's liberation and downplayed discrimination. The section ends by linking these reactions to the nationalism of the Patriotic Enlightenment movement after the 1905 Protectorate Treaty.

In the contents of this work

The Transformation in Korean People’s Perceptions of the Display of Koreansp. 34Asia

Kwon's penultimate section traces how Korean intellectuals' views of the displays changed between 1903 and 1907. Kwon places both reactions within social Darwinism and the idea of a racial struggle that Japanese politicians such as Itō Hirobumi promoted, and argues that elites at first hoped Japan would lead a yellow-race solidarity against the West. The 1903 protest grew from that hope, Kwon suggests, but the Russo-Japanese War and the 1905 Protectorate Treaty weakened it. Quoting the diaries of Yun Ch'i-ho and the editorials of Chang Chi-yŏn, Kwon shows that the 1907 reaction was angrier and more nationalist as Japan took control of the Korean state. The section notes that the display coincided with the emperor's envoys to The Hague and the later dethronement of the emperor. It concludes that Korean intellectuals turned from solidarity to nationalism.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Assimilating Seoul by Henry studies how the Japanese colonial government in Keijo, the capital of colonial Korea, used public spaces between 1910 and 1945 to draw Koreans into imperial rule. Henry's argument is that colonial governance reached the main arteries and monuments of the city but faltered in everyday life, and that settlers, Korean elites and poor residents each shaped those spaces to their own ends. The book has an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue, each concerned with one domain of public life: urban planning, Shintō shrines and festivals on Namsan, the expositions held on the Kyŏngbok Palace grounds, sanitation and hygiene, and wartime imperial subjectification. The argument rests on colonial and Korean newspapers, official plans, police records and visitor accounts, and the epilogue follows the postcolonial remaking of the same sites.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject: the book studies one city in colonial Korea, so attention to the region is continuous and central. Japan is present throughout as metropole and colonizer, through the Government-General, the settler community and imperial shrine cults. Chapters 2 and 5 treat Japanese religious and state displays in Seoul, including the 1940 Great Keijo Exposition. Exhibitions with East Asian participation are concentrated in chapter 3, Material Assimilation: Colonial Expositions on the Kyŏngbok Palace Grounds, where a Korean journalist's critique of the 1929 Korea Exposition notes Taiwanese bananas, Mongolian sheep and Manchurian soybean products in its halls. Chapter 4, Civic Assimilation: Sanitary Life in Neighborhood Keijo, covers the 1933 Disease Prevention Exhibition. Taiwan, Manchuria and Mongolia appear mainly as sources of goods or as comparisons: a Taiwanese amulet registration rate is cited in chapter 5, and a planner's trip to Manchuria appears in chapter 1. Chinese people are not studied as visitors, exhibitors or residents, and Chinese cities figure only through comparison, such as the Tianjin treaty port in chapter 4. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is discussed, and the only related item is a Philippines title in the bibliography.

About the work as a whole Asia: central

Park's article examines the Chosŏn Industrial Exhibition held in Seoul from September to October 1915, five years after Japan's annexation of Korea. Park argues against reading the event mainly as colonial propaganda and treats it as a cultural machinery that addressed Korean visitors as modern subjects, drawing on Foucault's panopticon and apparatus. It traces how the exposition idea entered Korea from the 1880s, analyses the Western-style pavilions and the 'old' palace space at Kyŏngbok Palace, and examines visitors' responses to discipline, spectacle and commodities. A brief conclusion places the exhibition at the start of colonial exposition culture in Korea.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia is the whole subject, since the exhibition took place in Korea under Japanese rule, and Korea is discussed in every chapter. The attention is central, concentrated in the chapters on the introduction of the exposition idea and on 'Modern Korea', with the visitor chapter on Koreans' experience. Japan is the second presence: as colonial government it designed the Western-style pavilions in the palace grounds on the model of the Tokyo Taisho Exhibition of 1914, and it sold Japanese goods through merchants such as Mitsukoshi. China appears only as the former power over Korea, whose troops suppressed the 1884 coup. Korean participation abroad is traced in the introduction: the Chicago exposition of 1893 and the Paris exposition of 1900, where Korea showed itself as an independent empire but was read as a 'Hermit Kingdom', and the Kyŏngsŏng Exposition of 1907. Koreans are otherwise the audience: over a million visitors came in 1915, and folk arts and customs were shown in the 'old' palace space to set off Japanese progress. Chinese visitors and exhibitors do not appear in the text as read. Southeast Asia is absent: none of its countries, colonies or peoples is named.
