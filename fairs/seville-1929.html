Ibero-American Exposition

Exposición Ibero-Americana

The Exposicion Ibero-Americana was a world's fair held in Seville in 1929 and 1930, in the Parque Maria Luisa (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). The two encyclopedic reference works date it 1929-1930, while Allwood, Allan and Reid date it 1930 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255; Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). It was staged alongside the Barcelona exposition as the General Exposition of Spain. Historians use it to study how Spain sought prestige through its ties with the Americas, and how a late-1920s fair used modern design (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256; Harvey 1996p. 62; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

In one reference work the idea of a Spanish-American fair in Seville dates from 1905, when Spain still felt the Spanish-American War and the loss of its last American colonies. The fair was meant to win the colonies back culturally, if not politically (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). A national fair, Spain in Seville, was held in 1908 for the centenary of Spain's liberation from Napoleon, and an international exposition planned for 1914 stalled when war broke out (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255).

In 1924 Spain's new military dictatorship saw a fair as a way to advance hispano-americanismo, a strategy of restoring Spanish influence through a Spanish-American bloc (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). King Alfonso XIII preferred Seville to Madrid, which had been proposed, because of the city's role in Spanish exploration (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). Harvey instead dates the conception to 1889, when Spain was losing its last colonies, and describes the fair as a bid for new trade and diplomatic links (Harvey 1996p. 62).

Allwood, Allan and Reid place the change of plan a year earlier, in 1923. The Barcelona organisers first proposed an International Exhibition of Furniture to open on 13 September 1923 (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). That was the date when the monarchy began to fall and a military dictatorship followed (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). The new rulers widened the Barcelona show to cover industry, sport and Spanish art, and tied it to a Seville exhibition the following year, to form a General Spanish Exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). The dictionary dates the military government's interest in a fair to 1924 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255), so the two accounts differ by a year.

Organisation and site

The encyclopedia names José Cruz Condé as Royal Commissioner, Nicolás Díaz Molero as honorary president and Eduardo Aunos as minister of work (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 420). Cruz Condé became director in 1926 and completed planning and construction within three years (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255).

The reference works disagree on size. The encyclopedia says the engineer J.C.N. Forester laid out some 170.5 acres in 1914 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). Allwood, Allan and Reid give 120 acres (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208). The texts say little about money. Allwood, Allan and Reid record a loss without a sum, 4,400,400 attendance and five months open (Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Architecture and exhibits

The main entrance, the Plaza de Espana, faced the tobacco factory depicted in Bizet's opera Carmen (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). Its chief architect, Vicente Traver, blended Renaissance and Mudejar styles, and the plaza took fourteen years to build (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). The encyclopedia describes a 200-metre hemicycle with an 80-metre Giralda at each end, a fountain honouring four provinces, and tiled alcoves with historical scenes for Spain's 49 provinces (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

The encyclopedia credits the architect Gonzalez with laying out the main plazas and designing most of the 74 buildings (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). Three buildings on the Plaza de America, in neo-Mudejar, neo-Spanish Renaissance and neo-Spanish Gothic styles, are now museums (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

The programme told Spain's history. The Exposicion del Libro examined printing as a means of exporting Spanish culture, and a Historia de Sevilla exhibit showed artefacts from the Roman, Visigoth, Moorish, Christian and romantic eras (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). A Historia de America display used maps and documents from the Archivo de los Indios (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). Seville shared the Exposicion Nacional de Bellas Artes with Barcelona (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256).

Commerce was left out by design. The historical dictionary says that "The Ibero-American focus limited the exposition’s commercial potential for Spain" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256). Most participating countries were agrarian and needed US and European technology more than Spanish farm produce (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256).

Colour lighting at the Barcelona and Seville expositions of 1929 was used effectively on buildings and in fountains (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Rydell, Findling and Pelle say the Century of Progress lighting designers went further, surpassing the Spanish fairs by using gaseous tube lighting for the first time at a world's fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). The Plaza de Espana later became a film set, appearing in David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia (1962) and George Lucas's Star Wars Episode Two (2002) (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). A garden city-style workers' quarter was built at the same time just below the park (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254).

Nations and peoples on show

At least 13 foreign nations took part (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). Spanish architects designed the pavilions of Colombia, Venezuela and Guatemala, and native architects designed those of Portugal, Brazil, Peru, Uruguay, Argentina, Mexico, Chile, Santo Domingo, Cuba and the United States (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

Mexico's pavilion receives the fullest description. It was acclaimed as the best national pavilion, and Manuel Amabilis's Art Deco-Maya design was pre-Hispanic in inspiration, reflecting post-Revolutionary politics (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). Victor Reyes's frescos dealt with the Spanish and Indian wars, and Reyes saw Mexico's glory as rooted in its Hispanic and Indian past (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). The United States spent $700,000 on one permanent and two temporary buildings by San Diego architect William Templeton Johnson, and some twenty government agencies contributed films (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). The texts say nothing about living people put on display at Seville. Their attention falls on pavilions, art and historical exhibits.

The United States pavilion, by William Templeton Johnson, evoked Hollywood more than Spain (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). Its domed cinema auditorium was not a venue for Hollywood films. It was an early attempt by the US government to use informational films as a cost-efficient means of communication and propaganda at world's fairs (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). The films covered child welfare reform, women in industry, scenic grandeur and road building in the United States (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

Visitors and reception

Allwood, Allan and Reid describe Seville as a relatively modest affair beside Barcelona's far more spectacular show (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105; Allwood et al. 2001p. 105). Chicago's organisers visited Seville and other European fairs, but apart from Antwerp's colonial fair none captured their interest like Paris in 1931 (Rydell 1993p. 82). Harvey says Spain was unable to capitalise on the fair, whose timing fell on the eve of the European depression (Harvey 1996p. 62). The encyclopedia says the Plaza de Espana remains an important landmark today (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 255).

Politics and controversy

Planners widened the scope to include Portugal and Brazil. US participation provoked protest against alleged US imperialism in Central and South America, until the government invited the North Americans to take part (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). Harvey notes that Franco came to power soon after the fair, which in many ways marked the start of Spain's long isolation from Europe (Harvey 1996p. 62).

Aftermath and legacy

The encyclopedia calls the fair unusual because its grounds and many buildings remain intact (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254). The reference works record its effects on the city: new hotels and hostels, expanded parkland, public works and skilled artisans (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). A canal from the river to the Atlantic was dredged, and many hostelries were converted into public housing after the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256). The Teatro Lope de Vega, built for the 1929 fair, hosted a classical theatre festival during the 1992 exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 384). The US pavilion became the US Consulate-General (Allwood et al. 2001p. 174).

Chicago's designers also drew on the Spanish fairs. Rydell, Findling and Pelle say that modernism at Barcelona and Seville in 1929 significantly influenced the architects of the Century of Progress, and that coloured lighting there was the more immediate inspiration for Chicago's lighting (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).

Air travel and the airship

At the opening of the exposition, Argentina began an airmail service between Seville and Buenos Aires by launching a dirigible (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). While planning the Graf Zeppelin's 1933 visit to Chicago, the Zeppelin Company negotiated for a zeppelin facility in Seville (Ganz 2008; Ganz 2008). The Graf Zeppelin's final 1933 crossing returned to Friedrichshafen by way of Seville (Ganz 2008). The passages read here do not connect either arrangement with the 1929-1930 fair.

Press, records and printed guides

The archives of the Ibero-American Exposition are held in the Hemeroteca Municipal in Seville (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256). The official guide was planned in two volumes. The Libro de Oro Iberoamericano (1929) is the first, and volume 2 was never published (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). Two Spanish studies follow the fair through the press. Eduardo Rodriguez Bernal's study of the local press (1981) covers 1905 to 1914 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256), the year range in which the dictionary places the idea of the fair (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). Encarnacion Lemus Lopez's study (1987) treats 1923 to 1929 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256). The local paper ABC de Sevilla published a 32-part series on the exposition between 30 August and 12 October 1961 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 257). Frances Parkinson Keyes wrote about the American pavilions in Good Housekeeping in September 1929 (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 257).

Europe's run of fairs

Rydell says the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley stimulated a rapid succession of fairs across Europe (Rydell 1993p. 3), and lists Seville among them (Rydell 1993p. 3). By 1928 the number of fairs had led to the creation of the Bureau of International Expositions, charged with reducing the quantity and raising the quality of world's fairs (Maloney 2012p. 156; Maloney 2012p. 156). Maloney counts the Barcelona and Seville fairs of 1929 among the expositions of those years (Maloney 2012p. 156).

How historians have interpreted it

The encyclopedia and the historical dictionary read the fair as a cultural event. The dictionary's entry, by Palmer, says that "Fair officials decided to emphasize the fine arts and Spain’s cultural ties to its former colonies, leaving commerce and industry to the Barcelona exposition" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256). The encyclopedia makes the same contrast and adds that "Much like the later 1992 exposition in Seville, the Ibero-American Exposition spurred the modernization of Seville’s infrastructure" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

Harvey reads the fair differently, as a bid for trade and diplomatic links that was ill-timed, and as the start of Spain's long isolation under Franco (Harvey 1996p. 62; Harvey 1996p. 62). Harvey dates the conception to 1889, whereas the dictionary's entry dates the idea to 1905 (Harvey 1996p. 62; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255). Harvey uses the later Seville fair to argue that "The parallels with the 1929 exhibition are perhaps a little closer than the organizers would admit, and definitely closer than anticipated" (Harvey 1996p. 63).

Muñoz Torreblanca groups Barcelona and Seville together as a single Spain International Exhibition of 1929 (Filipová 2015p. 47). Rydell, Findling and Pelle use the Spanish fairs as precedents for modern design (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000). Allwood, Allan and Reid present Seville as modest beside Barcelona (Allwood et al. 2001p. 105).

The main disagreement concerns purpose. The reference works present a fair whose cultural aims left commerce to Barcelona (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254), while Harvey treats trade and diplomatic links as central (Harvey 1996p. 62). Historians also differ on whether Barcelona and Seville were one event or two concurrent ones (Filipová 2015p. 47; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254).

Harvey dates the fair's conception to 1889 and adds that Spain was beginning to look like a colonised state itself as it lost its last colonies (Harvey 1996p. 62; Harvey 1996p. 62). Harvey argues that the European crisis made the fair possible, because Latin American economies were buoyant as a result, yet Spain could not profit from it (Harvey 1996p. 62; Harvey 1996p. 62). The encyclopedia names Mauricio Tenorio-Trillo's Mexico at the World's Fairs (1996) as one of the few English-language texts on the fair, with a chapter on the 1929 exposition (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256). Sylvie Assassin's study of 1992 focuses on the fair's architecture (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256), and Rodriguez Bernal's history of 1994 is among the Spanish works listed (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).

Themes in the scholarship

  • Cultural against commercial aims — The reference works say Seville stressed cultural ties with former colonies while commerce and industry went to Barcelona (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254).
  • Ibero-American and Hispanic-American ties — The fair was meant to restore Spain's prestige through links with the Americas; Harvey reads it as a bid for trade and diplomatic links (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255; Harvey 1996p. 62).
  • Plaza de Espana and its tiles — The plaza's Giralda towers, fountain and provincial tile scenes are the best-described feature of the site (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 254; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 255).
  • Mexico and the Hispanic-Indian past — Mexico's pavilion and Victor Reyes's frescos are the one national display the texts describe at length (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).
  • Seville's modernisation — The fair brought hotels, a canal, public works and skilled artisans to the city (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 256; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 256).
  • Chicago's debt to the Spanish fairs — Modern design and coloured lighting at Barcelona and Seville are named as precedents for Chicago's fair (Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000; Rydell, Findling & Pelle 2000).
  • Economic limits — Harvey stresses the timing as the Depression began, and the statistics table records a loss (Harvey 1996p. 62; Allwood et al. 2001p. 208).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the organisers present a fair about the Americas as a cultural event rather than a commercial one, and what does that choice reveal about Spain's position in the 1920s?
  2. Should the fair's origins be dated from 1905, as the historical dictionary has it, or from 1889, as Harvey argues, and what does each date imply about its purpose?
  3. Should Barcelona and Seville be studied as one event, as one chapter does, or as two concurrent fairs with different remits?
  4. How far can a fair's architecture outlast the event that built it, and what did the Seville site leave to the city?
  5. What did Chicago's designers take from the Spanish fairs, and why did the Paris fair of 1931 matter more to them?
  6. How does the later 1992 Seville exposition change the way historians read the 1929 fair?
  7. Did the airmail flight and the Zeppelin plans make air travel part of Spain's case for the fair, or were they unrelated to it, and what do the sources allow us to say?
  8. Why does one dating put Spain's military government's interest in a fair in 1923 and another in 1924, and how far does the choice of starting year change what the fair is taken to have been for?
  9. Why did the press studies of Rodriguez Bernal and Lemus divide the fair's history at 1914 and 1923, and what do the gaps between them leave out?

Entry 'Seville 1929-1930 (Exposicion Ibero-Americana)', printed pp. 254-256; index p. 254; fair officials in Appendix C

This encyclopedia entry reads the Seville fair as the cultural counterpart to the concurrent Barcelona exposition. Barcelona concentrated on world-wide commerce, while Seville stressed Spain's cultural ties with its former colonies through exhibits on printing, Seville's history and the Americas. The entry presents the fair's aim as restoring Spain's standing through Ibero-American links, and its lasting significance as a catalyst for Seville's modernisation and as a set of architectural monuments that survive. Its political history is brief (a 1914 plan stalled by war, the 1926 appointment of Jose Cruz Conde), so its main argument is the contrast between the cultural purpose of Seville and the commercial purpose of Barcelona.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. Southeast Asian people appear mainly as displayed people: native villages from Dutch colonies at Amsterdam 1883, the Philippine reservation at St. Louis 1904, an Igorot village at Seattle 1909, and the 55 Filipinos at Ghent 1913, who went unpaid after their agency went bankrupt and nine of whom died of exposure. Indochina appears as colonial display at Paris 1931, with an Angkor Wat replica. South Asia (Calcutta 1883-1884, Ceylon at Wembley) is treated alongside, as contrast.

Harvey, Penelope. Hybrids of Modernity: Anthropology, the Nation State and the Universal Exhibition. London: Routledge, 1996.
Chapter 'The nation state', pp. 61-63 (index: Ibero-American Fair (1929), pp. 61-62, 63)

Harvey's chapter on the nation state uses Expo'92 in Seville as its main case and reads the 1929 Ibero-American Fair as an earlier attempt at the same task. She argues that the 1929 fair aimed to build new trade and diplomatic links with Spain's former colonies, but was ill-timed because of the coming 1930s depression, and that it marked the start of Spain's long isolation under Franco. Her closing point is that the aftermath of Expo'92 (price collapse, inflation and debt) resembled the 1929 fair more closely than its organisers admitted.

About the work as a whole Asia: occasional

Harvey's Hybrids of Modernity is a reflexive study of Expo'92, the Seville universal exhibition, written by an anthropologist whose access to the fair was as a journalist with press credentials. Its main argument is that the nation state, the universal exhibition and social anthropology are linked modernist institutions, and that the hybrid forms of late twentieth-century culture do not dissolve them but carry effects their founders did not expect. The scope is one fair in one year, with comparison to earlier exhibitions such as London in 1851 and Osaka in 1970. The chapters take up anthropology's handling of culture and context, the nation state in the national pavilions, technology among multinational exhibitors, and visitors as citizens and consumers, and a conclusion sets representational knowledge against embodied practice.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia receive occasional attention, and nearly all of it concerns one case: Japan's pavilion at Expo'92. Chapter 2 (Anthropology) describes the Japanese pavilion, a wooden building by Tadao Ando sited opposite Fujitsu's spherical cinema, and says Japan concentrated on tradition rather than technology. Chapter 3 (The nation state) quotes the official guide on Japan's emergence as a technological power at Osaka in 1970, and Chapter 4 (The universal exhibition) notes that Fujitsu's staff denied any special link between Japan and the company. Chapter 5 (Hybrid subjects) reports visitors' disappointment that Japan showed tradition instead of the latest technology. Southeast Asia appears once, in Chapter 2, where an Indonesian procession passes through the site and the author, as a tourist, cannot interpret it. Chapter 3 cites the Expo press dossier's eighteen Asian participating countries, and the conclusion quotes a Swatch executive on Asian production. The book does not discuss Chinese or Korean pavilions, colonial display of Asian peoples, or Asian visitors.

Allwood, John, Ted Allan and Patrick Reid. The Great Exhibitions: 150 Years. London: Exhibition Consultants, 2001.
Barcelona and 'General Spanish Exhibition' passage, p. 105; 'The European Scene' chapter (PDF p. 224; printed page not visible); fair statistics table (PDF p. 257)

This general history of world's fairs gives the Seville fair only a few sentences, in its account of the Spanish exhibitions of the 1920s and in its table of fair statistics. It presents Seville as a modest companion to Barcelona: the two were to form a 'General Spanish Exhibition', but Seville held only the exhibits of the foreign countries named in its title and was a relatively modest affair beside Barcelona's far more spectacular show. Its only other comment is that the Seville fair left architectural monuments, including the US pavilion, which became the US Consulate-General, and that the city later used these as links with past exhibitions before Expo '92.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

Allwood, Allan and Reid trace the international exhibition from medieval fairs and national shows to the world expositions of 1851 to 2000, asking why nations keep staging them despite frequent losses. Their argument is that the medium was driven first by trade and then by national prestige, and moved from subject-based halls and national showcases towards thematic display and civic legacy. The book runs chronologically from London in 1851 to Hanover in 2000, through chapters built around a few named fairs such as Chicago 1893, St Louis 1904 and Expo 67. Appendices list the events and the BIE documentation. The final chapter argues that the recognised-event system has allowed more fairs than the medium can sustain, and sets out steps for future hosts.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East Asia gets substantial attention, mostly concerning Japan. Japanese material appears from the early fairs: at London in 1862 and Dublin in 1853 (chapter 3), and through the Japanese delegation to Vienna in 1873 (chapter 4). Chapter 14, 'The Eastern Hemisphere', is mainly about Japan's state-led expositions from Okinawa 1975 to Osaka 1990, with the Aichi 2005 bid, and also covers Korea's Taejon 1993 and China's Kunming 1999. Osaka 1970 is treated as a Japanese event in chapter 12, while Chinese pavilions at Knoxville 1982 and New Orleans 1984 appear in chapter 13. Asian states are shown as exhibitors and hosts, and Asian visitors mostly as Japanese crowds at home. Southeast Asia is slight, appearing mainly as displayed peoples and colonial architecture. Wikipedia

The Ibero-American Exposition of 1929 (Spanish: Exposición iberoamericana de 1929; Portuguese: Exposição Ibero-Americana de 1929) was a world's fair held in Seville, Spain, from 9 May 1929 until 21 June 1930. Countries in attendance of the exposition included: Portugal, the United States, Brazil, Uruguay, Mexico, Peru, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Cuba, Venezuela, the Dominican Republic, Bolivia, Panama, El Salvador, Costa Rica, and Ecuador. Each Spanish region and each of the provinces of Andalusia were also represented. General Miguel Primo de Rivera, then Prime Minister of Spain, gave the opening address. Primo de Rivera allowed the Spanish King Alfonso XIII to give the final words and officially open the exposition.
