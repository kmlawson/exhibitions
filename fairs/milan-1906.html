Milan International Exposition (Sempione)

Esposizione Internazionale del Sempione
OverviewIn depthAsian presenceThemesQuestionsChapter guideAnnotated bibliographyFurther readingPrimary sources onlineOn the web

The Esposizione Internazionale del Sempione was an international exhibition held in Milan from 28 April to 31 October 1906 (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). It was planned to celebrate the opening of the Simplon tunnel, a 12.3-mile railway tunnel through the Alps (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185). Historians have used it to study how one fair could mix engineering, fine art and national display, and how a national section such as Hungary's presented its country as modern (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 147).

In depth: what our sources say

Written from the texts listed in the annotated bibliography below and from nothing else. Each reference in brackets leads to the text the statement rests on.

Origins and aims

The fair grew out of a railway achievement. The encyclopedia and the dictionary both present it as a celebration of the completion of the Simplon tunnel, which they compare in some ways with the Panama Canal (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). The government and King Victor Emmanuel III supported the fair, and some 12 million lire was subscribed to finance it (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).

Site and layout

The fair occupied two sites about two miles apart in Milan, linked by a specially built electric railway (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). The Parco Real, an existing municipal park, held the fine arts buildings and cultural events. The Piazza d'Armi, formerly an open space, held a more formal plan of foreign pavilions and industrial and engineering exhibits (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). The planners wanted art and engineering kept apart, but many foreign pavilions were highlighted by fine arts displays, which blurred that division (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).

Critics did not rate the architecture highly. The buildings the texts single out are the Galleria del Sempione, with a tunnel-like entrance and a replica of the actual tunnel; an ornate concert hall in a mix of baroque and rococo styles; and the Marine Transport Building, whose 200-foot lighthouse tower gave the best view of the grounds (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). Two pavilions stood out by subject: one on hygiene, with medical exhibits from Red Cross uniforms to surgical instruments, and the Motor Car building, which the encyclopedia calls probably the first at a world's fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).

The texts disagree on the size of the site. Maloney gives 400 acres and calls the fair the largest of four overseas fairs in the passage (Maloney 2012p. 117). The statistics table in Allwood, Allan and Reid gives 250 for Milan in its acres column (Allwood et al. 2001p. 207).

Exhibits and attractions

King Victor Emmanuel opened the fair on 28 April in the Parco Real, and a second ceremony two days later blessed the exhibits at the Piazza d'Armi (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). An aeronautic section, where tethered balloons made ascents, and displays of railway engineering added to the attractions (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). Houze says the theme was work, which highlighted transport and new technology, alongside manufactured goods and the entertainments that had been standard at fairs for more than half a century (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161).

The fair coincided with the first international automobile races in Italy (Della Coletta 2006p. 110). An automobile parade on 26 May was held in conjunction with an international congress on the automobile (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). Other special attractions included Pope Pius IX's railway car, Buffalo Bill's Wild West Show and a Streets of Cairo village (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 194). Della Coletta uses the 1906 guidebook to show how the African village and the Via del Cairo were presented as authentic: the village as a living inventory of animals and peoples, the street as a true corner of Egypt (Della Coletta 2006p. 292). A large pavilion that the book calls grandiose reproduced the Arctic, with polar rocks and white bears (Della Coletta 2006p. 116). The Simplon Pavilion was hailed in the guidebook as the triumphal display of the latest feat of human genius and labour (Della Coletta 2006p. 287). The fair also held scholarly meetings, and Romano counts 82 of them during the exposition (Della Coletta 2006p. 277).

Art and the Hungarian display

Italian and Hungarian art exhibits drew the most attention after the engineering displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). The fine arts pavilion was given over to Italian national art and to younger artists (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). The dictionary notes that "The Hungarian exhibit in an adjacent building received much praise for its lack of commercialism" (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).

Houze places the Hungarian display at the centre of the fair's decorative arts. Géza Maróti decorated the Hungarian rooms in an Art Nouveau style, which critics read as a modern expression of a Byzantine past (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 147). Maróti and Ödön Faragó worked together on more than thirty rooms, corridors and galleries, where visitors saw traditional crafts beside modern interiors (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). The display occupied a rounded, wedge-shaped corner of the Pavilion of Decorative Art, designed by Sebastiano Giuseppe Locati (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161).

The critic Alfredo Melani, writing in The Studio, found the Hungarian display "a revelation of grace and pride" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162). Melani took the folk-inspired work of Faragó and the Gödöllő artists, a colony of artists, as an expression of modernity (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163). Houze reads this praise as turning rural authenticity into urban fashion (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163). Melani also preferred Maróti's rooms to Italian Renaissance models, and wrote that the Hungarian artists had embroidered the flowers of their art to affirm the character of their race (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163). Allwood, Allan and Reid note that embroidery was most amply represented at Milan, even in section curtains and banners (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87).

Maróti's entrance hall was built from carefully chosen materials. Its semicircular archways carried low-relief wheat sheaves, and the thirteen-foot figure of Genius stood there (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). Piroska Ács writes that Maróti coated the walls with a silver layer over yellow shellac and a grey patina, so that they looked like old churches. The lower third was clad in Zsolnay tiles with a bluish-gold eosin glaze, and a fountain of ceramic wild ducks stood at the centre of the room (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). The central Court of Doves, also by Maróti, held lace, jewellery and silver in glass cases, and its connecting rooms held interiors by Pál Horti, Ede Toroczkai Wigand, Sándor Nagy and Aladár Körösfői Kriesch (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161). Wigand, Nagy and Körösfői Kriesch belonged to the Gödöllő colony (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162). A large far room of embroidered textiles had walls painted by Ödön Faragó (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162).

Visitors to the rooms of commercial furnishers, Gábor Steinbach and Endre Thék, first had to cross a gallery of Zsolnay vases and other decorative objects. Houze reads this route as an echo of Ödön Lechner's belief in the link between traditional crafts (háziipar) and modern applied arts (iparművészet) (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162).

Melani singled out needle lace by Árpád Dékáni, dining-room furniture by József Mócsay to Wigand's design, and carpets by Laura Kriesch-Nagy in three rooms arranged by Sándor Nagy (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163). Tapestries designed by Körösfői Kriesch and woven by Leo Belmonte hung in one of the two rooms for the Hungarian Ministry of Education (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163). Vilmos Veszely's nursery and wooden toys were praised by both Melani and Kálmán Györgyi (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163).

Visitors and reception

The fair closed on 31 October with about 5.5 million visitors (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 194; Allwood et al. 2001p. 207). Contemporary observers judged it a success for showing Italian industrial and engineering progress, and for showing artists and designers beginning to reflect modern art (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). Roche groups Milan with the other international expositions of 1901 to 1915, which the book says each drew from a minimum of 7 million to a maximum of 19 million visits. It gives no separate figure for Milan, so that range sits above the 5.5 million that the reference works give (Roche 2000p. 95). Della Coletta notes that the 1906 Milan fair, like Turin's 1902 architectural exposition, was designated international but did not reach the scope of Turin 1911 (Della Coletta 2006p. 274).

Fire and aftermath

A fire destroyed the joint Italian and Hungarian art building during the run of the exhibition (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). The texts date it differently. The encyclopedia gives 23 August: "Tragically, both the Italian and the Hungarian art exhibits were destroyed in a fire on August 23" (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186). The dictionary gives 2 August (Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). Houze gives 3 August in the text, but a footnote in the same chapter calls it a November fire (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 170).

The Hungarian artists quickly assembled a new installation, less grand than the first, and reused a mural by Aladár Körösfői Kriesch from St Louis, framed by Faragó (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). The Genius sculpture from the entrance hall was the last object to succumb to the flames (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). Maróti recast it in duplicate, and the twin figures were later mounted on the outside of the Royal National Hungarian Academy of Music in Budapest (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 171). Geppert records that the fair's only permanent structure was a huge aquarium, intended as a memorial of the exhibition (Geppert 2010p. 239). Houze argues that the Milan success paved the way for Maróti's later career, including a 1908 commission for a glass window for the Mexican National Opera Theater (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).

Houze describes the burning of the Hungarian section of decorative arts and dates the fire to 3 August, which she places five months after the opening (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). The fair opened on 28 April (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186), so by that reckoning the fire came about three months after the opening. Houze also describes only the Hungarian section burning, against the joint Italian and Hungarian art building described above (Allwood et al. 2001p. 87).

Houze notes that foreign commentators praised the Hungarian rebuilding for its ability to "rise from the ashes" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). She reads this as a metaphor for the Magyar spirit that had fought Ottoman invaders and resisted Habsburg rule (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). Later, when the Music Academy in Budapest moved into its new building in 1907, its interior carried painted murals and frescoes by Körösfői Kriesch and mosaics and stained glass by Miksa Róth. Maróti's Genius figures were placed on its façade (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 171).

Who organised the Hungarian section

Kálmán Györgyi, Secretary-General of the Hungarian Applied Arts Association, described and illustrated the Hungarian exhibit in a report in Magyar Iparművészet in 1906 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 161; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 170). Elemér Czakó published a book on the Hungarian applied arts display at Milan in 1907, which Houze cites (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 170). Houze argues that the Ministry of Education, with the Hungarian Society for Applied Art, controlled how Hungarian culture was presented abroad by designing its difference. She suggests that this strategy may have pushed aside designers whose work had received mixed reviews in Paris (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 166).

Maróti after Milan

In 1927 he went to the United States at the invitation of Eliel Saarinen to help decorate the Cranbrook Academy of Art. He stayed until 1932 and worked with Albert Kahn on Detroit buildings, including mosaics for the Fisher Building (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 164). Houze ties the friendship with Saarinen to the Finnish pavilion at the Paris exposition of 1900, designed by Gesellius, Lindgren and Saarinen (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 164), and to a Budapest exhibition of modern Finnish art in 1907 (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). His 1911 Turin pavilion had a pitched-roof tower meant to evoke "Attila’s tent" (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165). Houze says his last work, still unpublished, was a study of Atlantis (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).

How historians have interpreted it

The main readings differ with the question each scholar asks. Houze, in the Raizman and Robey volume on nationalisms, takes Milan as the last case in a study of how Hungarian designers moved between vernacular and international visions of modernity, and of how fairs helped build a national identity that was often fluid (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 147; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 147). Della Coletta, writing on Turin, reads the Milan fair as part of a wider appetite for vicarious travel that exhibitions fed with polar, African and Egyptian displays (Della Coletta 2006p. 116; Della Coletta 2006p. 167). In that book Milan serves as a comparison rather than the subject (Della Coletta 2006p. 274). Geppert, writing on exhibition cities, calls Milan one of the hardly less important European exhibitions left out of the sample of nine fairs (Geppert 2010p. 14), and uses it as an example of an exposition that gave its site a lasting meaning (Geppert 2010p. 239). Roche treats the fair as one of the mass-audience events of the period, but gives no figure for it alone (Roche 2000p. 95).

The texts agree on the broad character of the fair: a railway celebration that mixed engineering with fine art, and a fire that destroyed the national art displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Allwood et al. 2001p. 87). They disagree on the site's size and the date of the fire (Maloney 2012p. 117; Allwood et al. 2001p. 207; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). The reference works point readers to Carden's 1906 review in Architectural Record and to Milani's studies of Italian and Hungarian art at the fair (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 194).

Miklós Székely, in his chapter A Capital in the Margins in Marta Filipová's volume on lesser-known exhibitions, reads the Hungarian pavilions of these years as instruments of the state (Filipová 2015p. 23; Filipová 2015p. 34). He writes that vernacular modernism was an "appropriated representational tool for the state" between 1906 and 1911 (Filipová 2015p. 34). He also gives the economic motive a larger place. Products in the modern national style raised the country's reputation abroad and sold well (Filipová 2015p. 34). Székely calls the pavilions at Turin, St Louis, Milan, Bucharest, Rome and Dresden "true mirrors" of a modernised economy and culture, made for a mostly foreign audience, whose main aim was to make the economy prosper (Filipová 2015p. 36). In a note he counts the Hungarian pavilions in Milan and Bucharest, both of 1906, among those that followed this concept (Filipová 2015p. 41). Houze stresses instead that national identity was an elusive concept, shaped by political and economic aspiration but also by an appreciation for urban style and fashion (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 166).

Asian presence at the fair

East, South and Southeast Asian participation, displays and visitors, as our texts describe them.

The texts place Japan and China among the foreign nations at Milan, but their evidence on the Asian displays is thin. The encyclopedia and the dictionary both list Japan and China among the countries that took part, after most of Western Europe (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193). Neither says what the Japanese or Chinese displays showed. Turkey is also listed, but it falls outside the East, South and Southeast Asian scope of this section.

China

China's presence is the best documented, and it comes from one dissertation, Hur's study of the Qing government and world expositions. Hur says the Qing government continued to take part in international expositions after St Louis in 1904, including with a Chinese pavilion at Milan in 1906 (Hurp. 122). The St Louis experience had led to new regulations in 1905 that put overseas exhibitions under the Department of Commerce (Hurp. 122).

Zhang Jian led the civilian side of the Chinese preparations, centred on businessmen of the coastal areas, and Hur calls Zhang Jian one of the most significant figures in the history of Chinese world exhibitions in the late Qing period (Hurp. 154). Duanfang, whom Hur describes as one of the few progressive Manchu officials, was in Italy in 1905 and 1906 as a member of the Five Constitutional Study Commissioners, and visited the Milan fair in person (Hurp. 126).

A Dongfang Zazhi article judged the Chinese pavilion "quite humiliating" compared with the Liège fair (Hurp. 123). It said the government pursued only diplomatic activities and merchants only profit (Hurp. 123). It credited Chinese students in Belgium with keeping embarrassing incidents to a minimum at Liège, and warned that Milan was unpredictable without such support (Hurp. 123). Hur reads the article as revealing distrust of the Qing government, set against the capability of the students (Hurp. 123).

Japan

Japan appears only in a list. In an appendix to their study of the Japan-British Exhibition of 1910, Hotta-Lister records Japan's participation in international exhibitions and enters an international exhibition at Milan under 1905, with no figure given (Hotta-Lister 1999p. 222). That date is a year before the fair opened, and Hur gives the Milan fair as 1906 (Hurp. 122). The texts do not say what Japan showed, who organised its display, or how it was received.

South and Southeast Asia

The participant lists name no South or Southeast Asian country (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).

Themes in the scholarship

  • The Simplon tunnel — the fair was built to celebrate the tunnel, and the reference works treat engineering as its main subject (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193; Della Coletta 2006p. 287).
  • Art against engineering — the planners separated the two, but foreign pavilions were filled with fine arts displays (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 185; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193).
  • National display — the Hungarian decorative-arts section is the best-documented national display, which Houze reads as a claim to modern nationhood (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 147; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162).
  • Chinese participation — Hur finds the Chinese pavilion poorly regarded and its preparation divided between officials and merchants (Hurp. 123; Hurp. 154).
  • Fire — the destruction of the Italian and Hungarian art exhibits, which the texts date differently (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165).
  • Vicarious travel and spectacle — polar, African and Egyptian displays, automobiles and Buffalo Bill's show (Della Coletta 2006p. 116; Della Coletta 2006p. 292; Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186).
  • Attendance — the 5.5 million figure set against the 7 to 19 million range given for expositions of the period as a group (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Roche 2000p. 95).

Questions for study

  1. Why did the Italian and Hungarian art displays, rather than the engineering exhibits, draw the praise that the reference works record, and what does that say about what the fair was for (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193)?
  2. How far can a national section's reception be read through its critics, such as Melani, and how far through the state's own aims (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162; Hurp. 123)?
  3. Did the poor reputation of the Chinese pavilion reflect Qing government policy, the conditions of the fair, or the views of the reviewer (Hurp. 123)?
  4. Did the Milan fair leave a lasting urban legacy, or was the aquarium an exception among expositions (Geppert 2010p. 239)?
  5. What do the conflicting fire dates show about how reference works and monographs record such events (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Findling & Pelle 1990p. 193; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 170)?
  6. How should the 5.5 million visitors be weighed against the 7 to 19 million range given for the period's expositions as a group (Findling & Pelle 2008p. 186; Roche 2000p. 95)?
  7. Székely places the Hungarian pavilions under the state's economic aims, and Houze places them under fashion and identity. Which account better fits the commercial rooms and the Ministry rooms described above, and what would each leave out (Filipová 2015p. 36; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 166)?
  8. Houze's text gives the Genius as the last object to burn in an August fire (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 165), while her footnote places its destruction in a November fire (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 170). Which record should a student rely on, and what would settle it?
  9. Why did the Hungarian display set rooms for the Ministry of Education beside rooms for commercial furnishers, and what route did that order make visitors take (Raizman & Robey 2018p. 162; Raizman & Robey 2018p. 163)?

Chapters that treat it at length 1 in 1 work

Raizman, David, and Ethan Robey, eds. Expanding Nationalisms at World’s Fairs: Identity, Diversity, and Exchange, 1851–1915. London: Routledge, 2018.
8“A revelation of grace and pride”: cultural memory and international aspiration in early twentieth-century Hungarian designRebecca Houzep. 147

Houze follows Hungarian decorative art through four international exhibitions, Paris 1900, Turin 1902, St. Louis 1904 and Milan 1906, asking how these displays built a national identity that moved between folk and cosmopolitan models. Her starting point is Hungary's position within Austria-Hungary, which pushed designers to find forms distinct from Austrian ones, drawing on folk motifs and a supposed Eastern origin of the Magyars. In Paris a historicist national pavilion stood beside Bálint and Jámbor's ornate installation, and critics divided over its folk emphasis. Pál Horti's Turin entrance, with stylised Art Nouveau arabesques, won praise from an Austrian reviewer. His St. Louis display of wooden towers and a Székely gate turned the village into a market stall and led to commissions from American furniture makers. Géza Maróti's Milan rooms, admired by Alfredo Melani, burned in August 1906 and were rebuilt in less grand form. Houze concludes that the fairs made Hungarian identity an elusive, fashionable and commercial construct.

Chapters that discuss it more briefly 1

Hotta-Lister, Ayako. The Japan-British Exhibition of 1910: Gateway to the Island Empire of the East. Richmond: Japan Library, 1999.
1Background of the Time (1905-10)p. 9Asia

Hotta-Lister sets out the diplomatic and economic setting for the exhibition in the years 1905 to 1910. Japan had become a great power after the Russo-Japanese War, and its legations in London, Washington and other capitals were upgraded to embassies between 1906 and 1908. The chapter then traces strains in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which British newspapers and officials increasingly questioned over Manchuria, the 'open door' pledge and Japanese immigration. It goes on to tariff revision, which Komura pursued against British objections, and to Japan's trade deficit and reliance on foreign loans. Its last sections cover the annexation of Korea in August 1910 and the rivalry between Katsura and Saionji. It also surveys the Japanese and English-language newspapers on which much of the study relies, including The Times, the Japan Weekly Mail and the Chuo Shimbun. The chapter concludes that these pressures explain why the government was so committed to the exhibition.

About the work as a whole Asia: substantial

The Encyclopedia of World's Fairs and Expositions, edited by Findling and Pelle, is a reference work of about a hundred entries, each on one fair, arranged in date order from London in 1851 to Shanghai in 2010. Each entry is signed by a contributor and closes with its own bibliography. The foreword by Vicente Gonzalez Loscertales of the Bureau of International Expositions argues that expos remain valuable and sets out criteria for a theme. The preface, by the editors, traces fairs from medieval trade fairs through the Crystal Palace and argues that American fairs grew out of European experience, with more weight on civic pride and entertainment. Appendixes cover the BIE, fair statistics, officials, excluded fairs and planned fairs that were never held. The back matter holds a general bibliography, internet resources, contributor notes and an index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a real but secondary strand. Full entries on fairs in the region, all within the Entries chapter, are Hanoi 1902-1903 (Rydell: French Indochina, with exhibits from the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea), Nanking 1910 (Pelle: China's first world's fair, with fourteen foreign nations and fifteen provinces), Osaka 1970 (Anderson: the first expo held in Asia, with Hong Kong among its participants), Okinawa 1975-1976 (Manning: Japan's ocean expo on the Ryukyu island), Tsukuba 1985 (Narita: a Japanese science fair with 47 countries), Taejon 1993 (McGregor: Korea's first fair, with 108 international participants), Aichi 2005 (Heller: Japan, with a seventeen-country Asia Common) and Shanghai 2010 (Fernsebner: China's urban-themed fair). Tokyo 1996 and Manila 2002, both planned and never held, have entries in Appendix E. Asian participation is more often treated within entries on other fairs. St. Louis 1904 (Boger) built a Philippine Reservation of over a thousand Filipinos, including Igorots and Filipino scouts, presented as part of American imperial rule. Seattle 1909 had Japanese naval officers, a Japanese Day and an Igorot village on the midway. Okinawa 1975 lists pavilions for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and the Republic of Korea. Apart from Hanoi, fairs planned or held in Southeast Asia are few: Manila 2002 would have been the first sanctioned fair hosted by a developing country, but the Manila effort collapsed in 1999.

Findling and Pelle's Historical Dictionary of World's Fairs and Expositions (1990) is a reference work. Its core is about ninety-five short entries, arranged chronologically, on international fairs from London in 1851 to Glasgow in 1988, with bibliographies for each. The introduction traces the fairs from medieval trade fairs and early national exhibitions to the Crystal Palace of 1851, and argues that they moved from single halls to national pavilions with fine arts and amusements, serving national prestige and, in the American case, civic rivalry. Appendixes cover the Bureau of International Expositions (John M. MacKenzie), fair statistics (Ted Allan), fair officials, fairs not included, fairs that never were, and fairs yet to come, followed by a general bibliography and index.

East and Southeast Asia in this work. East and Southeast Asia are a secondary but steady presence. Five entries concern fairs held in the region: Hanoi 1902-1903, a French colonial exposition in Tonkin with pavilions for the Philippines, Malaya, Siam, Japan, China, Formosa and Korea; Nanking 1910, China's first world's fair; Osaka 1970, described as the first world exposition in Asia; Okinawa 1975-1976, a Japanese ocean exposition; and Tsukuba 1985, a Japanese government fair on technology and the environment. The Osaka entry is the fullest: 2.7 percent of its 64 million visitors were foreign, Asian nations shared an International Place pavilion, and China and North Vietnam were not invited. Japan's planned fairs of 1912, 1917 and 1940 appear in the chapter on fairs that never were. Asian participation in other fairs is handled as national display. Japan and China are the most frequent, at Vienna 1873, Amsterdam 1883, New Orleans 1884-1885, Barcelona 1888, Liege 1905, Portland 1905, Chicago 1933-1934 and New Orleans 1984. The volume, edited by David Raizman and Ethan Robey, collects eleven essays on how nations used the world's fairs between 1851 and 1915 to present national identity through manufactured goods and architecture. Hur's dissertation examines how the late Qing government tried to present China as a modern state at world exhibitions, and how that effort was undermined by the Imperial Maritime Customs, Japan, and Chinese reformers and revolutionaries. On the web

Wikipedia

The Milan International was a world's fair held in Milan in 1906 titled L'Esposizione Internazionale del Sempione, or sometimes The Great Expo of Work. The exhibition took place between April 28 and November 11, 1906, and was held to celebrate the opening of the Simplon Tunnel. Focused on transport, it showcased technological advancements like electric railways, automobiles and airships, attracting 40 countries, over 35,000 exhibitors, and 4,012,776 visitors. The event covered roughly 1 million square meters (about 250 acres). It was the first world's fair held in Italy and solidified Milan as a leading European industrial hub.
